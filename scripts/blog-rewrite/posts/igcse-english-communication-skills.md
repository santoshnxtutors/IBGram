IGCSE English communication skills come down to three habits: reading a text closely enough to say what the writer is doing, writing for a specific reader and purpose instead of for "the examiner", and speaking and listening with enough control that meaning survives under pressure. A student who grows these three habits improves in the English paper and also in History, Geography, Economics and Business, where the marks depend on reading a question properly and answering it in organised prose.

This guide is for parents of Grade 9 and Grade 10 students in the International General Certificate of Secondary Education (IGCSE), and for the students themselves. It explains what the English syllabuses actually assess, how First Language English differs from English as a Second Language (ESL), what each skill looks like on the page, the mistakes that cost marks, and a weekly routine that builds all three without turning English into a memorising exercise. It is written with Cambridge International Education and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in mind, and the facts about syllabus codes come from their published guides. Always confirm the current syllabus with your child's school.

## What Do IGCSE English Communication Skills Actually Include?

IGCSE English communication skills are the abilities to understand written and spoken English accurately, to write clearly for a defined purpose and audience, and to speak and listen effectively. The papers test them as reading comprehension and summary, directed and creative writing, and, depending on the syllabus, listening and speaking. They are skills of control, not of ornament.

That last point shapes everything that follows. Many students think good English means long words, elaborate sentences and a stock of impressive phrases. Examiners tend to reward the opposite: a sentence that says exactly what is meant, in a register that fits the reader, with punctuation that carries the meaning. A student who writes "The protagonist's tribulations exemplify the pervasive duplicity of society" and cannot say what happens in the story has communicated less than a student who writes "He lies to his friend twice in the first chapter, and the narrator makes us dislike him for it."

Communication has a **sender**, a **message** and a **receiver**. Almost every mark lost in IGCSE English is lost because one of the three is forgotten. The student forgets the receiver (writing a letter to a headteacher in the voice of a text message), forgets the message (a lovely paragraph that does not answer the question), or forgets that they are the sender (copying phrases from the passage instead of putting ideas in their own words).

## Which English Syllabus Is Your Child Actually Taking?

"IGCSE English" is not one subject. Families regularly discover this in Grade 10, when the revision plan they were following turns out to be for a different paper. The table below lays out the main options for Indian schools, based on the published Cambridge and Pearson Edexcel syllabuses.

| Syllabus | Board | What it is | Typical student |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Language English 0500 | Cambridge | A reading paper, then directed writing and composition, or a coursework portfolio route | Student whose English is strong and used daily |
| English as a Second Language 0510 | Cambridge | Reading and writing plus listening; speaking is reported separately | Student who learns English as an additional language |
| English as a Second Language 0511 | Cambridge | As 0510, but speaking counts towards the grade | Same, where the school enters for the speaking-inclusive option |
| Literature in English 0475 | Cambridge | Study of set texts and literary response | Student taking a literature subject, often alongside a language one |
| International GCSE English Language A (4EA1) | Pearson Edexcel | A language syllabus with reading and writing assessment | Student following the Edexcel route |
| International GCSE English Language B (4EB1) | Pearson Edexcel | A second Edexcel language option | Student whose school has chosen this route |
| International GCSE English as a Second Language (4ES1) | Pearson Edexcel | ESL for students learning English as an additional language | Edexcel ESL candidate |

Two points before going further. First, the **school decides** which syllabus and which exam series a student sits. A family cannot swap from 0500 to 0510 because it seems easier. Ask the school which code your child is entered for, and read the syllabus document for that code. Second, many students in Indian international schools take English at a level that sits between the typical First Language and ESL candidate, which is why the choice is made by the school after assessing the cohort.

If your child will move on to the IB Diploma Programme (DP) after IGCSE, the language groups there are English A (Language and Literature, or Literature) and English B, with English ab initio for beginners. The IGCSE choice does not decide that outcome by itself, but the skills carry over, which is covered near the end of this guide.

## How Do First Language and ESL Differ in What They Reward?

First Language English rewards control of style: shaping a voice, choosing detail for effect and varying structure deliberately. ESL rewards accuracy and range across all four skills, reading, writing, listening and speaking, in a clearly communicated register. Both reward answering the actual question, but they weigh fluency and correctness differently.

In First Language English, a student is expected to be comfortable with the language and is assessed on how well they use it: the precision of word choice, the shape of an argument, the effect of a descriptive passage. A grammatically correct but flat piece is a mid-band piece. An ambitious piece with repeated slips in punctuation also loses marks. The target is sophistication *and* control.

In ESL, the focus shifts to whether the student can understand varied texts and recordings, and produce clear, accurate writing and speech for everyday and wider purposes. The candidate is not expected to write like a novelist. They are expected to organise ideas, use a range of structures, avoid errors that obscure meaning, and respond to what they hear and read. Listening is part of the assessment, and, in 0511, speaking counts towards the grade, while in 0510 it is reported separately. Check which one your child's school has entered.

| Skill | First Language emphasis | ESL emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Inference, writer's effect, own-words summary | Understanding detail, gist and purpose across texts |
| Writing | Voice, register, structure, effect | Accuracy, range of structures, clear organisation |
| Listening | Not a separate paper in the 0500 structure described here | Directly assessed |
| Speaking | Check the current syllabus for how it is reported | Counted in 0511, reported separately in 0510 |
| Typical risk | Over-writing, losing control of sentences | Errors that obscure meaning, narrow vocabulary |

For a student whose mother tongue is Hindi, Punjabi or another language and who attends an English-medium school, the sensible approach is to ask the school which route fits and to ask an English teacher for an honest read of the student's last two written pieces. A tutor can then work on the specific weaknesses, not the label.

## The Reading Skills Beneath Every Paper

Reading is the quiet foundation. A student who reads a passage carelessly cannot summarise it, cannot answer a question about the writer's attitude and cannot choose evidence for an argument. The skill has four layers, and most students stop at the first.

**Literal understanding.** What does the text say? "The shopkeeper closed the shutters early" is literal.

**Inference.** What does the text suggest without stating? Closing the shutters early might suggest worry, or a rumour of trouble, or simply a festival. The examiner wants the student to name the inference and point to the words that support it.

**Writer's effect.** How do word choices, sentence length and structure shape the reader's response? A short sentence after a long one produces a pause, a jolt or relief. Students who write "the writer uses a metaphor to make it more interesting" have named a technique and said nothing about its effect.

**Purpose and audience.** Why was this written, and for whom? An advertisement, a speech and a diary entry do different jobs, and a reader who spots that can explain choices in all three.

### A practical technique: the three-pass read

Pass one, read for gist and note the purpose and audience in a few words. Pass two, read the question and underline its command words and constraints, such as "in your own words", "from paragraph 3" or "explain how". Pass three, return to the passage with the question in mind and mark the lines you will use. It takes about the time many students spend panicking, and it replaces panic with a plan.

### Own-words summary

Summary tasks, a standard feature of First Language reading, test whether a student can pick out relevant points and restate them concisely without lifting from the passage. The common mistakes are listed below.

- Copying whole phrases from the text, which shows the examiner nothing about understanding.
- Including examples and detail that the question did not ask for.
- Writing in a long, flowing paragraph that buries the points instead of a clear, economical sequence.
- Ignoring any word limit, which is a signal of control as much as a constraint.

The fix is a habit: for each point, close your eyes, say it in your own sentence, and only then write it down. If you cannot say it without looking, you have not understood it.

## Writing for Purpose and Audience

Writing is where most of the visible marks live, and where IGCSE English communication skills show up most plainly: where a student's communication either works or does not. The central idea is simple: **know who you are writing to, why, and in what form, before writing a word.**

The syllabuses ask for different kinds of writing. Directed writing responds to reading material and asks for a text such as a letter, article, report or speech with a defined audience. Composition asks for descriptive or narrative writing, or in some versions argumentative or discursive pieces. Check the current syllabus for exact task types and word limits, because they differ by code and are revised.

A simple planning frame, which a student can write in the margin in thirty seconds, is **PAF**: Purpose, Audience, Form.

| Element | Question to answer | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | What should the reader think, feel or do afterwards? | Persuade the principal to extend library hours |
| Audience | Who are they, and how formal should I be? | A headteacher, adult, busy, sceptical |
| Form | What conventions does this text type use? | A formal letter with a clear opening, reasoned paragraphs and a courteous close |

:::steps A thirty-second planning routine
Purpose | What should the reader do or feel?
Audience | Who, and how formal?
Form | Letter, article, speech, report
Points | Three or four, in order
:::

### Register: the ladder from casual to formal

A large share of the errors students make are errors of register: the tone does not fit the reader. A useful picture is a ladder.

| Level | Features | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Casual | Contractions, slang, exclamations, direct address | A message to a friend, a diary entry, informal blog |
| Neutral | Plain vocabulary, complete sentences, few contractions | A school magazine article, a general report |
| Formal | Precise vocabulary, no slang, measured tone, no chatty asides | A letter to an official, a speech to adults, a report for a committee |

Students who move between levels inside the same piece, starting formal and slipping into "you guys" by the third paragraph, are marked down for control. The remedy is to decide the level in the planning stage and read the finished piece once, only for tone.

### A worked example: the same point, three audiences

Consider a student making one point: the school canteen should offer more vegetarian options.

*To a friend:* "Honestly, the canteen is a nightmare if you don't eat meat. There's like one sad paneer thing and that's it."

*To the principal (formal):* "I am writing to request that the canteen extend its vegetarian menu. At present, students who do not eat meat have only one regular option, which limits their choice and, for many, makes the canteen impractical."

*In a school magazine article (neutral):* "Walk into the canteen at lunch and look at the menu. If you are vegetarian, your choices run out quickly. A wider range would serve a large part of the school."

Same content, three voices, and each is correct for its reader. The examiner is not assessing the content here. They are assessing whether the student can *hear* the difference and *control* it. That is a communication skill, and it can be practised.

### Structure that carries meaning

Paragraphs are the unit of structure. A paragraph makes one move: a point, its support and a link back to the purpose. Students who write one long block, or who start a new paragraph for every sentence, show that they are not controlling the flow of ideas.

**Openings and endings.** For directed writing, open by establishing the purpose and the relationship with the reader, and close by returning to what you want from them. For narrative, avoid over-long scene-setting and start close to a moment of change. For description, choose a viewpoint and keep to it.

**Linking.** Connectives such as "however" and "as a result" help only when the logic really is contrast or consequence. A student who inserts "furthermore" between two unrelated sentences is decorating, not linking.

### Accuracy: the small errors that decide bands

Punctuation and grammar are not a separate topic. They carry meaning. The errors below recur across scripts and are fixable within weeks.

| Error | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Comma splice | "The ground was wet, we stayed inside." | Full stop, semicolon or a linking word |
| Run-on sentences | Five ideas in one breathless sentence | Break at each complete thought |
| Apostrophe errors | "the childrens books", "it's colour" | Check possession versus contraction |
| Tense drift | Past tense that slides into present mid-paragraph | Choose a tense and reread once for it |
| Fragment used by accident | "Because the road was closed." | Complete it, or use deliberately and rarely |
| Agreement | "The list of items are on the table." | Match the verb to the subject, not the nearest noun |
| Confused words | their/there, affect/effect | Keep a short personal list and reread for it |

A student can eliminate most of these with a proofreading pass that is *different* from rereading. Read the piece once backwards, sentence by sentence, looking only at the sentence in front of you. This breaks the habit of reading what you meant to write.

## Descriptive and Narrative Writing: Control Before Colour

Creative tasks tempt students to pile on adjectives and similes. The result reads like a list of techniques.

A better principle is **specific over decorative.** "The old man was very tired and sad" tells. "He lowered himself into the chair as if the last hour had been a staircase" shows, through one precise comparison. One strong image beats five weak ones.

For descriptive pieces, choose a small frame, a single room, a bus stop, a market at closing time, and give the senses one at a time. Vary sentence length. Include one detail that is unexpected, because the unexpected is what makes the reader believe the rest.

For narrative pieces, the common failure is trying to cover too much time. A story that spans a week in six hundred words becomes a summary. A story that stays in one scene, with a clear change at the end, is stronger. The ending does not need to be a twist. It needs to feel earned by what came before.

## Listening and Speaking: The Skills Families Overlook

Because most revision focuses on the written papers, listening and speaking often get the least practice. They matter for two reasons: in ESL they are assessed directly, and in every syllabus they underpin the confidence a student brings to writing.

### Listening

Listening in an exam is a single pass or a very limited number of hearings, so the skill is anticipation. Before the recording starts, read the questions and predict what kind of information is needed: a number, a name, a reason, a feeling. While listening, write short notes, not sentences. Afterwards, answer from the notes. Common errors include writing an answer that sounds right but is not what the speaker said, and missing the correction that follows a first statement ("It starts at six. Actually, no, seven").

A home routine that works: ten minutes a day of a news bulletin, a podcast or a documentary clip at a clear speaking pace, followed by writing three sentences about what was said, without replaying. Then replay and check. This trains attention and recall together.

### Speaking

Speaking is assessed in different ways depending on the syllabus and the school: a conversation, a presentation, a discussion with the examiner. In 0511 it counts towards the grade, and in 0510 it is reported separately, so check which applies. The skills are the same in either case.

- **Answer the question asked**, then extend. "Do you like reading?" is not an invitation to recite a memorised paragraph.
- **Use a range of sentence structures**, not only simple ones. A brief reason, a contrast or a condition adds range.
- **Avoid long pauses filled with sounds.** A short pause to think is natural. A stream of "um" is distracting.
- **Ask for clarification when needed.** "Could you repeat that?" shows control, not weakness.
- **Stay on topic and develop a point.** One idea, a reason and an example is a miniature paragraph, spoken.

For students who are shy, practice with one person at a time builds the habit, then a small group, then a recorded presentation. Recording on a phone and watching the playback is uncomfortable and very effective.

### Group discussion and presentation habits

Even where they are not examined, these are the skills a student will use for the rest of school and university: listening to a point before responding, disagreeing without hostility, and signposting a presentation so the audience knows where it is going. A structure such as "I'll make three points, first..." helps the speaker as well as the listener.

## Vocabulary: Precision Over Ornament

Students are told to "improve vocabulary" and then given lists of rare words to memorise. This produces essays with misused words. A better route is **precision**: knowing the right word for what you mean.

A useful exercise: take a sentence you wrote, find the vaguest word in it (often "good", "bad", "thing", "very", "lot"), and replace it with a word that says exactly what you meant. "The speech was good" becomes "the speech was persuasive", or "moving", or "well-organised", depending on what you meant. Each replacement carries different information. The same exercise works for verbs: "walked" becomes "strolled", "trudged" or "marched", and each tells the reader something different.

Reading widely supplies words in context, which is the only reliable way to learn how a word behaves. Newspapers, long-form articles and fiction at the student's level, with a notebook for words that were genuinely new and worth keeping, will do more than any list. Learn a word with a sentence, and use it in your own writing within a week, or it fades.

For ESL candidates, a related priority is **collocation**, the words that naturally go together: "make a decision" not "do a decision", "heavy rain" not "strong rain". Collocation errors are common in otherwise good writing, and they are learned by reading and listening, not by rule.

## Choosing Exam Technique Over Panic: Time and Planning

A timed English paper is a project in miniature. The common failure is spending forty minutes on the first question and ten on the one with more marks.

:::timeline A planning approach for a timed writing task
First 2-3 min | Decide purpose, audience, form
Next 3-5 min | List three or four points and an ending
Main block | Write, keeping paragraphs to one move each
Last 5 min | Proofread for tense, punctuation and tone
:::

Planning feels like lost time, and it is the opposite. A student with a plan writes faster, because they are not deciding what comes next in every sentence. Even a three-line plan reduces the chance of drifting or ending halfway through an idea.

For reading papers, allocate time in proportion to marks, and answer the highest-mark question with the most care. If a question has a word limit, respect it. If it asks for evidence, quote briefly and comment, rather than copying a sentence and moving on.

## Coursework Versus Exam Route in First Language English

Some Cambridge First Language English entries use a coursework portfolio, at the school's choice, and building IGCSE English communication skills through drafts looks different from exam preparation. If your child's school uses the portfolio route, the skills are the same but the process is different: pieces are drafted and revised over weeks, and the school submits a selection.

The caution for parents is the same as for any assessed coursework. The work must be the student's own. A tutor can teach technique, discuss a draft, and ask questions about the choices the student made, but must not write or rewrite the text. If the student cannot explain why a sentence is in a piece, the piece is not really theirs, and a moderator or teacher may notice. Ask the school for its policy on drafts and feedback.

## A Weekly Routine That Builds All Three Skills

Building IGCSE English communication skills alongside six or seven other subjects needs a routine that is short and regular. Here is a plan that fits roughly four to five hours a week, which can be scaled down.

| Day | Activity | Time | Skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Read a short article, annotate purpose and audience, write a three-line summary in own words | 40 min | Reading |
| Tuesday | Plan and write one directed-writing piece with PAF, timed | 50 min | Writing |
| Wednesday | Listen to a ten-minute clip, write notes, answer three self-set questions | 30 min | Listening |
| Thursday | Redraft Tuesday's piece after proofreading backwards | 40 min | Accuracy |
| Friday | Speak for two minutes on a topic, record and play back | 25 min | Speaking |
| Saturday | Read for pleasure, noting five words worth keeping | 40 min | Vocabulary |
| Sunday | Rest, or review the error list from the week | 15 min | Review |

The **error list** matters as much as it does in maths. Each week, write down the three errors that recurred in your writing: comma splices, tense drift, registers that slipped. Next week, proofread specifically for those. A short, honest list beats a long vague resolution.

## What a Good English Tutoring Session Looks Like

English is a subject where families sometimes think a tutor is unnecessary, and sometimes think a tutor can fix everything in a month. Neither is right.

A useful session usually starts with the student's own writing, brought in a few days earlier. The tutor reads it first, marks no more than three issues, and begins the lesson by asking the student what they were trying to do in a particular paragraph. The student rewrites that paragraph during the session, with the tutor asking questions, not supplying phrases. A short exercise follows on the pattern behind the error, such as three comma-splice sentences to repair. The session ends with one task for the week, small enough to be completed.

For listening and speaking, a tutor can conduct a mock conversation, give specific feedback on structure and range, and record an answer for the student to hear. This is one of the areas where a good tutor adds value that independent study struggles to replicate, because the student cannot hear their own habits.

Tutoring can run at home or online. Online works well for English, since the work is mostly reading, talking and annotated text, and the tutor can mark up a shared document live. ## When Does a Student Not Need an English Tutor?

A student does not need an English tutor when they read regularly, write confidently, receive useful feedback from their teacher and score consistently near their target. Tutoring adds most when feedback at school is thin, when a specific skill such as summary or directed writing is lagging, when speaking confidence is low, or when the student is moving between boards.

Self-study works well for English because the raw material, reading and writing, is available everywhere. The parts that are hard to do alone are getting precise feedback on writing and hearing yourself speak. If your child's English teacher offers feedback on drafts, use it first. If a month of regular practice shows no change in specific errors, a tutor is worth considering. For wider guidance on running a learning method at home, read [personalised learning in IGCSE](/blog/personalized-learning-igcse-education/).

## From IGCSE English to the IB Diploma

Many students move from IGCSE to the IB DP. The communication skills carry over directly, though the assessment changes.

In the DP, English A (Language and Literature, or Literature) has Paper 1 (guided textual analysis of unseen passages), Paper 2 (a comparative essay on two studied works) and an Individual Oral of 15 minutes, and HL students also write an HL essay of 1,200 to 1,500 words. English B, for students whose first language is not English, is built on five themes and assesses writing, listening and reading, and an individual oral. A student who has learned to identify purpose and audience, to analyse effect and to speak with control at IGCSE has done much of the groundwork. The IB's own overview of the programme is at [the IB Diploma Programme page](https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/).

## Common Parent Questions About Supporting English at Home

Parents who read little English themselves often feel unable to help. They can still help substantially.

**Create the environment.** A shelf of books at the student's level and a family habit of talking about what is read or watched supports vocabulary and listening more than any app.

**Ask for explanation, not performance.** "Tell me what that article was arguing" builds reading and speaking at once.

**Read the student's writing as a reader, not a marker.** "I got lost in the third paragraph" is feedback a teenager can use. "Your grammar is bad" is not.

**Protect writing time.** A piece drafted on a Tuesday and revised on a Thursday is better than two hurried pieces. The revision is where learning happens.

**Keep the board and syllabus straight.** Know which code your child is entered for and which series. Confirm with the school.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the most important communication skills in IGCSE English?

The most important skills are reading closely, writing for a defined purpose and audience, and, depending on the syllabus, listening and speaking with control. Close reading supports every paper. Writing for a named reader separates mid-band from high-band answers, and accurate punctuation carries meaning throughout. Check the exact assessment for your child's syllabus code.

### What is the difference between IGCSE First Language English and ESL?

First Language English assumes strong everyday English and rewards style, voice and control. English as a Second Language assumes English is an additional language and rewards accuracy and range across reading, writing, listening and speaking. The school decides which syllabus a student sits, so ask which code applies before choosing revision materials.

### How can my child improve IGCSE English writing quickly?

Plan before writing, using Purpose, Audience and Form. Then proofread backwards for the three errors your child makes most often, such as comma splices, tense drift or tone slips. Two timed pieces a week with a redraft of one of them usually shows visible change in a month. Quality of feedback matters more than quantity of pieces.

### Is speaking and listening part of the IGCSE grade?

It depends on the syllabus. In Cambridge ESL 0511, speaking counts towards the grade, while in 0510 it is reported separately. Listening is part of ESL assessment. For First Language English, check the current syllabus for how speaking and listening are reported. The school's English department can confirm what applies to your child's entry.

### How do I stop my child copying from the passage in summary questions?

Teach a three-step habit: read a section, close the page, say each point aloud in your own words, then write it down. If your child cannot say it without looking, they have not understood it yet. Marks go to relevant points in the student's own words, not to neat quotations.

### My child reads a lot but still scores low in English. Why?

Wide reading helps vocabulary and instinct, but exam marks reward specific skills: answering the question set, structuring a response, matching register and proofreading. A voracious reader who writes without planning, or who misses command words, can still lose marks. A diagnostic on two recent pieces will show which skill is lagging.

### How much English practice is enough each week?

Around four to five hours across reading, writing, listening and speaking is a realistic target for a student with a full timetable, and 2 to 3 hours is still useful if it is regular. Short daily activity beats a long weekend session. Increase the writing share in the weeks before the exam series.

### Can online lessons work for IGCSE English?

Yes. English suits online lessons because the work is reading, discussion and annotated text, all easy to share on screen. Speaking practice needs a stable audio connection. Choose a tutor who marks live on a shared document and asks the student to redraft, not one who only talks.

### Should my child take a tutor for English or for maths first?

Choose by where marks are being lost and where feedback is thinnest, not by the subject's reputation. English improvement is slow and cumulative, so starting early helps, but a student struggling badly in a calculation-heavy subject may need maths support first. Compare the last two reports and ask the teachers.

### What if my child is in the wrong English syllabus for their level?

Raise it with the school's English teacher and coordinator early in Grade 9, with the student's recent work. Changing syllabus mid-course is the school's decision and depends on the exam entry deadlines. Do not assume it is possible late in Grade 10. A tutor can help the student perform well in the current entry while the question is discussed.
