IGCSE maths past papers are the closest thing a student gets to a rehearsal of the real exam, and they pay off only when each paper is attempted under time, marked line by line against the mark scheme, sorted into types of error, and followed by targeted repair. Doing a lot of papers without that loop mostly produces a comfortable feeling and the same mistakes in the same places. Doing fewer papers properly changes the result.

This guide is written for students taking Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 or Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1), and for the parents who help them plan. It explains when to start past papers, how many are worth doing, how to attempt, mark and analyse one, how to use mark schemes and examiner reports, how to build an error log that actually changes marks, and how to pace the whole thing across the final months. It follows the method of Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, whose method starts from diagnosing a student's real errors rather than assuming a syllabus order.

The wider revision plan across subjects is in the [six-week IGCSE revision plan](/blog/igcse-revision-six-week-plan/), and the topic-by-topic list of common errors is in [top 10 mistakes in IGCSE maths](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/). This post stays on one question: how to get the most out of the papers themselves.

## Why do past papers matter so much in IGCSE maths?

Past papers matter because they show how examiners combine topics, word questions and award marks, which textbook exercises organised by topic do not. A chapter exercise tells the student which method to use before they start. An exam paper does not. Learning to choose the method, spot the trap and show enough working is a skill that only exam-style practice builds.

Three things happen in a properly used paper that cannot be replicated elsewhere.

**The method is no longer announced.** In a chapter on percentages, every question is a percentage question. In Paper 4, a percentage might sit inside a compound-interest question on the same page as a trigonometry problem and a cumulative-frequency graph. The student has to recognise what is being asked.

**Time becomes real.** A student who solves any single question correctly in ten minutes may still run out of time over a whole paper. Past papers expose pace problems that no topic worksheet can.

**Marks become visible.** A mark scheme shows that a question worth three marks is not three times one idea. It is typically a method mark, an accuracy mark and sometimes a mark for a correct statement. Seeing this repeatedly changes how a student writes.

## What do the 0580 and 4MA1 papers look like?

Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams) is tiered. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher, with two calculator papers per tier. Core tops out at grade C and Extended reaches A*.

:::stats The paper structure at a glance
2 | Papers per tier in Cambridge 0580
2 | Calculator papers per tier in Edexcel 4MA1
C | Highest grade from Cambridge Core
:::

Two consequences follow for how to use papers.

First, the student should practise on the right tier. An Extended student doing Core papers is rehearsing the wrong exam, and a Core student attempting Extended papers will mostly practise failing. If you are unsure which tier is right, the diagnostic and the school's advice come first; the tier question is covered in [IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606: a parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

Second, the non-calculator paper (Paper 1 or 2 for Cambridge) needs separate preparation. Many students let hand arithmetic decay because everything else uses a calculator. A set of non-calculator questions each week is worth doing even when the exam is months away.

Exact paper lengths, mark totals and any syllabus changes belong in the current syllabus document from Cambridge or Pearson. Check that document, or ask the school, rather than rely on older notes. Past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports reach students through the school and the exam boards' official channels, and the version the school recommends is the right one to use.

## When should a student start IGCSE maths past papers?

Start with short, section-level past-paper questions once a topic has been taught, and build to full timed papers over the last two to three months before the exam series. The early use is diagnostic: it shows whether a topic has really been understood. The late use is rehearsal: it builds stamina and pace. Waiting until the final month wastes the diagnostic value.

A workable progression across the two-year course:

- **Grade 9, after each unit.** Three to five exam-style questions on that unit, marked properly. These are not for scores; they show whether the unit stuck.
- **Start of Grade 10.** Mixed question sets that combine units from Grade 9, to practise choosing a method.
- **Three to four months before the series.** First full papers, untimed or loosely timed, to see the whole shape.
- **Final two months.** Full papers under strict timing, one or two a week, with serious marking.
- **Final two weeks.** Short timed sections, error-log repairs and rest. No panic papers.

Exam series for Cambridge run in February/March (offered only in India), May/June and October/November, and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE series run in January and May/June. The school decides which series a student sits, so work back from the school's actual date. [IGCSE exam preparation](/blog/igcse-exam-preparation-guide/) explains the series and entry decisions in more detail.

## How many IGCSE maths past papers are enough?

There is no magic number, but a useful working range is eight to fifteen carefully marked full papers on the student's own tier, plus many shorter timed sections. A student who has done six papers with deep error analysis is usually better prepared than one who has skimmed thirty. The number is limited by how many papers the student can mark and learn from properly.

:::bars Where the learning in a paper comes from (illustrative)
Attempting the paper | 25 | Quarter
Marking against the scheme | 30 | Important
Sorting and logging errors | 45 | Largest share
Repairing the weak topic | 45 | Largest share
Illustrative split of effort and value, not a research finding.
:::

That figure is a judgement about where the benefit sits. The attempt itself is the smallest part of the learning. A student who spends an hour on a paper and five minutes on marking has spent most of the time on the lowest-value part.

If papers are running low, a student can reuse older ones. Redoing a paper after a gap of a month or two is useful, because the student no longer recalls the answers and can see which errors are gone and which remain. Different series of the same syllabus are generally comparable in style, though syllabus changes mean the school should confirm which older papers still match the current content.

## How to attempt a past paper properly

The attempt should be as close as possible to the real exam. A weak attempt, with a phone nearby and the textbook open, produces scores that mislead and habits that will not survive the exam hall. The numbered process below is short enough to follow from the first paper onward.

1. **Choose the right paper.** Same tier, same calculator rule, and a series that matches the current syllabus.
2. **Prepare the desk.** Pens, pencil, ruler, protractor, compasses, an approved calculator for the calculator papers, and the formula sheet only if the board allows one. Phone out of the room.
3. **Set the time.** Use the paper's real duration from the front page, and run a visible clock.
4. **Write full working.** Treat every question as a method-mark question, even when it feels easy.
5. **Flag and move on.** If a question stalls, circle the number, note the minute, and move on. Return only after the rest is done.
6. **Stop on time.** Draw a line under the work at the time limit. Anything finished after the line is marked in a different colour.
7. **Mark the next day if possible.** A short gap helps the student read their own working honestly.

The line at the time limit is a small but powerful habit. Questions finished after time earn marks in the mark scheme but do not count towards a real exam score, and the difference between the two totals is a measure of pace.

### What about the calculator papers?

Calculator papers deserve a habit of their own. Students should know which functions they need, such as fractions, standard form, statistics mode and trigonometric functions, and should check angle mode before starting. The student's own calculator, set up the way it will be in the exam, is part of the preparation, not a detail.

## How to mark a past paper like an examiner

Marking is the most useful part of the process, and the student should do it themselves, with a pen of a different colour, with the tutor or parent checking. The mark scheme shows what earns marks, not only the correct answer, and students who read it carefully begin to anticipate what examiners reward.

Cambridge mark schemes use a standard set of codes that students should recognise:

- **M marks** are method marks, awarded for a correct method even if the answer is wrong.
- **A marks** are accuracy marks, usually dependent on an earlier method mark.
- **B marks** are independent marks for a correct statement or value.
- **FT or follow-through** allows credit for later answers that follow correctly from an earlier error.

Mark scheme details and abbreviations vary by board and paper; read the introduction to the scheme the school provides. The general principle holds across boards: partial credit exists, and it is earned by visible working.

### A worked example of marking

Consider a typical three-mark question: "A shop reduces the price of a jacket by 15%. The sale price is 204. Find the original price."

A student writes: 204 ÷ 0.85 = 240.

The student has the right answer and will probably score full marks if the working is accepted. But consider a second student who writes: 204 + 15% of 204 = 204 + 30.6 = 234.6.

That is the classic reverse-percentage error: treating 204 as the original rather than 85% of the original. The answer is wrong, and the method is wrong, so no method mark is likely. The correct approach is to recognise that 204 represents 100% minus 15% = 85% of the original price, so original = 204 ÷ 0.85 = 240.

The marking lesson is not "the answer is 240". It is: this student confuses a percentage of the sale price with a percentage of the original. That is a concept error, and it belongs in the error log under "reverse percentages", with a repair task of four reverse-percentage questions.

### Mark generously or strictly?

Strictly. Self-marking tends towards kindness: "I meant that" or "I would have got that if I had been more careful". A careless slip still costs the mark in the exam. A rule that works is to award a mark only if a stranger could award it from the page alone.

## How do you use examiner reports and mark schemes together?

Use mark schemes to see how marks are awarded on specific questions, and use examiner reports to see which mistakes were common across all candidates and why. The mark scheme shows what a good answer earns. The report shows the typical failures: the sign slip, the unread instruction, the unsupported statement. Together they tell a student where marks are most often lost.

Cambridge publishes principal examiner reports for each series, and Pearson publishes examiners' reports for International GCSE papers. They reach students through the school and the boards' official channels. A reasonable approach is to read the report for the paper just attempted after marking it, and to note any comment that matches the student's own error.

Look for three kinds of comment:

- **Instruction errors.** For example, candidates giving the answer to the wrong degree of accuracy or forgetting to give a reason when one is asked.
- **Method errors.** Typical wrong approaches, which the student can compare with their own.
- **Presentation errors.** For example, showing no working on questions that clearly invited it.

Not every report comment is useful to every student, and the reports are written for teachers. A tutor can help translate them into a short list of habits.

## How to build an error log that changes your marks

An error log is a list of every lost mark, with its cause and the repair. Its power is not in the writing, but in the retesting: each entry produces a task, and the task is done again later to check the error has gone. Without retesting, the log is a diary of regret.

Use six columns:

| Column | What goes in it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Paper and question | Where the mark was lost | Paper 4, Q7(b) |
| Topic | The syllabus area | Reverse percentages |
| Error type | One of a fixed list | Concept error |
| What I did | Honest description | Added 15% to the sale price |
| Correct method | Short, in the student's words | Sale price is 85% of original; divide by 0.85 |
| Repair task | Specific and dated | Four reverse-percentage questions, Thursday |

Keep the error-type list short so that sorting is quick:

- **Concept:** did not know or misunderstood the idea.
- **Method:** knew the idea but chose a poor route.
- **Arithmetic:** correct method, calculation slip.
- **Reading:** misread or ignored part of the question.
- **Presentation:** working missing, units or accuracy ignored.
- **Time:** left unfinished or rushed.

Over three or four papers, the count in each category tells the student what kind of problem they have. A student whose log is mostly arithmetic and reading errors does not need more content teaching; they need slower reading, a check routine and non-calculator practice. A student whose log is mostly concept errors in a handful of topics needs targeted teaching before more papers.

:::steps The past-paper loop
Attempt | Timed, exam conditions, full working
Mark | Against the scheme, strictly
Sort | Each lost mark gets an error type
Repair | Targeted practice on the weak topic
Retest | Same error type, a week later
:::

## What does a good repair task look like?

A good repair task is small, specific and aimed at the cause of the lost mark. After marking, the student should not simply attempt another full paper. If the lost marks came from algebraic simplification, the repair is a short set of simplification questions that move from basic to exam-style. If they came from reading errors, the repair is a set of questions where the student underlines the instruction and the unit before starting.

A sequence for a student who keeps dropping marks on expanding and simplifying brackets might be:

- Start simple: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12.
- Add a negative: 2(x + 5) − 3(x − 2) = 2x + 10 − 3x + 6 = 16 − x.
- Move to an exam-style context: expressions inside an area or perimeter question, where the algebra serves a larger problem.
- Retest in a mixed set a week later.

The three steps move from isolated skill to embedded use, which is the same route the exam asks the student to take. A student who only ever practises the isolated skill may still fail when it appears inside a longer problem.

## Time management inside a paper

Past papers are the only place to practise pace. Students often discover that they finish the first half quickly and then lose several minutes on a single stubborn question. Recording the time spent on each section during timed practice shows where this happens and makes the fix concrete.

Some habits are worth building early:

- **A marks-to-minutes rule.** As a rough guide, a mark should take about a minute or a little under, so a four-mark question should not be consuming ten. Check the specific paper's marks and duration, since the ratio differs by paper and tier.
- **A leave-and-return rule.** If no progress has been made in the time a question deserves, move on and mark it clearly to return to.
- **A reserved checking window.** Leave a few minutes at the end, and use it with a plan: calculation questions, answers with units, rounding, and any question where a formula was used.
- **A check that everything has been attempted.** Blank answers score nothing; even a start can earn a method mark.

Students who finish with time to spare often waste it by re-reading without a plan. A targeted check, such as substituting an answer back into an equation or estimating a calculation, finds errors; a rereading usually does not.

## A worked example of checking and estimating

Take the quadratic x² − 5x + 6 = 0. A student factorises as (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 and states x = 2 or x = 3. The checking habit is to substitute each root: 2² − 5(2) + 6 = 4 − 10 + 6 = 0, and 3² − 5(3) + 6 = 9 − 15 + 6 = 0. Both work, so the answer stands.

Now consider a slip. A student expands (x − 2)(x − 3) as x² − 5x − 6. Substituting x = 2 gives 4 − 10 − 6 = −12, not 0. The check exposes the error immediately, before the mark is lost.

Estimation catches a different group of errors. If a student calculates the square root of 81 and obtains a number far from 9, something has gone wrong. In questions involving percentages, money or measurement, a quick estimate of the expected size of an answer is a cheap sanity check. These habits are small, but across a paper they are often the difference of several marks.

## What does a good three-month past-paper plan look like?

A good plan moves from topic questions to mixed sets to full papers, with marking and repair built into every week. It leaves space for rest and does not assume the student is doing nothing else. The plan below is a template that a tutor can adapt to the school's calendar and the student's error log; for a complete study plan, see [the three-month study plan for IGCSE Maths 0580](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/).

:::timeline Past papers across the last three months
Month 3 | Mixed topic sets, first full papers untimed
Month 2 | One timed paper a week, full marking
Month 1 | Two papers a week, error log drives topics
Final fortnight | Short timed sections, light repair, rest
:::

### A sample week in the middle of the plan

| Day | Task | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Repair task from last paper's log | 40 minutes |
| Tuesday | Non-calculator set | 30 minutes |
| Wednesday | Mixed exam-style questions on two topics | 45 minutes |
| Thursday | Repair retest on last week's error type | 30 minutes |
| Friday | Light revision of formulae and methods | 20 minutes |
| Saturday | One full timed paper | Paper duration |
| Sunday | Marking, error log, next week's targets | 60 minutes |

The weekend carries the heavy work, so weekday tasks stay short. A student with school tests, sport or heavy homework can swap days, but should keep the order: attempt, mark, sort, repair, retest. The point is the cycle, not the calendar.

## Mistakes students make with past papers

**Doing papers in topic order.** Selecting only the questions on the topic currently studied is useful for repair, but it does not rehearse method selection. Mix in whole papers.

**Marking too quickly.** Checking the final answer and moving on skips the method and presentation marks where most of the loss happens.

**Reusing the same papers too soon.** A student who repeats a paper after two days is testing memory, not understanding. Leave several weeks.

**Never touching non-calculator work.** The non-calculator paper often catches students who rely on the calculator for every step of fraction, surd or percentage work.

**Stopping at "careless".** Calling an error careless explains nothing. Careless errors are habits: skipped line, rushed copying, unchecked sign. They can be trained away by a specific routine such as underlining the instruction and writing each algebraic step on a new line.

**Practising only the topics they like.** Students tend to repeat what they are good at. The error log forces attention to the rest.

**Ignoring the tier.** A Core student who needs to move to Extended, or an Extended student who is struggling badly, is making a decision that should be made with the school and not by accident through the choice of papers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I start doing IGCSE maths past papers?

Start with short exam-style questions as soon as a unit is taught, and move to full timed papers about three to four months before the exam series. Early questions are diagnostic and show whether a topic has really been understood. Full papers are for stamina and pace. Waiting until the final month leaves too little time to repair what the papers reveal.

### How many IGCSE maths past papers should a student complete?

A useful range is eight to fifteen carefully marked full papers on the student's own tier, plus many shorter timed sections. The number matters less than the quality of marking and repair. A student who completes six papers with a proper error log is usually better prepared than one who completes thirty without analysis. If papers run low, redo older ones after a gap.

### Should my child do past papers under timed conditions every time?

No. Early papers can be untimed or loosely timed so the student can learn the shape and the methods. As the exam approaches, most papers should be strictly timed with the real duration. A good mix in the last two months is mostly timed full papers, with shorter timed sections on weak topics and occasional untimed work to repair concepts.

### Where do I find IGCSE maths past papers and mark schemes?

Through the school and the exam boards' official channels. Cambridge and Pearson publish past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports, and schools can usually provide the right versions for the current syllabus. Use the board's own materials where possible, and confirm with the school that older papers still match the current syllabus content before relying on them.

### My child gets a good score in past papers but a lower grade in the real exam. Why?

The usual reasons are different conditions, generous marking and repeated papers. If practice papers are done untimed, with notes nearby or marked leniently, the score overstates readiness. Another cause is memory: a student who redoes papers too soon remembers the answers. Strict conditions, strict marking and spaced repetition give a truer picture.

### How do I know which tier my child should be practising on?

Ask the school, and use the diagnostic evidence from recent papers. Core caps at grade C and Extended reaches A*, so the decision matters for the student's options later. The right tier is usually the highest one where the student can score solidly without constant failure. The tier question is covered in the [parent guide to IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

### What is the best way to use an examiner report?

Read it after marking the matching paper and look for comments that match your own errors. The report highlights common mistakes, such as ignored instructions, wrong accuracy or unsupported statements, and often explains what examiners hoped to see. Convert useful comments into short habits in the error log, for example, "underline the degree of accuracy before starting".

### Can past papers alone get a student a top grade?

Past papers are necessary but not sufficient. They build exam skill, but a student with real content gaps needs to teach those gaps first, and papers alone will not do that. The strongest preparation combines secure content, repeated timed papers, strict marking and targeted repair. No tutor or method can guarantee a particular grade.

### My child hates past papers because they feel like constant failure. What can we do?

Reframe them as diagnostics and keep early ones short. Use papers from the right tier, start with sections the student can do well, and celebrate fixing a repeated error rather than a high score. Low confidence often improves when the student sees from the log that specific errors are shrinking. A tutor can also help by setting realistic, small targets.

### Is it worth paying for a tutor just to mark past papers?

Sometimes. If your child cannot tell why marks were lost, repeats errors, or marks too leniently, expert marking and diagnosis can be worth it, even for a few sessions. If the student already marks honestly and fixes errors, a tutor may add little. Try a short trial, then decide whether the feedback is changing how the student works.

1. **Pick one paper and set it up.** Choose a recent paper on the right tier, set the real time, and attempt it with no notes and full working.

3. **Retest in a week.** Redo similar questions on the error type and compare. Then plan the next two papers around what the log shows.
