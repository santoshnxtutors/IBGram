## What Is Concept-First MYP Maths Tutoring?

Concept-first MYP maths tutoring is a teaching order: the student first understands why a mathematical idea works, then learns to recognise it inside patterns and unfamiliar contexts, then practises expressing the reasoning clearly. Procedures and speed come after that, built on the understanding, rather than the other way round.

The reason for the order is how MYP maths is assessed. Each subject is marked against four criteria, A to D, each scored 0 to 8. For maths those are Knowing and understanding, Investigating patterns, Communicating, and Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. The criterion total out of 32 is converted into a 1 to 7 grade through IB boundaries. Only one of the four, Knowing and understanding, rewards what a conventional worksheet drills. The other three reward things a worksheet rarely trains: noticing a pattern nobody pointed out, writing a justification, and interpreting an answer in a real situation.

A student who has only ever practised procedures can be fluent and still score modestly. A concept-first approach aims at the whole set.

### The four stages Ajay Vatsyayan works through

His approach moves through Concept, Pattern, Application and Expression. The stages are sequential for a given topic and overlapping across a term.

:::steps Concept, Pattern, Application, Expression
Concept | Why the idea works, in words and pictures
Pattern | Spot it in sequences, shapes and data
Application | Use it in a context nobody pre-solved
Expression | Write reasoning a marker can follow
:::

The movement matters more than the labels. A student begins with "I know the formula" and the aim is to reach "I can explain why, notice where it applies, and write it so another person can follow." Each stage can fail separately, which is why the first session is a diagnostic and not a lesson.

## What Happens in the First Session?

The first session is a diagnostic: the tutor reviews recent marked work, watches the student solve a short mixed set aloud, sets one unfamiliar problem with no method hinted, and ends by telling the parent plainly where marks are being lost and what the first weeks will target. It is not a syllabus walk-through and not a sales presentation.

Here is how it usually runs, as a process you could hold any tutor to.

1. **Recent work comes first.** The student brings the last two or three marked pieces, including any task with teacher comments. The tutor reads the comments with the student and asks what each one meant to them.
2. **A short mixed set, solved aloud.** Not a test. The point is to hear where the thinking hesitates, because hesitation shows the weakness more reliably than a wrong answer does.
3. **One unfamiliar problem.** No method given. The tutor watches how the student begins: do they draw something, test a small case, re-read the question, or freeze.
4. **A conversation about how maths feels.** Confidence and anxiety shape results more than parents expect, and the student's own account is data.
5. **A plain-language summary to the parent.** Where marks are being lost, which of the four criteria is most affected, and what the first three to four weeks will do about it.

The diagnostic separates five different problems that look identical on a report: a conceptual gap, a procedural gap, an application gap, a communication gap and an examination-technique gap. A student who loses marks because they misunderstand ratios needs a very different intervention from one who understands everything but writes two lines where six are needed. Without that separation, the default response to a weak result is "more practice", which helps only one of the five.

## How Does a Concept-First Session Actually Run?

A typical session runs 60 minutes and has a repeating shape: a short review of last week's errors, a concept or skill block, practice where the student does the thinking, and a closing reflection. The tutor asks more than they tell.

Consider a student who arrives saying "I don't understand functions." A conventional session might start with ten function questions. A concept-first session starts with a different question: what do you think a function does?

The student may struggle to say. So the idea is built from something concrete: an input goes in, a rule acts on it, an output comes out. The tutor gives a few inputs and asks the student to predict the outputs before revealing them. The student begins to notice that 1 gives 5, 2 gives 7, 3 gives 9. Only then does the notation f(x) = 2x + 3 appear, and now it reads as a description of a relationship the student has already discovered, not a string to memorise. From there the questions escalate: what changes if the coefficient changes, what does that do to the graph, what does the rule mean if x is a number of items and f(x) is a cost, and how would you explain it to someone who has not seen it.

That one example contains all four stages, and it illustrates why the sessions look slower than a worksheet drill but are not. The student leaves with something that transfers.

### A typical 60-minute session

| Minutes | Block | What the student does |
|---|---|---|
| 0-10 | Review | Re-attempts last week's error without notes, explains why it went wrong |
| 10-30 | Concept or skill | Builds the idea with the tutor, predicts, tests, restates in own words |
| 30-50 | Practice | Solves a graded set, from familiar to unfamiliar, tutor questions rather than tells |
| 50-60 | Reflection | Writes two lines: what I understand now, what is still unclear |

The final ten minutes matter. A student who can write "I understand why the pattern gives 4n minus 1, but I cannot yet justify it for all n" has just told the tutor, and themselves, what next week is for.

### The questioning habit

Throughout, the tutor leans on a small set of questions that students are expected to learn to ask themselves:

- Why does this work?
- How do I know it is true for every case, not just the ones I tried?
- What happens if one condition changes?
- Can I show it differently: a picture, a table, an equation, words?
- Where might this mathematics appear outside a textbook?

By the end of a term, the aim is that the student asks these unprompted. That is the point at which tutoring starts to become less necessary, and it is the stated goal of the method: independence, not a permanent arrangement.

## Worked Examples of What the Method Trains

Two short examples show the difference between answering and thinking in the way MYP assessment rewards.

### Criterion B in miniature: a sequence

A student is given the sequence 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 and asked to investigate. A student trained only on formulas writes the arithmetic-sequence formula and finishes in thirty seconds. An investigation-trained student works differently: they notice the differences are all 4, they test the guess that the rule involves 4n, check it against the first term (4 × 1 = 4, but the first term is 3), adjust to 4n − 1, test it on the fifth term (4 × 5 − 1 = 19), and then write a sentence explaining why the rule must keep working: each step adds 4, so the rule grows by 4 each time.

Same answer, very different evidence. The second response shows conjecture, testing, generalisation and justification, which is what Investigating patterns is about. Practising this deliberately, rather than hoping it will emerge, is part of every term.

### Criterion D in miniature: an average that misleads

A student is given the monthly pocket money of five friends: 200, 250, 300, 350 and 2,000. They compute the mean as 620 and report it. A concept-first tutor asks: does 620 describe any of these friends? The student sees that four of the five receive far less, and that one large value drags the mean upward. The student is then asked which measure would describe a typical friend better, and why, and arrives at the median, 300. The calculation was never the hard part; interpreting it was.

This is the kind of question that separates a numerically correct answer from a high-band response to a real-life task. The criteria for that task type are laid out in [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D explained](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/), and the specific reasoning skills for pattern work are in [IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/).

## What Do Parents Receive?

With an Ajay Vatsyayan IB MYP maths tutor arrangement, the reporting is designed to make progress visible before grades move.

Parents receive a plain-language diagnostic summary after the first session, a short note every fortnight, access to the student's error log if they want to see it, and a review conversation about once a month. They do not receive a flood of worksheets or percentage-improvement promises.

The point of the reporting is to make progress legible before grades move. A school grade is a slow, noisy signal; a parent who waits for it can go months with no information. So the notes track what changes first.

| What the parent sees | Where it shows up | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|
| The diagnostic summary | After session one | Week 1 |
| Which criterion is being targeted and why | Fortnightly note | Weeks 2-4 |
| Repeated errors becoming less frequent | Error log | Weeks 3-6 |
| The student starting unfamiliar problems without asking first | Homework, in the room | Weeks 3-8 |
| Written reasoning becoming easier to follow | Marked school work | Weeks 4-10 |
| A change in school criterion levels | Reports and assessments | Often weeks 8-12 or later |

These are typical orders, not promises. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and IB Gram does not state expected improvements. If nothing in the first four rows has moved after a month of weekly sessions, the diagnosis or the match should be reviewed, and the parent should say so.

### The fortnightly note

A fortnightly note has four short parts: what we worked on, what improved, what is still difficult, and what the student should do alone before the next session. It is deliberately brief. A parent should be able to read it in two minutes and know whether to worry.

### The error log

Every session ends with the student adding to a log. Each entry names the topic, what went wrong, the cause, and the correct approach in the student's own words. The tutor does not write it for them. Over a month, patterns appear, and they are usually narrower than families expect: two or three habits cause most of the lost marks. The log makes those habits visible and trainable, rather than leaving them as vague "carelessness".

## Who Does This Approach Suit?

The Ajay Vatsyayan IB MYP maths tutor method has a clear sweet spot, and equally clear limits.

The approach suits students whose problem is diagnostic or conceptual: marks that do not match effort, a tendency to freeze when a question looks unfamiliar, strong calculation with weak explanation, or comfort with routine work and discomfort with investigations. It suits students from MYP 3 upwards most clearly, because the criteria carry real weight by then, and students who are about to meet MYP 4 and 5 content ahead of the Diploma Programme (DP).

It also suits stronger students looking for extension: harder investigations, modelling tasks and early exposure to the style of DP reasoning. Here the aim is challenge, not repair. ### Who it does not suit, or does not need to

An honest list is more useful than a sales page.

- **A student who needs homework supervision.** If the goal is to get this week's worksheet done, a general tutor or a senior student is cheaper and perfectly good. Concept-first sessions are not homework help.
- **A student who needs fluency drilling only.** Where the gap is basic arithmetic or algebra speed, repetition with a general tutor works. The diagnostic should say so, and a specialist should be willing to say it.

## How Does a Student Move From Confusion to Clarity?

A student moves from confusion to clarity when the intervention matches the type of confusion. The same report comment, "needs to improve", can mean four different things, and each calls for a different change in how the student works.

Consider a student who does well on routine class exercises but poorly on investigations. A parent reasonably thinks, "my child knows the maths, why is the grade not higher?" The likely answer is that the knowledge is stronger than the communication and application. Fifty more routine questions will not move that. What moves it is practising explanation: describing a pattern in words before using symbols, justifying each step, interpreting a result in context.

The shift that matters is in the question the student asks. Early on it is "which formula do I use?" Later it becomes "what relationship is this problem describing?" That change is the real sign of progress, and it often shows weeks before a grade. It is also what carries into the DP, where the questions are less labelled and the same habit decides whether a student can start an unfamiliar problem.

## How the Method Handles Common MYP Moments

MYP maths has predictable pressure points across the year. The method adapts to each, without duplicating the guides that own them.

### Before a criterion-based task

When a Criterion B investigation or a Criterion D real-life task is about to be set, sessions shift to practising the process on a similar, unrelated task: notice, conjecture, test, generalise, justify, interpret. The student never rehearses the school's actual task, and the tutor never writes or edits submitted work. The task is the student's own, and a tutor may teach the skill and question the reasoning but not produce the content.

### Ahead of the MYP 5 eAssessment

The MYP eAssessment in MYP 5, on-screen examinations plus ePortfolios, is optional, and each school decides whether to offer it. For students who sit it, preparation covers concept revision, unfamiliar problem-solving, timed practice and getting comfortable with the on-screen environment, so mental energy goes into the mathematics. The detailed plan is in [MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/).

### After a disappointing result

A weak result is treated as data. The student and tutor sort the lost marks by cause (knowledge, calculation, interpretation, reasoning, communication or time), rebuild the concept where the cause is conceptual, then attempt a similar question without notes. The six-week pattern is in [improving IB MYP mathematics performance](/blog/improve-ib-myp-mathematics-performance-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/), and confidence-specific work is in [MYP maths exam confidence](/blog/ajay-vatsyayan-sir-ib-myp-mathematics-exam-confidence-gurgaon/).

### When a student has switched from another board

A student arriving from CBSE or another board often has strong procedures and less practice with open investigations and written justification. The adjustment is real and usually temporary; the comparison of the two approaches is in [IB MYP maths vs CBSE maths](/blog/ib-myp-maths-vs-cbse-maths-teaching-approach/).

## Is Mathematical Language Part of the Teaching?

Yes, and it is more important than most families expect. An MYP student may understand a relationship privately and still be unable to state it. Words such as therefore, because, proportional, equivalent, increases, assumption, approximately and hence are not decoration; they are the tools of written reasoning, and Criterion C rewards their accurate use.

A short routine is used in sessions. After solving a problem, the student states the result in one sentence that a classmate could follow, then in one sentence that says what it means in context. A student who writes "the cost increases by 4 for each extra item, so ten more items add about 40" has communicated; a student who writes "4" has not. The routine takes two minutes and, repeated, changes how a student writes in school assessments.

## What Does the Tutor Do Between Sessions?

Between sessions the tutor reviews the error log and the last piece of work, chooses the next practice set so that each question exercises a specific weakness, and prepares the first unfamiliar problem for the next session. This is the preparation that a lesson fee has to cover, and it is why a thin homework sheet does not mean thin work. Parents can ask what happens between sessions and expect a specific answer.

The student's job between sessions is deliberately small: complete the chosen set, mark it, and add to the error log. A heavy homework load defeats the purpose, because the point is to build a habit the student can sustain through a busy MYP term.

### What is a concept-first approach to MYP maths?

It is a teaching order in which the student first understands why a mathematical idea works, then recognises it in patterns, applies it in unfamiliar contexts and writes the reasoning clearly. Procedures and speed are built on that understanding. It fits MYP assessment because three of the four criteria reward investigation, communication and application, not only recall.

### How is the first session different from a normal lesson?

The first session is a diagnostic. The tutor reviews recent marked work, watches the student solve a short mixed set aloud, sets one unfamiliar problem without a method, and then gives the parent a plain summary of where marks are being lost. It aims to identify which of five causes applies before any teaching plan is made.

You receive a diagnostic summary after the first session, a short note every fortnight covering what was done, what improved, what is difficult and what the student should do alone, and a monthly review. ### Can this tutor guarantee my child an MYP 6 or 7?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade. Results depend on the student's starting point, effort, attendance, school assessment and time available. A tutor can improve the quality of learning and remove specific mark losses. Treat any promise of a fixed grade as a reason to ask more questions.

### Is this suitable for a student who is struggling badly?

Yes, if the first step is a careful diagnosis and a foundation-building plan. A struggling student often has one or two root gaps, such as fractions or algebra, that make everything after them difficult. The tutor rebuilds those first, at a pace the student can manage, before returning to the criterion work. Confidence is part of the repair.

Yes, as extension. A strong student can work on harder investigations, modelling tasks, unfamiliar problems and early DP-style reasoning, so the aim is challenge, not repair. If the student is already performing at a high level and enjoying the subject, a short block of sessions may be better value than a long arrangement.

Neither is automatically better.
