Knowing how to choose the right IB MYP maths tutor starts with a surprise: the best candidate is rarely the person who can solve the hardest problem in the room. A tutor with a postgraduate degree in mathematics can still be wrong for a child in the International Baccalaureate (IB) Middle Years Programme (MYP), because MYP Mathematics is not marked by a single answer key. It is assessed against four criteria, A to D, each scored 0 to 8, and the student is rewarded for understanding, investigating, communicating and applying, as well as calculating.

This guide is for parents in any city, or abroad, who are choosing an MYP maths tutor and want a method they can run themselves. ## What does an MYP maths tutor need to know that a general maths tutor does not?

An MYP maths tutor needs to know the four assessment criteria and how tasks are marked against them, as well as the mathematics itself. MYP Mathematics criteria are A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts, each scored 0 to 8. The criterion total out of 32 converts to a final 1 to 7 grade through IB grade boundaries.

A general tutor teaches algebra, geometry, trigonometry, statistics and number well. An MYP specialist teaches the same topics and also understands why a student who got every calculation right can still lose marks:

- **Criterion B** rewards finding a pattern, stating a general rule and justifying it. A student who finds the answer for n = 5 and stops has not generalised.
- **Criterion C** rewards clear mathematical communication: correct notation, a logical sequence, and a justified conclusion. Correct answers with no reasoning earn little.
- **Criterion D** rewards applying mathematics to a real-life context, choosing a model, checking whether it fits and interpreting the result honestly.

A tutor who does not know this will give your child more practice at what the child can already do, and the criteria that are costing marks will remain untouched. That is the single most common mismatch I see.

There is a second layer. Schools often split MYP 4 and 5 mathematics into standard and extended. Extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA), so a tutor who knows where your child is headed can sequence the work. If you are choosing a tutor with the Diploma Programme in mind, the guide to [MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics tutoring](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) explains the topic map and the path to the AA or AI decision.

## How do you work out what your child actually needs help with?

Work out your child's real problem before you contact anyone by writing one sentence that names the criterion, the type of task and the evidence. "She is weak at maths" cannot be tutored. "He scores well on Criterion A but loses marks on B and D, where the task is unfamiliar" can.

Use the teacher's report and two recent marked assessments. Look for these patterns:

| What you see | Likely cause | What a tutor should do |
|---|---|---|
| Strong A, weak B | Procedures fine, but no habit of exploring patterns or generalising | Investigation routines, from specific cases to a general rule |
| Strong A and B, weak C | Understands but writes too little or uses loose notation | Practise explaining each step; teach notation and structure |
| Strong A, weak D | Cannot choose or interpret a model in a context | Real-life tasks with modelling and checking the result |
| Weak A across the board | Gap in prerequisites or concepts | Rebuild foundations before the current unit |
| Fine in class, weak in timed tests | Exam technique, anxiety or pace | Timed practice and an error log |
| Marks fluctuate by topic | Uneven understanding | Diagnostic by topic, then targeted repair |

If you do not know the criterion scores, ask the teacher for them. The report often shows levels per criterion, and the guide for parents on [reading MYP maths criteria](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/) shows how to interpret them and what to ask. For a deeper look at each criterion, [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D explained](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/) is the hub.

Equally important: do you need a tutor at all? A student on target who acts on feedback and has a manageable week does not. Hold the budget until a trigger appears. The wider decision is explored in [one-to-one IB coaching in Gurgaon](/blog/1-to-1-ib-coaching-in-gurgaon-is-it-worth-it/), and most of it applies wherever you live.

## What are the steps to follow when choosing a tutor?

The method for how to choose the right IB MYP maths tutor is six steps in order: name the problem, shortlist by fit, interview against the criteria, run a trial, decide on evidence and review after six weeks. Skipping steps is how families end up choosing on charm or on a profile that reads well.

1. **Name the problem in one sentence,** using the teacher's report and two marked pieces of work.
2. **Shortlist two or three tutors** whose profiles show MYP experience, the right year group and a mode you can use.
3. **Interview each by message or call** using the questions in the next section. Take notes.
4. **Run a trial lesson with each,** using the same tasks, and score them straight afterwards.
5. **Decide on evidence:** who found the problem, who made your child think, who has a plan.
6. **Agree a six-week review** before the first paid month ends, with a written plan and a date.

Problem | One sentence, criterion and evidence
Shortlist | Two or three with MYP experience
Interview | The same questions for each
Trial | The same tasks, scored after
Review | Six weeks, keep or change
:::

## What should you ask a prospective MYP maths tutor?

The interview is where how to choose the right IB MYP maths tutor stops being theory. Ask questions that cannot be answered with a slogan: how they diagnose, how they teach each criterion, what a typical session looks like, how they handle your child's school calendar, and what they will not do. The answer to listen for is specific and describes actions, not adjectives.

Use these questions, in this order, and listen for the response in the right-hand column.

| Question | A good answer | A weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| How do you find out what my child needs? | A diagnostic from recent work, watching the child solve, and probing earlier ideas | "I will teach the syllabus" |
| How do you teach criteria B, C and D? | Names routines: investigate cases, generalise, justify; explain steps; model a context and check | "We practise lots of questions" |
| Which MYP year and which tasks have you taught? | Specific year groups, standard or extended, and the kinds of assessment | "All levels, all boards" |
| What happens in a typical session? | Review marked work, work one priority with the student writing, set a short task | "I explain the chapter" |
| How do you use my child's school calendar? | Asks for the scheme of work and assessment dates | Does not ask |
| How do I see progress? | A written plan, marked work and a review | "Marks will go up" |
| What will you not do? | Will not do assessed tasks or investigations for the student | Cannot say |
| Can you do a trial lesson? | Yes, with a clear aim | Pushes to pay in advance |

Two further questions are worth asking when relevant. If your child is in MYP 5, ask how the tutor prepares students for the on-screen eAssessment, which is optional and which each school decides whether to offer; the details are covered in [MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/). If your child is heading into the Diploma Programme, ask how the tutor will build habits that carry across, which is the subject of the guide on [choosing a tutor who can carry a student from MYP into DP](/blog/how-to-choose-ib-maths-tutor-pathways-student-gurgaon/).

Pay attention to how the tutor talks about your child. A tutor who asks about the child, the school, the teacher's comments and the child's feelings about maths is collecting evidence. A tutor who talks mostly about themselves is selling.

## What qualifications and experience should you look for?

Look for sound mathematics, direct experience teaching MYP students at the right year group, familiarity with the criteria and the ability to explain to a young person. Qualifications matter as evidence, but the combination of MYP-specific knowledge and a record of explaining well matters more than any single degree.

Parents often over-weight three things.

- **The highest degree.** A strong mathematician can be a poor explainer of Year 8 algebra to a nervous student. Ask how they explain, not just what they hold.
- **A long list of subjects.** A tutor who teaches every board and level may be thin on any. MYP is distinct enough to need specific knowledge.
- **Testimonials and ratings.** These are easy to display and hard to verify. Ask for specifics you can test in a trial instead.

What you can rely on is verification of the profile itself. ## What should a trial lesson include?

A trial lesson is the practical core of how to choose the right IB MYP maths tutor, and it should include a short diagnostic on a real task, a prerequisite probe, a pattern-and-generalisation task, a communication task and a plan in plain words. It should leave you with evidence about how the tutor teaches, not only how pleasant they are.

Plan it before the session. Send the tutor the child's year group, the current unit and two recent marked assessments, and ask the child to attempt the tasks without help first. Then watch for five things.

### Task 1: A cold attempt

The tutor gives a problem from the current unit, slightly above comfort, and says nothing while the child works. A good tutor watches where the pen stops, notes the first method tried and asks afterwards, "what were you thinking there?" A weak tutor rescues at the first pause.

### Task 2: A prerequisite probe

The tutor steps back to one idea from an earlier year the current work depends on, for example fractions before algebraic fractions, or proportion before trigonometric ratios. Many MYP problems are a gap two steps back, and a tutor who never looks back cannot find them.

### Task 3: An investigation

This is the Criterion B test. Give a short pattern task: the number of dots in a sequence of shapes, or the handshakes in a group. The tutor should ask the child to find the answer for the first few cases, look for a rule, state it in words, then test it on a new case. A tutor who hands over the formula has removed the investigation, which is exactly where the marks are.

### Task 4: A communication task

Ask the child to write a full solution to one question as if for a marker. The tutor reads it and names one thing to improve: a missing definition of a variable, a skipped step, a conclusion that is not stated. Watch whether the feedback is specific and whether the child rewrites a line.

### Task 5: A plan in plain words

At the end, ask the tutor for three priorities, the order and how progress will be checked. "We will cover the syllabus" is not a plan. "He loses Criterion C marks because he does not show steps, so we will start with writing out solutions, and I will return marked work each week" is.

:::bars What a trial lesson should show (illustrative judgement of priorities)
Finds the real problem | 90 | most important
Child does most of the work | 80 | not the tutor
Teaches a criterion, not only a topic | 70 | B, C or D
Gives specific feedback | 65 | a line to fix
Warm and clear with the child | 55 | necessary, not sufficient
Illustrative weighting of what a parent can observe; no official data.
:::

After the trial, ask your child, privately, whether they understood what to do next and whether they would be comfortable saying "I do not get it" to this person. A child who cannot say that will waste the hours.

## How do you judge explanation quality in real time?

You judge explanation quality by whether the tutor asks before telling, uses a picture or a concrete case, ties a rule to the reason it works and checks understanding by asking the child to act. A child's nod proves nothing. A child doing the next question alone proves the explanation worked.

Consider a tutor teaching why the sum of the angles in a triangle is 180 degrees. A weak version states it and moves on. A stronger version asks the child to tear off the corners of a paper triangle and line them up, then to say what that suggests, then to ask whether it would work for every triangle, which is a question about generalisation. The second version builds a habit of questioning that carries into Criterion B tasks. The first builds recall.

Another quick check is the "explain it back" test. At the end of a topic, ask the child to teach it to you in two minutes. If they can, the tutor taught. If they can only repeat steps, the tutor demonstrated. For a closer look at how reasoning is built in each MYP year, the guide to [MYP maths reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/) shows the routines that tutors should be teaching.

## What are the red flags?

The main red flags are a promised grade, a tutor who solves problems while the child watches, no questions about the school or the criteria, no marked work returned, and any offer to complete the child's assessed tasks. One of these is enough to ask a direct question, and two are enough to stop.

- **A grade guarantee.** No tutor can guarantee a grade.
- **Doing the task for the child.** MYP investigations and tasks are assessed as the student's work. A tutor may teach the skills and ask questions, and must not supply the solution.
- **Only practice, no diagnosis.** A tutor who prescribes more worksheets without finding the cause is guessing.
- **Procedure only.** A tutor who teaches only methods and "tricks" leaves Criteria B and D untouched.
- **No questions about the school's calendar.** Without assessment dates, planning is blind.
- **A bundle before a trial.** A demo session before commitment is reasonable.
- **Dismissing the school teacher.** Good tutoring works with the school.
- **Vague answers to specific questions.** If you ask how they teach Criterion D and hear "we do real-life questions", ask for an example. If none comes, you have your answer.

## Should you choose a home tutor or an online tutor?

To choose the right IB MYP maths tutor, you must also choose a mode, and that depends on the child, not on fashion. Home sessions suit children who focus better with a person in the room and who need to be seen working; online sessions suit families far from a good specialist, with long commutes, or whose child is comfortable with a pen tablet and a shared whiteboard. Both can be excellent for MYP maths.

For families outside India, or in India outside the big cities, online is often the only way to reach a specialist, and it works well when set up properly: a camera or tablet that shows the child's handwriting live, a shared whiteboard, a quiet space and a reliable connection. Photos of finished work sent afterwards are a weaker feedback loop than watching the pen move.

For families abroad, time zones and the school calendar need care, because the northern and southern hemisphere school years differ, and an exam or assessment window at your child's school may fall at a different time of year than a tutor in India expects. ## What is a coaching centre doing differently from a private tutor?

A coaching centre teaches a group on a fixed timetable, while a private tutor builds each session around one child's work. A centre suits a child who needs structure and peers and is broadly on track; a private tutor suits a child with a specific gap, a criterion problem or a pace mismatch.

The questions to ask of a centre are the same as for a tutor, with one addition: who exactly will teach my child's batch, and how many students are in it? Criteria B, C and D are hard to teach in a large group because they rest on the child's own reasoning and writing, which needs individual feedback. ## What should the first six weeks look like?

The first six weeks should contain a diagnostic, a written plan, weekly marked work, a mid-point check and a review at the end. By week six you should be able to name your child's three most common errors and see a change in at least one.

1. **Week 1.** The diagnostic, using the current unit and one earlier prerequisite, plus recent marked work from school.
2. **Week 1 or 2.** A written plan of three to five priorities, each tied to a criterion or a topic, with the order and the reason.
3. **Weeks 2 to 4.** Each session opens with the previous task, works one priority with the child writing and ends with a short task. Marked work is returned.
4. **Week 4.** A mid-point check: what is better, what is not, what changes. A short conversation with the parent.
5. **Weeks 5 and 6.** A timed or assessment-style task under conditions close to school, with the child's own error log reviewed.
6. **End of week 6.** The review: keep, change the plan or change the tutor.

:::timeline The first six weeks with an MYP maths tutor
Week 1 | Diagnostic, prerequisite probe, school work shared
Week 2 | Written plan, first marked task returned
Weeks 3-4 | Priorities worked, error log started, mid-point check
Week 5 | Assessment-style task under timed conditions
Week 6 | Review with parent and child: keep, adjust or change
:::

Marks follow understanding and often lag by a few weeks, so one test is a weak signal. Look first at whether the child starts questions without prompting, names their own typical error and writes more complete solutions. For a longer, structured plan, the guide on a [12-week improvement plan for MYP mathematics](/blog/improve-ib-myp-mathematics-performance-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) takes diagnosis through to review in three months.

## How do you tell whether the tutoring is working?

Six weeks in, how to choose the right IB MYP maths tutor becomes how to confirm you did: the tutoring is working when your child's errors change shape: fewer repeats of the same mistake, more complete solutions, better explanations in their own words and more willingness to attempt an unfamiliar question. Grades matter, but they move later than behaviour does.

| Indicator | When it often appears | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| The child can name their typical error | Weeks 2 to 3 | "I forget to define my variable" |
| Starts unfamiliar questions without a hint | Weeks 3 to 5 | Tries a case, then looks for a pattern |
| Written solutions get fuller | Weeks 3 to 6 | Steps and reasons on the page |
| Repeat errors drop in the log | Weeks 4 to 6 | The same slip no longer appears |
| School marks move | Weeks 6 to 10 | Often a band, not a leap |
| Confidence in class | Varies | Asks a question, volunteers a method |

If there is no change in behaviour by week six, and the child has done the set work, ask the tutor to explain what they see and what they will change. If the answer is vague, change the tutor. ## What if the child does not click with the tutor?

If your child does not click with the tutor, say so early and ask for a change. A mismatch of style, pace or personality is common and is nobody's failure, and the cost of persisting for a term is far higher than the awkwardness of switching.

Before switching, talk to the tutor about what is not working. Sometimes a small change, such as slower pace, more worked examples before independent work, or a different time of day, fixes it. If it does not, move on. Through IB Gram, a family can ask for a different match. Most children recover quickly after a poor fit, but a child who decides "I am bad at maths" because of a mismatch may carry it for years, and that is the real risk.

A related issue is a child who resists tutoring entirely. Do not use a tutor as a punishment or as a reaction to one poor result. Explain the plan, let the child take part in choosing, and keep the first block short and specific. If the resistance is about workload, the better fix may be to reduce activities, not to add a session.

## How should MYP maths tutoring fit with the rest of the week?

MYP students carry a full week: school, homework, language, sport, activities, and the Personal Project in MYP 5 or the Community Project in MYP 3 or 4. A tutor session should fit into the week without crowding out sleep or the child's own practice.

Practical rules that help:

- **One subject, one session a week,** unless the child is close to an assessment.
- **A slot that avoids sport, bus journeys and late nights.** A 9 pm session on a school night is rarely a good session.
- **Practice between sessions of 15 to 25 minutes,** done on a few days, not a single long block.
- **A weekend session for assessment-style practice** if the weekdays are packed; the guide on [weekend IB tutoring in Gurgaon](/blog/weekend-ib-tutoring-gurgaon/) discusses how to split sessions without burning out.
- **A quiet space and a fixed place** where the work is done.

Timing matters too. A tutor engaged early in MYP 3 or 4 can repair foundations before extended content builds on them. For the stage-by-stage view of when to start, see [when to start IB tutoring](/blog/when-to-start-ib-tutoring-gurgaon/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important thing when choosing an MYP maths tutor?

The most important thing is whether the tutor can find your child's actual problem and teach to the MYP criteria, not only the topics. A tutor who diagnoses before teaching, makes the child do the work and returns marked feedback is more valuable than one with a longer list of qualifications. A trial lesson built around a cold attempt, a pattern task and a plan shows this directly.

### Does the tutor need to have taught the IB MYP specifically?

Yes, it matters, because MYP marking is by criteria A to D, not an answer key. A strong mathematician who has not taught MYP may spend months practising what the child can already do while Criteria B, C and D remain weak. A tutor new to the MYP can still be good if they study the criteria and are honest about it, but a tutor who dismisses the difference is a risk.

### How many trial lessons should we run?

One well-planned trial with each shortlisted tutor is usually enough, and a second only if the result is close. Give each tutor the same tasks, and score them straight afterwards. A trial that is only a conversation tells you about personality, not teaching. A demo or trial session can be arranged through IB Gram before you commit.

### Can a tutor help with MYP investigations and projects?

A tutor can teach the skills but cannot do the work. MYP tasks are assessed as the student's own, so a tutor should ask questions, model methods on different examples and point the child at the criteria, while the child writes and reasons. A tutor who offers to complete or heavily rewrite an assessed task is putting the student at risk and should not be hired.

### Is online tutoring as good as home tutoring for MYP maths?

It can be, if the setup lets the tutor see the child's handwriting live through a pen tablet, shared whiteboard or a camera on the page. Online suits families far from a specialist or with long commutes. Home suits children who focus better with a person in the room. Many families mix the two. Try both in a trial if unsure.

### How often should my child meet the tutor?

Most children do well with one session a week in maths, with short practice between sessions, rising to two in the weeks before a major assessment. The value comes from the diagnose, practise, mark and review loop, not from the number of hours. A child who does not do the between-session work will not benefit from more sessions.

### How do I know if the tutor is working after a month?

Look at behaviour before marks. By week four the child should be able to name their typical error, start unfamiliar questions without a hint and write fuller solutions, and the tutor should be able to show a plan, marked work and a mid-point review. If none of this is visible after four weeks of real effort, ask the tutor for an explanation, and change the plan or the tutor if it is vague.

### Should I choose a tutor who also teaches DP maths?

It can help, especially for MYP 4 and 5 students heading into the Diploma Programme, because the tutor can sequence the work towards Mathematics Analysis and Approaches or Applications and Interpretation. The key is that the tutor teaches the MYP criteria properly now, not that they list DP on their profile. Ask how they would prepare your child for the AA or AI choice.

### What if my child is in MYP 1 to 3? Is it too early to hire a tutor?

It is not too early if there is a specific problem, and it is often the best time to fix one, because foundations repaired in MYP 1 to 3 save effort later. If the child is on target and enjoys maths, a tutor is not needed. A student with a gap in fractions or algebra early is a good case for a short, targeted block.

### Can a tutor guarantee a higher MYP grade?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade, because the grade depends on the student's work across the criteria and the school's assessment. A good tutor raises the chance by fixing identifiable gaps and building habits. Treat any promise of a specific grade as a reason to look elsewhere.
