The fastest way to understand a good IB Internal Assessment is to put a weak one next to a strong one on the same topic and see exactly where they part ways. That is what these IB IA examples do. Each section takes a subject, shows a research question or a paragraph written badly, rewrites it properly, and says which criterion moved and why.

This post is for Diploma Programme (DP) students who are about to choose a topic, or who have a draft and a vague feeling that it is "fine but flat", and for parents who want to judge a draft without being a subject expert. It covers Mathematics, the three sciences and Economics, because those are the subjects where we see the most IA questions. It does not explain how the IA process works, what a teacher may comment on, or what a tutor may not do. That is covered in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/), and we will not repeat it here.

One caution before the examples. Every sample question and paragraph below is illustrative: written to show a pattern, not taken from a real student's submission, and not a topic to copy. Copying a topic from a list is the most reliable way to produce an IA that reads like everyone else's. Use these IB IA examples to learn what *kind* of thinking each criterion rewards, then build your own from something you actually care about.

## What Do Good IB IA Examples Have in Common?

Across subjects, strong internal assessments share four traits, and weak ones usually lack at least two.

**A narrow, answerable question.** The question names a measurable relationship, a specific model, a specific market or a specific text-world, and it can be answered with the time and equipment a school student has.

**A method chosen for a reason.** The student can say why this technique and not another. A weak IA uses a method because it was in the textbook; a strong one uses it because the question demands it.

**Analysis that interprets rather than reports.** The strong version of any graph, table or calculation is followed by a sentence that says what it means for the question. The weak version leaves the graph to speak for itself.

**Honest evaluation.** Strong students name the limitation that actually bites (a thermometer that drifts, a model that fails beyond a range) and say how much it matters. Weak students list generic flaws ("human error", "limited time") that apply to every project ever.

Keep these four in mind as you read the rest of the IB IA examples. Each rewrite below is really one of them, applied.

## How Do You Tell a Weak Research Question from a Strong One?

A weak research question is usually a topic wearing a question mark; a strong one is a claim that can be tested. If you can answer it with "it depends" and stop, or if no number, text or data set could settle it, it is not ready.

Here is a quick screen that works in almost any subject. Ask four things of your draft question.

1. What exactly will I measure, model, compare or argue?
2. Over what range, sample, period or market?
3. Could the answer plausibly be different from what I expect?
4. Can I finish this with the equipment, data and time I really have?

A question that fails the first test is a topic. One that fails the second is too broad. The third catches a question with a foregone conclusion, and the fourth catches the beautiful idea that needs a laboratory you do not have.

| Weak question | Why it fails | Stronger version |
|---|---|---|
| How does exercise affect the heart? | No variable, no range, no method | How does 60 seconds of step exercise change the resting pulse rate of Year 12 students, measured at the wrist? |
| Cricket and mathematics | A topic, not a question | How well does a quadratic model fit the flight path of a cricket ball filmed from side-on at a school ground? |
| Does inflation hurt people? | Unbounded; nothing to measure | To what extent did the 2022 increase in a named fuel tax change household demand for that fuel in one named country? |
| Why is Hamlet great? | Opinion with no analytical handle | How does repeated imagery of disease in one act construct a sense of moral decay? |
| Energy and cars | Several variables, no limit | How does tyre pressure affect the rolling distance of a toy car down a fixed ramp? |

Notice that the stronger versions are not necessarily more *interesting*. They are more *finite*. Interest comes from the student's real curiosity; finiteness is a craft skill, and it is the one most IAs lack at the start.

## IB Maths IA Examples: From Topic to Exploration

The Mathematics exploration is the same task for Analysis and Approaches (AA) and Applications and Interpretation (AI), at both HL and SL. It is 12 to 20 pages, worth 20% of the final grade, and marked out of 20 on five criteria. HL work is judged against a higher standard on criterion E. If you are still deciding between the two courses, our comparison of [IB Maths AA vs AI](/blog/ib-maths-aa-vs-ai-which-one-should-you-choose/) explains how the choice shapes the kind of exploration you can write.

:::bars Marks available in the Maths exploration (out of 20)
A Presentation | 20 | 4 marks
B Mathematical communication | 20 | 4 marks
C Personal engagement | 15 | 3 marks
D Reflection | 15 | 3 marks
E Use of mathematics | 30 | 6 marks
Bar length is each criterion's share of the 20 marks. Criterion E alone is nearly a third.
:::

### A weak Maths exploration

> Research aim: To investigate how mathematics is used in sport.

This will not survive past page two. "Sport" is a field, not a problem; there is no single mathematical tool implied, and the student will end up describing known formulae from a textbook. Criterion E, the largest at 6 marks, rewards mathematics that is sophisticated *and used correctly to reach a conclusion*. A survey of formulae cannot earn that, because nothing is being concluded.

### A strong Maths exploration, AA flavour

Consider a student who loves cricket and takes AA HL. They film a number of lofted shots from a fixed camera position, extract coordinates frame by frame, and ask: **how well does a parabolic model describe the flight, and where does it break down?**

The mathematics is chosen for a reason. They fit a quadratic by least squares by hand to a small data set, derive the launch angle and range analytically, and then add a drag term to see how the solution changes, comparing numerical output against the observed points. At HL, the move from the clean parabola to a differential equation with drag is where criterion E starts to reach the upper bands, because the student is not just applying a method; they are building a model, finding it inadequate, and improving it with tools from the course.

What makes this a good example is not cricket. It is the *arc*: simple model, evidence of failure, better model, honest comparison.

### A strong Maths exploration, AI flavour

Applications and Interpretation rewards modelling and statistics with technology. Consider a student who asks: **does the time a student spends on a revision app predict their score on the next class test, and is the relationship the same for boys and girls in my year group?** They collect anonymised data, plot, compute the correlation coefficient, perform a chi-squared test of independence or fit a regression, test whether the residuals look random, and discuss what a correlation does and does not show.

Here the weak version is the one that stops at "the correlation is 0.62, so there is a strong relationship". The strong version asks whether 0.62 is stable if two outliers are removed, whether the sample is representative, and whether the conclusion would survive a different class.

### What lifts each Maths criterion

| Criterion | The common weak habit | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| A Presentation | Chronological diary of what was done | Introduction that states the aim, then a structure a stranger can follow |
| B Mathematical communication | Calculator syntax pasted in (`2.5E-3`) and unlabelled graphs | Standard notation, labelled axes, defined variables, one worked example shown by hand |
| C Personal engagement | A line saying "I am interested in sport" | Evidence of choices, an unusual angle, a data set the student produced |
| D Reflection | One paragraph at the end saying the model was good | Short reflections throughout, plus a real limitation and what it changes |
| E Use of mathematics | Content already known from class | Mathematics beyond routine, applied correctly, with the limits of the model stated |

## IB Science IA Examples: What the Method Section Gets Right

The DP sciences, first assessed in 2025, share an IA structure. It is the scientific investigation, worth 20%, marked on four criteria of 6 marks each: Research design, Data analysis, Conclusion and Evaluation, with a maximum of 3,000 words. Students may investigate in groups of up to three, but each writes an individual report. HL and SL share the assessment structure.

:::bars Science IA criteria (6 marks each)
Research design | 25 | 6 marks
Data analysis | 25 | 6 marks
Conclusion | 25 | 6 marks
Evaluation | 25 | 6 marks
Equal weighting: the write-up is not a lab report with a bigger conclusion. Design and evaluation count as much as results.
:::

The practical meaning of equal weighting is that students who put 90% of their effort into data collection and 10% into design and evaluation are leaving half the marks under-served. Please check the current subject guide for the exact descriptors, because assessment details are revised between cycles.

### Biology: a good question versus a good-looking one

Weak: **How does temperature affect enzyme activity?**

This is a classic and not a bad idea, but written like this it has no named enzyme, no temperature range, no measurement. Teachers see it forty times a year.

Strong: **How does temperature between 20 and 60 degrees Celsius affect the rate at which catalase from potato breaks down hydrogen peroxide, measured by the volume of oxygen collected in 60 seconds?**

Now the research design practically writes itself. The independent variable has a stated range and increments, the dependent variable is measured by a method that can be explained, and the controls (enzyme volume, substrate concentration, pH) follow naturally. A design that names its controls and *explains why each matters* is what pushes a Research design criterion upward.

A more distinctive Biology question comes from local conditions. A student in Gurgaon during summer might ask how the **rate of germination of mung beans changes with the temperature of the water used to soak them**, a question that has a practical connection to cooking and to the actual climate. Teachers can tell when a question belongs to the student.

### Chemistry: honest uncertainty beats clever apparatus

Weak: **How does acid concentration affect reaction rate?** (No acid, no metal, no method.)

Strong: **How does the concentration of hydrochloric acid affect the initial rate of reaction with magnesium ribbon of fixed length, measured by gas syringe volume in the first 30 seconds?**

For Data analysis, what separates 4 marks from 6 is rarely the apparatus. It is what the student does with the numbers. A strong chemistry data section will:

- record raw data with the *right precision* for each instrument and state the uncertainty;
- process the data into rates, with a worked example for one row;
- plot with error bars and fit a line, noting whether the line really passes within uncertainty;
- propagate the uncertainty to the final result, and discuss whether the trend is distinguishable from noise.

A weak section prints a table, draws a graph through the points by eye and says "the rate increases with concentration."

### Physics: the model-versus-experiment pattern

Physics IAs improve sharply when the student compares a theoretical prediction to a measurement. Consider this question: **how well does the period of a simple pendulum follow the small-angle prediction as the release angle increases from 5 to 45 degrees?**

The weak version only measures the period at a few angles and says it "increases slightly". The strong version derives the expected small-angle result, computes the percentage difference at each angle, finds that the discrepancy grows in a particular way and relates this to the approximation used. That comparison gives both the Conclusion (a justified statement about when the model is valid) and the Evaluation (the discrepancy is partly real physics, partly timing error, and the student has separated the two) something concrete to stand on.

### What lifts each science criterion

| Criterion | Weak pattern | Stronger pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Research design | Procedure copied from a textbook | Variables justified, a trial run that changed the method, safety and ethics addressed briefly |
| Data analysis | Averages and a hand-drawn line | Uncertainty handled, an appropriate processing method, and a graph that fits the claim |
| Conclusion | Restates the trend | Answers the question, uses the data and the scientific theory, and says how far the claim can be pushed |
| Evaluation | "Human error" and "more time" | Two or three specific limitations ranked by effect, with realistic improvements |

## IB Economics IA Examples: Choosing the Article Matters

Economics is different in kind. The internal assessment is a portfolio of three commentaries, each up to 800 words, on published news articles. Each commentary draws on a different syllabus unit and a different one of the nine key concepts: scarcity, choice, efficiency, equity, economic well-being, sustainability, change, interdependence and intervention.

There is no research question in the usual sense. The decision that matters is the article, and the analysis is a diagram plus applied reasoning.

Weak: an article titled "Why prices are rising everywhere" that discusses inflation in general terms, with no single market and nothing a diagram can show.

Strong: a short article about a named government introducing a tax on one product in one market, with a stated effect on price or quantity. This lets the student draw the supply and demand diagram, show the shift, label the incidence of the tax, discuss elasticity using figures in the article, and then *evaluate* whether the policy achieved its aim for different groups.

A rule of thumb: if you cannot sketch the diagram after reading the article, it is the wrong article. And check that your three commentaries use three different units and three different key concepts, because a portfolio that repeats a unit loses breadth however well each one is written.

### A short paragraph, weak and strong

Weak: "The tax raised the price of the good. This shows how markets work. The government wanted to reduce consumption, and this was achieved."

Strong: "Because the article reports that the price rose by 12% while quantity bought fell by 4%, the implied price elasticity of demand is inelastic. Consumers therefore bear most of the burden of the tax, which weakens its aim of cutting consumption, but increases revenue. Whether this is a good outcome depends on whether the revenue is used in ways that help the lower-income households who spend a larger share of income on this good."

The strong paragraph uses the article's numbers, applies a concept, and then evaluates for stakeholders. That order (data, concept, evaluation) is what the criteria are asking for.

## What Does a Strong IA Structure Look Like in Practice?

A strong IA reads as one argument in a fixed order, with each part answering a question the reader would otherwise ask. For a science or Maths exploration that order is: the aim, the reason it matters, the method and why, the evidence, the analysis, the conclusion and the evaluation.

:::steps The spine of most strong investigations
Question | Narrow, measurable, bounded
Method | Chosen for a stated reason
Evidence | Recorded with its uncertainty
Analysis | Interpreted, not just displayed
Evaluation | Specific limits, ranked by effect
:::

Where students go wrong is not the order but the *weight*. A typical weak IA spends two pages on background the examiner already knows, one page on method, and one paragraph on evaluation. A typical strong IA spends a short paragraph on background, a careful page on why the method suits the question, and gives analysis and evaluation the room they need.

### A quick test for any section

Before you finish a section, ask: *if I deleted this, would the answer to my research question change or become harder to believe?* If not, the section is decoration. Ruthless cutting is why short, focused IAs often outscore long ones.

## Why Do Two IAs on the Same Topic Score So Differently?

They score differently because the topic is the smallest part of the mark. The difference is almost always in how the student treats uncertainty, how explicit they are about their choices, and how far their analysis goes beyond the obvious.

Consider two students who both investigate how the angle of a ramp affects the speed of a rolling cart.

**Student 1** takes three readings at five angles, averages them, plots the speed against angle and writes: "As the angle increases, the speed increases. This is because gravity pulls the cart harder." The evaluation notes "human error in starting the timer" and suggests "using more angles".

**Student 2** runs a trial first and discovers that timing by stopwatch gives a spread larger than the effect at small angles. They switch to timing the cart through two light-gate positions, or filming at a known frame rate, and report why. They calculate expected acceleration from the angle using a standard model, compare measured values, and find that the measured values are consistently lower. They attribute the gap to friction, test that by estimating a friction term from the shallowest angle, and show that the corrected model fits within uncertainty.

Same topic, same equipment. Student 2's work shows decision-making at every point, and every marking criterion can find something to reward. Student 1's work is accurate and will land in the middle bands.

:::timeline What Student 2 did that Student 1 did not
Before data | Ran a trial run and changed the timing method
During data | Recorded uncertainty with every reading
In analysis | Compared measurements with a physical model
In evaluation | Tested one limitation instead of listing five
:::

## How Much Does Personal Engagement Really Show?

Personal engagement is the most misread criterion, in Maths especially, where it carries 3 marks. It does not mean writing "I love football". It means the work shows *evidence that this is the student's own thinking*.

In practice that looks like: a choice of data source that required effort (the student collected it, rather than downloading it), a decision to change direction after something failed, an unusual connection between two parts of the course, or a question the student could only have asked because of their own experience.

| Signal | Reads as engagement | Reads as filler |
|---|---|---|
| Source of data | Collected by the student, with notes on problems | Downloaded and used without comment |
| Direction change | "My first model failed because..., so I..." | A smooth narrative with no false starts |
| Voice | Specific choices explained in first person | Generic enthusiasm in the introduction |
| Connection | Links two units or a unit and a real situation | Lists the syllabus topics used |

A student who has real curiosity shows it without announcing it. The test is whether a reader could tell the work apart from an essay on the same topic written by another student.

## Common IA Mistakes the Examples Reveal

The weak versions above share a handful of patterns. They are worth naming, because most students recognise at least one in their own draft.

**The topic that is really a field.** "Sports", "social media", "inflation", "climate". Narrow until a single number, text or diagram settles it.

**The method that was never questioned.** If the justification for the method is "this is what we did in class", the Research design or Use of mathematics criterion is capped.

**The graph with no sentence.** Every figure needs a sentence saying what to see in it and why it matters to the question.

**The conclusion that repeats the data.** A conclusion answers the research question, with the strength the evidence deserves, and stops.

**The evaluation that lists but does not weigh.** Name the two limitations that most affect your result, say by how much, and say what you would change.

**The over-large investigation.** Fewer variables, done carefully, outperform many variables done thinly.

**The last-minute data set.** If you discover in the final week that you need more data, you will not have time to analyse it properly. Plan the data collection to finish early, as the [IB DP core planning timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/) shows for how subject IAs collide with CAS, the Extended Essay and Theory of Knowledge.

## A Practical System for Turning an Idea into an IA

Use this sequence once you have an idea and want to test it before committing.

1. Write the idea as a single question with a verb, a variable or concept, and a limit.
2. List what you will measure, model or cite, and where it will come from.
3. Do a one-hour pilot: collect five data points, draw one rough graph or attempt one calculation.
4. Ask: what surprised me, and did the method work? Rewrite the question to fit what you learned.
5. Show the revised question and pilot to your teacher before investing weeks.
6. Draft the method before collecting the main data, and write down why each choice was made.
7. Draft analysis as soon as there is data, even if incomplete, so problems appear early.

The pilot is the step students skip, and it is the one that most often saves a topic. An hour of testing exposes the unmeasurable variable, the unavailable data or the formula that goes nowhere, before it costs a month.

## What About IAs in Other Subjects?

Languages, History, Geography, Psychology, Computer Science and other DP subjects each have their own internal assessment with its own task, word limit and criteria. This guide has limited itself to the subjects whose structure we can state accurately, and we would rather leave a gap than guess. For those, use the current subject guide as the authority and ask your teacher for past examples released by the school.

The principles carry over, though. In any subject, a narrower question, a justified method, interpretation instead of description, and evaluation that weighs rather than lists will move marks. Ask your teacher for the subject-specific criteria, read them against your draft, and rewrite any paragraph that earns only the lowest descriptor of a criterion.

## How Should You Use Example IAs Without Copying Them?

Use IB IA examples to learn structure and standards, never topics or wording. Read a high-scoring sample alongside its criteria and mark it yourself, then mark your own draft in the same way.

Here is a method that works. Take a sample your teacher provides or one released with the subject materials. Highlight in one colour every sentence that makes a *choice* and in another every sentence that merely *reports*. A strong sample has more choices than you expect. Then do the same on your own draft; the contrast is usually uncomfortable and useful.

What not to do: lift a topic from a sample, from a list online or from a friend's older sibling. Every IA is run through similarity checking by the school, and reusing a well-known topic with a slightly different variable invites both a weak mark and an integrity conversation. Where tutoring fits, and where it stops, is set out in the IB's integrity position, which also appears in our [Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/). A tutor may teach the skill, question your reasoning and point you at a criterion; a tutor may not write or edit your submission.

## When Does a Student Need Help With an IA?

Most students do not need a tutor for the IA. They need a clear question, a teacher who reads one draft, and a calendar. A tutor becomes useful in specific situations.

- The student can describe the topic but cannot turn it into a question that is both narrow and answerable.
- The mathematics or the statistics is beyond what they have studied and they need to learn it, not be given it.
- Data analysis is where marks keep being lost and the student cannot see why.
- The student is strong at content but weak at writing explanations of choices.
- Time is short, and the student needs help planning, not writing.

## Where the IA Sits Beside the Rest of DP Preparation

An IA competes for the same hours as exam revision, the Extended Essay, TOK and CAS. Students who treat it as a separate project often finish it in the same fortnight as mock exams. The kinder approach is to place it deliberately.

| Period | IA task | Competing demand |
|---|---|---|
| End of DP1 | Question chosen and tested with a pilot | End-of-year exams |
| Summer break | Data collection or exploration drafting | Extended Essay research |
| Early DP2 | Analysis and a full draft for teacher feedback | Mock exam preparation |
| Mid DP2 | Final write-up and checks | TOK, CAS reflections |
| Before May | Nothing: IA submitted | Exam revision |

The Extended Essay has its own process and rules, covered in [how to write an IB Extended Essay](/blog/how-to-write-a-perfect-ib-extended-essay-ee/), and for the full picture of preparation in the months before the May session, the [IB exam preparation guide](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/) is the hub. ## A Checklist Before You Submit

Read your draft against the criteria one at a time, not top to bottom. For each criterion, find the paragraph that earns the marks, and if you cannot, that is your next edit.

- The research question is narrow, bounded and answerable with the data I have.
- Each method choice has a stated reason.
- Every graph, table and calculation is followed by a sentence of interpretation.
- Uncertainty or limits of the model are stated and carried through.
- The conclusion answers the question and is no stronger than the evidence.
- The evaluation names specific limits, ranks them and proposes realistic changes.
- Sources are acknowledged as the subject requires and the word or page limit is met.
- The work is entirely my own thinking and follows the school's and the IB's integrity rules.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What makes an IB IA topic good?

A good IA topic is narrow, measurable or arguable, and genuinely interesting to the student. It should be answerable with the data, equipment and time available, and the answer should not be obvious in advance.

The best test is to try a pilot: gather a few data points or attempt one calculation in an hour. If the pilot teaches you something, the topic has room. If it is either impossible or trivial, change the question before investing more.

### Can I use an IB IA example I found online as my topic?

You should not. Using a published or shared topic with small changes invites weak marks and an academic integrity discussion, because the school checks submissions for similarity.

Treat any set of IB IA examples as a guide to structure and standards. Then choose a question from something you have observed, measured or wondered about yourself. A less glamorous but personal topic nearly always scores better than a borrowed impressive one.

### How long should an IB IA be?

It depends on the subject. The Maths exploration is 12 to 20 pages, the science investigation has a maximum of 3,000 words, and each Economics commentary is up to 800 words.

Check the current subject guide for your subject's limits. Within the limit, shorter and sharper usually beats padded; examiners reward precision and do not mark extra material that exceeds the limit.

### Is a strong IA more about the topic or the writing?

It is more about decisions. The topic matters least; what counts is how the question is narrowed, how the method is justified, how data is interpreted and how limitations are judged.

Two students can investigate the same topic and score very differently because one shows reasoning at every step and the other reports results. Writing quality matters as clarity, not as style.

### How many drafts can a teacher comment on?

The IB's academic integrity position allows a teacher to give feedback on one draft. Teachers do not rewrite or edit the work, and students should not expect them to.

Because there is only one round, make that draft as complete as you can. Our [Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/) explains how the feedback limit and the rest of the rules work together.

### Can a tutor help me write my IA?

A tutor can teach the skills behind the IA, question your reasoning and point you to a criterion. A tutor cannot write, rewrite or edit your submission.

Good tutoring on an IA looks like a session where you explain your method and the tutor asks why, then you go away and improve the draft yourself. If anyone offers to "do the IA", that is malpractice, and the consequences fall on the student.

### My IA data looks bad. Can I still get a good mark?

Yes. A good mark depends on how well you analyse and evaluate what you found, not on whether the results matched the theory. Unexpected data, honestly explained, often shows stronger thinking than neat data.

Do not discard or adjust results. Investigate why they came out this way, estimate the effect of each limitation, and say what you would do differently. That is exactly what the evaluation criterion looks for.

### What if I have already written a weak research question?

Rewrite it now, not later. Add the missing variable, range, sample or text, then check that your existing data or plan still fits.

If the data does not fit the stronger question, a small pilot or a few more data points is cheaper than building the whole report around a vague aim. Show the new question to your teacher before continuing, since they can tell you whether it is workable.

### Do IA marks change between subjects?

Yes. The Maths exploration is marked out of 20 on five criteria, the science investigation on four criteria of 6 marks each, and Economics uses a portfolio of three commentaries. Other subjects have their own tasks.

Never assume that advice from one subject carries over exactly. Use the current subject guide for the criteria and use examples from your own subject to calibrate.

### When should I start my IA?

Start thinking about the topic in DP1 and run a pilot before the summer break. Collect data or draft the exploration over the break and have a full draft ready early in DP2.

Starting early matters because the IA competes with the Extended Essay, TOK, CAS and mock exams. The [DP core planning timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/) shows how to space them out so that none of them lands in the same fortnight.
