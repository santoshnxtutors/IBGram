IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills come down to one repeated move: look at some cases, say what you notice, write a rule that covers every case, and then explain why the rule must be true. Students who can do that in writing, on a problem they have not seen before, tend to do well in Criterion B, Investigating patterns, and they carry the habit into every other criterion and into the Diploma Programme (DP) later. Students who can only follow a worked example do not, however neatly they copy it.

This guide is for students in the Middle Years Programme (MYP) of the International Baccalaureate (IB), MYP 1 to 5, and for the parents and tutors who work with them. It is not specific to any city. It explains what reasoning means in MYP maths, what Criterion B asks for at each level, how to run a reliable routine on an unfamiliar problem, how reasoning develops from MYP 1 to MYP 5, the mistakes that cost marks, and how to practise at home without a tutor. The real-life modelling side, Criterion D, is covered separately in [MYP maths problem solving](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/); this page stays on patterns, rules and justification.

A note on honesty before we start. IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills grow with practice and are not a trick, and no tutor or guide can guarantee a particular criterion level or final grade. What follows is a method that many students find clear, tested against the kind of tasks MYP teachers actually set.

## What Are Reasoning Skills in IB MYP Mathematics?

Reasoning skills in MYP mathematics are the abilities to notice structure, state it as a general rule, and justify that rule with a logical argument, using correct mathematical language. They are assessed most directly in Criterion B, Investigating patterns, but they also show up in Criterion C, Communicating, when a student explains working, and in Criterion D, Applying mathematics in real-life contexts, when a student justifies a model.

The MYP assesses mathematics against four criteria, each scored 0 to 8: A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. The criterion total out of 32 converts to a final grade from 1 to 7 through IB grade boundaries. Criterion B is therefore a quarter of the available marks, which is more than many families realise. A student can know every method in Criterion A and still lose a large share of the total if reasoning is weak.

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
32 | Criterion total before conversion
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

Three words sit at the centre of IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills, and they are often blurred.

**Verify** means to check that a rule works for particular cases. Checking five examples verifies; it does not prove.

**Justify** means to give a reason why a rule must hold in general, in words, with a diagram or with algebra. A justification is an argument, not a list of examples.

**Prove** is the strongest form of justification: a logical chain from known facts to the statement, with no gaps. In MYP, the expectation depends on the year and the task, and many teachers accept a clear, general explanation at the lower levels and a more formal algebraic argument later.

If your child's reports say "needs to justify", "show why", or "generalise", this is the area. It is often not a knowledge problem. It is a habit problem.

## What Does Criterion B Ask Students to Do?

Criterion B asks students to investigate patterns: to choose and use problem-solving techniques to discover patterns, to describe those patterns as general rules consistent with their findings, and to verify and justify the validity of those rules. At the higher achievement levels, the work should show a student who works independently on complex patterns and justifies general rules properly. Check your school's current MYP Mathematics guide and the task-specific clarification the teacher issues, because wording and expectations are set by the school within the IB framework.

In plain terms, a Criterion B task has three layers.

:::steps The three layers of a pattern investigation
Discover | Try cases, organise results, spot structure
Generalise | State a rule that covers every case
Justify | Explain why the rule must be true
:::

The levels of achievement run in bands: 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6 and 7 to 8. Roughly, the lower bands reward applying a given technique to find a simple pattern; the middle bands reward describing the pattern as a rule; the top bands reward testing, verifying and justifying the rule, often with complex patterns and with the student choosing their own approach. The detailed descriptors belong to the school and the teacher, and [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/) goes through each criterion in depth. For parents reading a report, [MYP maths criteria explained for parents](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/) is the better starting point.

Where do marks go missing? Typically at the third layer. A student discovers a pattern, writes a rule, and stops. The rule may even be right. But "I checked it for n = 1, 2, 3, 4 and it works" is verification only. The step that earns the top band is explaining why.

## How Do You Investigate a Pattern Step by Step?

To investigate a pattern, build a table of small cases, look for a constant or changing difference, state a rule in words and then in algebra, test it on a case you did not use to find it, and finally explain why it works using the structure of the pattern. This routine works for most MYP pattern tasks, from number sequences to geometric growth.

1. **Draw or compute the first four to six cases** and record them in an organised table, not scattered across the page.
2. **Look for what changes and by how much.** Differences between terms, ratios, or what is added each time.
3. **State the pattern in words**, for example "each term is 3 more than the last".
4. **Write a general rule** in terms of n, the position, and say what n means.
5. **Test the rule** on a case you did not use to find it, and on one larger case.
6. **Justify** why the rule works, using a diagram, the structure of the construction or algebra.
7. **State the limits**: for which n does the rule hold, and is there any case where it breaks?

Step 5 is where students feel lazy and step 7 is where the top-band marks sit. Let us see the whole routine on a real task.

### Worked example 1: matchstick squares

A row of squares is made from matchsticks. One square needs 4 matchsticks, two squares joined in a row need 7, three need 10.

Discover. Table of squares and matchsticks: 1 and 4, 2 and 7, 3 and 10, 4 and 13. The difference is 3 each time.

Generalise. In words: start with 4, then add 3 for each extra square. In algebra, with n squares, the number of matchsticks is 4 + 3(n − 1) = 3n + 1.

Test. For 10 squares the rule gives 31. Draw or count a construction of 10 squares: the first has 4 sticks, each of the other 9 adds 3, so 4 + 27 = 31. The rule agrees.

Justify. Why 3n + 1? Each square after the first shares one side with the previous square, so it adds only 3 new matchsticks, the top, bottom and right side. The first square is the only one that needs all 4. So the total is 4 for the first plus 3 for each of the remaining (n − 1). Another way to see it: n squares need one vertical stick at the left end, then n groups of three (top, bottom and right-hand side) giving 1 + 3n. Two ways of seeing the same rule, and both come from the structure of the picture, not from the numbers.

That last paragraph is what the top band wants. It explains the rule from the construction. A student who writes only "the rule is 3n + 1 because it works for 1, 2, 3 and 4" has verified, not justified.

### Worked example 2: why checking is not proving

Consider the expression n² + n + 41. Try n = 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on. You get 41, 43, 47, 53, 61, all prime numbers. Keep going up to n = 39 and every value is prime. A student might conclude that the expression always produces a prime. At n = 40, however, the value is 40² + 40 + 41 = 1681, which is 41 × 41, not a prime.

Another famous case: put n points on a circle and join every pair by chords. The number of regions the circle is cut into is 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 for n = 1 to 5, which looks like doubling. For six points, the count is 31, not 32. The pattern broke.

These are not tricks. They are the best argument for why the MYP asks for justification. A pattern that holds for many cases may still fail, and only a reason that covers every case can be trusted. A student who has seen these examples understands why "I tried it and it works" is not the end.

## Which Techniques Help Students Discover Patterns?

Several techniques help students discover patterns, and the skill is knowing which to try first. The main ones are tables, differences, diagrams, working backwards and trying a simpler case. Teaching students to choose among them, rather than to apply one blindly, is a big part of Criterion B at the higher levels, where the student is expected to select and use techniques independently.

| Technique | When it helps | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Table of cases | Almost always, as a first step | Squares and matchsticks |
| Finding differences | Number sequences and growth patterns | Differences constant means linear; differences of differences constant means quadratic |
| Drawing a diagram | Geometric growth, counting problems | Dots in a triangle or a staircase |
| Trying a simpler case | When the general case is overwhelming | Solve for 3 people before n people |
| Working backwards | When the end state is known | Reverse a process to find the start |
| Looking at the structure | When you suspect why, not just what | Grouping the matchsticks into threes |
| Testing extremes | To find where a rule breaks | n = 0, a negative or a very large n |

A table of differences deserves a specific mention, because it classifies patterns quickly. If the first differences are constant, the rule is linear, of the form an + b. If the second differences are constant, the rule is quadratic, of the form an² + bn + c, with a equal to half the second difference. A student who knows this can find a rule for a sequence like 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 (differences 4, 6, 8, 10, second difference 2, so a = 1) and get n² + n. Then they must still explain why the pattern is quadratic, perhaps by relating it to rectangles of size n by n + 1.

## How Do You Write a General Rule That Earns Marks?

Write the rule in words first, then in algebra, and define every symbol, including what n stands for and what values it can take. A rule without that context is incomplete. The words show you understand the pattern; the algebra shows you can generalise; the definitions show you communicate.

A well-written rule looks like this: "Let n be the number of squares in the row. The number of matchsticks, M, is given by M = 3n + 1. This holds for every whole number n of 1 or more." Notice what is included. The variable is named, the quantity is named, the equation is stated, and the domain of n is given.

Common weak versions: "The rule is 3n + 1" (no meaning attached), "It goes up by 3" (a description, not a rule), "M = 3 × n + 1 where n is the number" (n undefined). Each loses marks at the middle and upper bands.

Two further points on rules. First, there are often several equivalent forms, such as 4 + 3(n − 1) and 3n + 1. Both are correct, and a student who can show they are equal demonstrates algebraic fluency. Second, a rule that gives a correct value for the first few terms but fails for larger ones is not a rule. This is why testing on a larger case matters.

## How Do You Justify a Rule Without Formal Proof?

Justify a rule by connecting its form to the structure of the pattern: show where each part of the formula comes from in the diagram or the process. A justification of this kind is a legitimate and often expected form of reasoning in MYP, particularly in MYP 1 to 3, and it is the foundation for formal algebraic proof later.

Four styles of justification are worth practising.

**Visual argument.** Two copies of a staircase of dots make a rectangle, so the triangular number is half the rectangle.

Take the triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. The nth is n(n + 1)/2. Why? Draw the staircase with n steps, then draw a second copy upside down beside it. Together they form a rectangle with n rows and n + 1 columns, so n(n + 1) dots, and the staircase is half of that. The formula is not found by guessing. It is seen.

**Structural argument.** The sum of the first n odd numbers is n². Add 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4². Why? Each new odd number wraps an L-shaped border around the previous square: 1 is a 1 by 1 square, adding 3 makes 2 by 2, adding 5 makes 3 by 3. An L around a k by k square needs 2k + 1 dots, which is the next odd number.

**Algebraic argument.** The sum of three consecutive integers is always a multiple of 3. Call the middle one n, so the three are n − 1, n and n + 1, and the sum is 3n. That is 3 times a whole number, so it is a multiple of 3, whatever n is. This is a complete proof, short and general.

**Counterexample.** To show a rule is false, one case is enough. If a student claims "squaring always makes a number bigger", then 0.5 squared is 0.25 and disproves it.

Which style fits which task? Diagrams suit geometric patterns, structure suits sequences built by repetition, algebra suits statements about whole numbers and expressions, and counterexamples suit false claims. Students who can pick the right style show independence, which the upper bands reward.

## How Does Reasoning Develop From MYP 1 to MYP 5?

Reasoning develops in stages, from describing patterns in words in MYP 1 to proving general results algebraically in MYP 5. Schools set their own sequences, so the table below is a typical pattern, not a prescription, and you should check what your child's school covers.

:::timeline How reasoning typically grows across the MYP
MYP 1 | Spot and describe patterns in words; simple sequences
MYP 2 | Write rules in symbols; linear patterns; test with new cases
MYP 3 | Compare linear and non-linear patterns; justify with diagrams
MYP 4 | Quadratic and exponential patterns; algebraic justification
MYP 5 | Prove general statements; choose methods independently
:::

| Year | What good reasoning looks like | A practice task |
|---|---|---|
| MYP 1 | "Each shape has two more sticks than the last" with a table | Growing patterns of tiles; describe in words and extend |
| MYP 2 | A rule such as 2n + 3, with n defined | Find and test a rule for a number sequence |
| MYP 3 | A rule plus a diagram-based reason | Justify the rule for squares in a row by the construction |
| MYP 4 | A quadratic rule found from second differences, with a reason | Triangular numbers; justify with the rectangle argument |
| MYP 5 | A short algebraic proof of a general statement | Show the sum of two consecutive odd numbers is a multiple of 4 |

Many schools split MYP 4 and 5 mathematics into standard and extended, where extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches. Extended students meet more formal reasoning earlier. [The MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics topic map](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) explains the split, and the choice between the two DP maths subjects is covered in [IB Maths AA versus AI](/blog/ib-maths-aa-vs-ai-which-one-should-you-choose/).

### MYP 1 and 2: noticing and describing

At this stage the aim is the habit of looking. Students should be asked "what do you notice?" before being shown anything. Good tasks are visual: growing patterns of tiles, calendars, multiplication tables, number walls. The skill to practise is organising results in a table and saying a pattern in a full sentence. Algebra can wait for a gentle introduction in MYP 2, with the variable always tied to a meaning.

A common mistake at this stage is pattern-spotting from only two or three cases. Ask the student to find a pattern that fits the first three terms and a different one that also fits, so that they see why more cases are needed. For example, the sequence 2, 4, 8 could continue 16 (doubling) or 14 (adding 2 more each time, then 4... differences 2, 4, 6). Seeing two valid continuations teaches caution better than any lecture.

### MYP 3: generalising and explaining

The jump in MYP 3 is from "what is the rule" to "why is it the rule". Students should practise writing a rule and then a sentence beginning "This works because". Diagrams do most of the work. Linear patterns such as matchstick squares are ideal, and they should be contrasted with a non-linear pattern so that students learn to recognise the difference from the differences table.

### MYP 4: quadratic and exponential thinking

Patterns now include squares, triangular numbers and doubling. Students should compare growth types: linear adds a constant, quadratic adds an increasing amount, exponential multiplies by a constant. The rule-and-reason habit continues, with the reason increasingly algebraic. For example, they can show that (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1 by expanding, and relate it to the L-shaped border.

### MYP 5: proof and independence

By MYP 5 the best students are choosing their own methods, stating assumptions, proving general statements and evaluating the limits of their rules. Tasks may be open-ended, with no given pattern. The key shift is from following a technique to selecting one. This is exactly the skill DP Mathematics assumes. ## What Are the Most Common Reasoning Mistakes in MYP Maths?

The commonest slips in IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills are stopping at verification, writing a rule with undefined symbols, generalising from too few cases, ignoring the limits of a rule, and explaining with a restatement rather than a reason. Each has a simple fix, and each is visible on real scripts.

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Verification as justification | "It works for 1, 2, 3, 4" | Add the reason from the structure |
| Undefined variable | "The rule is 3n + 1" | State what n and the output mean |
| Too few cases | Rule from two terms | Use at least four to six cases and test one more |
| No limits | Rule given for all n without checking | State the domain; test n = 0 or large n |
| Restating, not reasoning | "It goes up by 3 because it adds 3" | Say where the 3 comes from in the picture |
| Skipping the table | Jumping to a guessed rule | Organise the data first |
| Unclear notation | Mixed symbols, missing equals signs | Use clean, consistent notation |
| Answering the pattern, not the question | A rule that is correct but not what was asked | Reread the question before writing the rule |

The one the teacher writes most often in the margin is "why?" A useful home habit is for the parent to ask the same question aloud after every solution: "Why does that work?" It does not matter whether the parent knows the maths. A student who can answer clearly to a non-specialist has understood.

## How Can Students Practise Reasoning at Home?

Students can build IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills at home in short, regular sessions: take one pattern a week, run the investigation routine, write the rule and the reason in full sentences, then check against a different method. Quality matters more than quantity, and one carefully finished investigation beats five rushed ones.

:::steps A twenty-minute home routine
Pick | One pattern or short investigation
Tabulate | At least five cases, neatly
Rule | In words, then algebra, with n defined
Reason | One paragraph on why it works
Test | One new case, and one extreme case
:::

Good sources of tasks include the school's own investigations, past MYP-style tasks the teacher shares, number and shape puzzles, and sequences from textbooks. Avoid tasks that only ask for the next term. Pick ones that ask for the rule and the reason.

A weekly pattern for MYP 3 to 5 students might be:

- Day 1: attempt the investigation alone for 20 minutes, no hints.
- Day 2: write it up in full, rule and justification.
- Day 3: swap with a parent or a friend, and answer "why?" aloud.
- Day 4: attempt a similar pattern with a different structure.

For students who find the start the hardest, a useful technique is "smaller first". If a problem has n people, try 2, then 3, then 4. If it has a large number, try a tiny one. This lowers the barrier to starting, which is where many students stall.

## How Does a Mentor Teach Reasoning in a Session?

A good reasoning session is mostly the student thinking aloud, with the mentor asking questions and withdrawing hints as the student improves. The mentor does not solve the pattern. They ask what the student sees, what they would try, and why, and they push for a reason after every claim.

Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, describes a similar approach in his own teaching: understanding before procedure, and working until the student can explain a method in their own words. In a reasoning session this looks like a short problem, a few minutes of silence, a conversation about what the student tried, and then a written solution that the student produces and the mentor reads for gaps. The mentor marks against the criterion language, not only against correctness, so the student learns what a justification looks like on the page.

Support is gradually removed. Early on, the mentor may offer a table template or a hint on what to look at. Later, the student starts the table alone. Finally, the student picks the method and states the limits of their rule. The aim is independence: a student who can open an unfamiliar task and begin without help.

## How Does Reasoning in MYP Prepare Students for the DP?

Strong IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills prepare students for the DP by building the habit of justification that DP Mathematics, and many other DP subjects, assume. DP Mathematics comes in two subjects, each at Higher Level (HL) and Standard Level (SL): Analysis and Approaches (AA), which is algebraic and proof-heavy, and Applications and Interpretation (AI), which is modelling and statistics-heavy with technology used throughout.

In AA, students prove results, manipulate algebra at length and justify steps. The MYP student who has practised showing why a rule works finds this familiar. In AI, students interpret models and justify choices, which also draws on reasoning. In the mathematics exploration (the Internal Assessment), the marks include mathematical communication and use of mathematics, both of which reward clear reasoning. The exploration is 20% of the final grade, 12 to 20 pages, marked out of 20.

Beyond mathematics, the same skill appears in sciences when a student justifies a conclusion from data, and in Theory of Knowledge when they ask how we know a claim. MYP maths is where many students first learn that an answer is not complete until it has a reason. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are reasoning skills in IB MYP maths?

They are the skills of noticing structure, stating a general rule and justifying it with a logical argument, using correct mathematical language. They are assessed mainly in Criterion B, Investigating patterns, and they also support Criterion C, Communicating, and Criterion D, Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. They develop from describing patterns in MYP 1 to proving general results in MYP 5.

### What is the difference between verifying and justifying a rule?

Verifying means checking that a rule works for particular cases; justifying means explaining why it must work for all cases. Checking five examples verifies but does not prove. A justification uses the structure of the pattern, a diagram or algebra. Criterion B rewards justification at the higher achievement levels, so students should always add the reason after testing.

### How do I help my child with Criterion B at home?

Ask "why does that work?" after every solution, and give one pattern a week to investigate. Have your child make a table, state a rule with n defined, test a new case and write one paragraph of reasoning. You do not need to know the maths. If they can explain it clearly to you, they probably understand it.

### Why does my child lose marks even when the answer is right?

Often because the working or reasoning is missing, which affects Criterion B and Criterion C. A correct final rule without a table, a defined variable or a reason shows less than a full investigation. Teachers mark the process against the criterion descriptors. Ask the teacher for task-specific feedback and compare the lost marks with the strands of the criterion.

### Is proof required in MYP maths?

It depends on the year and the task, and the school sets expectations within the IB framework. In the lower years, a clear explanation from a diagram or structure is typical; in MYP 4 and 5, especially in extended mathematics, short algebraic justifications are common. Check the school's guide and the task instructions, and ask the teacher what form of justification they expect.

### How many cases should a student test before stating a rule?

At least four to six cases to find the pattern, and then one more, preferably a larger or extreme case, to test it. Two or three cases are not enough, because different rules can fit them. The expression n squared plus n plus 41 gives primes for many values and then fails, which shows why a reason is needed.

### My child freezes at unfamiliar problems. What helps?

Start smaller: try the problem with tiny numbers, draw the first few cases and organise them in a table. The freeze is usually about not knowing where to begin, and a fixed routine removes that. Practise with low-stakes puzzles first, and praise the attempt, not the answer. Over weeks, the routine becomes automatic and the freeze fades.

### Does reasoning matter if my child is aiming for CBSE or another board later?

Yes, because reasoning improves understanding in any board, and students moving between boards often find the habit of explaining their steps useful. The IB emphasises justification more than many other systems, so a student returning to another board may find that part easy. The skill is transferable, even if the assessment style differs.

### Should my child have a tutor for MYP maths reasoning?

Only if a specific problem persists. If marks keep being lost at justification, if the student freezes at unfamiliar tasks, or if the student relies on memorised steps, a tutor can help. A good tutor diagnoses first, asks more than tells and withdraws support over time. Try a trial lesson before committing, and review after four to six sessions.

### How is Criterion B different from Criterion D?

Criterion B is about patterns and general rules, usually in abstract or structured contexts. Criterion D is about applying mathematics to real-life situations, including modelling and judging whether a model fits. Both need justification, but D adds interpreting a context and evaluating a result in that context. Practise B first for the habit of rule and reason, then apply it in D tasks.

### Can a tutor help with an MYP investigation without doing it for the student?

Yes, by teaching the skills and not writing the work. A tutor can show how to organise a table, ask questions that prompt a rule and read the student's own justification for gaps. The work submitted must be the student's own, in line with the school's academic integrity policy. A tutor who offers to write the investigation puts the student at risk.
