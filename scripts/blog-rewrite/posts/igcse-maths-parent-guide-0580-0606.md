IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 are two different Cambridge syllabuses, not two names for one course. Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 is the main maths qualification, taken by almost every Cambridge IGCSE student at either Core or Extended tier. Cambridge IGCSE Additional Mathematics 0606 is a separate, harder course, usually taken alongside 0580 Extended by students who are heading for IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA) Higher Level (HL) or A Level Mathematics. Many parents in Gurgaon meet both codes at once, often on a school letter, and are unsure which their child needs and what to do at home.

This guide is for parents of students in Grades 9 and 10 who are studying, or about to choose, Cambridge IGCSE or Pearson Edexcel International GCSE maths. You do not need to be good at maths to use it. It explains what each syllabus contains, how tiers work, how to decide about Additional Maths, what a sensible week looks like at home, and when outside help is worth considering. ## What Are IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606?

IGCSE Maths 0580 is the Cambridge International Education course in mathematics for ages roughly 14 to 16, examined through written papers with a Core and an Extended tier. IGCSE Additional Mathematics 0606 is a separate Cambridge course with no tiers and two written papers, in which a calculator is allowed, graded A* to E, covering algebra, calculus and trigonometry at a considerably higher level than 0580.

Put simply: 0580 is the course every student needs; 0606 is optional and is aimed at students who will go on to study maths seriously.

Those two sentences are worth keeping, because school letters rarely say them plainly. A student can sit 0580 alone, or 0580 plus 0606. Schools usually decide the combination in Grade 9 or the start of Grade 10, and most do not offer 0606 as a replacement for 0580. Always confirm the exact syllabus code, tier and exam series with the school, since the school makes the entries and the details change between years.

## How Are the 0580 Tiers and Papers Organised?

Cambridge 0580 has two tiers. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator). Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Core covers the essential syllabus and tops out at grade C; Extended covers everything in Core plus further topics and can reach A*.

That sentence carries the most important parental decision, so it deserves slow reading. Choosing Core is not a neutral convenience. It places a ceiling on the grade. A student who might have achieved a B or higher but is entered for Core cannot get above C, however well they do.

A few more practical points about papers, based on the official structure. Check each against the current syllabus document, because details such as marks and durations are set by Cambridge and can change.

- The two Cambridge papers for a tier split into a non-calculator paper and a calculator paper. The non-calculator paper tests arithmetic and algebra by hand, with exact answers. The calculator paper tests modelling, trigonometry, statistics and multi-step problem solving.
- Question order is not strict, but papers tend to begin with shorter questions and build towards longer ones.
- Method marks exist. A wrong final answer with correct working can still earn marks, which is why working must be written.
- Some questions ask for answers to a stated degree of accuracy. Rounding too early or giving the wrong number of significant figures loses marks.

## How Do You Decide Between Core and Extended?

Choose Extended unless there is a clear reason not to. A student who is aiming for IB Diploma Programme (DP) maths, A Level maths, an engineering degree or any course with a maths entry requirement needs the Extended tier, and a student who is unsure usually keeps more doors open by sitting Extended. Core makes sense for a student whose maths is genuinely fragile, whose other subjects will carry their future plans, and whose school agrees that C is a realistic and healthy target.

A fair way to decide uses three pieces of evidence rather than a feeling.

1. **Recent performance on Extended-level content.** Look at the last two school tests that included Extended topics, not only Core ones. A student who scores comfortably in Core questions but loses most marks on Extended questions has a real gap to close.
2. **Gaps that can be repaired.** If the gaps are in algebra or fractions, two or three months of focused work can close them. If the gaps are in understanding rather than speed, the plan needs more time.
3. **The target after Grade 10.** If the next step is IB DP with maths, Cambridge AS or A Level maths, or a competitive course at an Indian or overseas university, the decision is nearly always Extended.

When a family is on the boundary, the school's own advice carries weight because the school sees mock performance. A short diagnostic with an outside tutor, covering Extended-style questions, can also give a clearer picture before the school's deadline for entries.

Two honest cautions. First, moving a student from Core down to Extended late is harder than the reverse, because Extended content builds on Core in sequence and cannot be crammed in a few weeks. Second, entering a very fragile student in Extended can damage confidence if the gap is too wide. The answer is a plan with milestones, not a bet.

## What Does the 0580 Syllabus Cover?

The two courses overlap in algebra, so IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 reward the same foundation, but only 0580 is needed by everyone.

Broadly, Cambridge 0580 covers number, algebra and graphs, coordinate geometry, geometry, mensuration, trigonometry, transformations and vectors, probability and statistics. Extended adds further algebra (such as more advanced equations, functions and sequences), more demanding trigonometry and geometry, and further graph work. The exact topic list and the Core-Extended boundary are on the current Cambridge syllabus document; a parent can ask the school for the checklist version.

What matters more for a parent is the pattern of the course, which is cumulative. Algebra in Grade 9 is used in graphs, geometry, trigonometry and statistics by Grade 10. Fraction and percentage fluency from Grade 7 and 8 keeps resurfacing. A student who is shaky at the foundation feels every later topic as hard, not just one.

So when a student says "I hate geometry", the cause is often not geometry. It may be algebra inside a geometry question, or reading a long problem, or a calculator habit. Diagnosing the real cause saves months.

## Should Your Child Take Additional Mathematics 0606?

Additional Mathematics 0606 is worth taking when a student is strong in 0580 Extended content, enjoys algebra, and plans to take IB Mathematics AA HL or A Level Mathematics. It is not worth taking just because a friend is, or because it looks impressive on a record. It adds a real workload in Grades 9 and 10 on top of every other subject.

The 0606 syllabus includes functions, quadratic functions, factors of polynomials, equations, inequalities and graphs, simultaneous equations, logarithmic and exponential functions, straight-line graphs, coordinate geometry of the circle, circular measure, trigonometry, permutations and combinations, series, vectors in two dimensions, differentiation and integration. There are two papers, a calculator is allowed, and grades run from A* to E.

Notice what that list contains: logarithms, differentiation, integration, and series. A student who meets differentiation for the first time in 0606 arrives in the IB DP or an A Level course with a head start. Students who skip it can still succeed in AA HL, but the first term of DP1 is noticeably steeper for them. How that transition works is covered in [from IGCSE Mathematics to IB DP](/blog/from-igcse-mathematics-to-ib-dp-building-a-strong-foundation/).

## What Is a Realistic Weekly Routine for Grades 9 and 10?

A realistic routine is four short maths sessions per week, one longer weekly review, and one timed paper section every two weeks in Grade 10. It sounds modest because it is. A student who does three focused thirty-minute sessions and a weekly review for a year is better prepared than one who panics for six weeks before the exam.

:::steps A weekly rhythm for IGCSE maths
Three short sessions | Thirty minutes each, mixed topics
Weekly review | Redo last week's errors
Timed section | Fortnightly in Grade 10
Error log | Cause for every lost mark
:::

Here is what each part means in practice.

**Short sessions.** Thirty minutes at the same desk, phone away. Start with five minutes of old material, such as fractions or solving an equation, then move to the current topic. Old material is deliberate: a student who practises last month's topic this week remembers it in the exam.

**Weekly review.** Take the errors from the week's work and redo them from a blank page, without looking at the solutions. The point is to learn the method, not to recognise the answer.

**Timed sections.** In Grade 10, once a topic list is covered, give a student twenty to thirty minutes for a past-paper section and mark it with the official mark scheme. The thing to watch is not only the score but the timing: did they run out of time, or rush the first half and have ten minutes left?

**Error log.** A notebook, one line per lost mark: question, what went wrong, what to do next time. Over two months the same causes will appear again and again, and that is the plan for the next fortnight. The method is explained in [why past papers are key to IGCSE Maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/).

## How Can Parents Help If They Do Not Know the Maths?

You do not need to teach. Your role is to protect time, ask good questions and notice patterns. These five habits work for non-mathematical parents.

1. **Fix a place and a time.** The same table and the same slot, with no phone. Routine matters more than duration.
2. **Ask the student to teach you one thing a week.** Explaining a method aloud shows whether it is understood. You do not need to follow the maths; you only need to notice whether the explanation is fluent or stuck.
3. **Look at the working, not the answer.** A page of correct answers with no working is a warning. A page with working and a few wrong answers is progress.
4. **Check the calculator.** Is it the approved model, set to the right mode, and does the student know the key functions? Many marks are lost through calculator misuse.
5. **Keep the mood steady.** Maths anxiety grows when every bad mark is treated as a crisis. A calm "what will you do differently next time?" works better than another lecture.

Three things not to do. Do not do the homework for them, even to "help". Do not compare with a cousin or a classmate. Do not buy five revision books at once; one good set of past papers with mark schemes, plus the school's notes, is enough for most students.

## What Do the Grades Mean, and What Should a Parent Watch For?

Cambridge IGCSE grades most syllabuses on an A* to G scale, with some syllabuses having a 9-1 version under a different code, so confirm with the school which scale your child's entry uses. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE grades 9-1. A parent should find out three things early: which tier, which scale and which series the school has chosen.

For India, the Cambridge February/March series exists specifically for Indian schools, so Grade 10 results can arrive in time for admission to Class 11 or for IB DP and A Level entry in the same academic year. Schools decide which series they enter students for, and a student cannot choose a series independently. Some schools enter all students in the February/March series; others use May/June. Ask early, because the series date determines when your child's revision has to be complete.

The Association of Indian Universities issues equivalence for international qualifications. Families who may apply to Indian universities after Grade 12 should check current requirements with the university concerned and not assume.

## How Do You Read a School Report or Mock Result?

A school report tells you where the student stands, but only if you read beyond the headline. Look for four things.

**The tier of the mock.** If the mock was a Core paper, a score of 80% means something different from 80% on an Extended paper. Ask which was sat.

**The split between papers.** A student who scores well on the calculator paper and poorly on the non-calculator paper has a fluency gap in arithmetic or algebra by hand. The reverse suggests weak calculator habits or modelling problems.

**The topic breakdown.** If the school provides it, note the weakest two topics. If not, ask the teacher which topics lost the most marks.

**The type of mark lost.** Method marks lost because working was missing are a different problem from accuracy marks lost to slips. Ask to see a marked script and sit with your child to sort the lost marks into three piles: did not know it, knew it but slipped, did not understand the question.

Consider a student who scores 58% on an Extended mock, with most lost marks in the non-calculator paper on algebraic fractions, simultaneous equations and surds, and few lost marks in statistics. That profile points at algebra by hand, a tractable problem. A different student with the same 58% but losses scattered across every topic has a broader gap, and the plan, tier and timeline need to be discussed more carefully.

## When Is Outside Help Worth Considering?

For IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 alike, outside help is a tool, not a verdict on the student.

Consider outside help when the student is working regularly but the marks are not moving, when the same errors repeat despite effort, when the school's pace is faster than the student's, or when a new topic such as 0606 content needs a specialist. You may not need help at all if the student is steady, the marks are rising and the habit is in place.

What a good tutor adds is diagnosis, structure and accountability. A tutor looks at scripts and says, "these six lost marks share a cause," which a parent without maths may not see. A tutor sets a plan and holds the student to it. A tutor also tells you honestly when the tier is wrong or the target is unrealistic.

What a tutor cannot do is take the exam. No tutor can guarantee a grade. A tutor who promises one is not one to hire.

## Common Mistakes Parents Make with IGCSE Maths

Parents of students on IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 tend to repeat the same handful of errors.

**Waiting until the final term.** By then the foundation cannot be rebuilt. The best time to start support is the first sign of a pattern, not the last term.

**Treating Core as the safe option for everyone.** It caps the grade at C. Choose it deliberately, not by default.

**Adding 0606 for prestige.** It is a good choice for the right student and a heavy one for the wrong student.

**Buying a stack of resources.** One syllabus-matched set of past papers with mark schemes is worth more than five books.

**Measuring progress only by scores.** Marks move late. Habits move first: working shown, errors logged, papers timed.

**Ignoring the calculator.** The wrong mode, a model that is not permitted or a student who cannot use the table and statistics functions costs marks across the calculator paper.

**Reading only the grade.** The grade tells you where; the script tells you why. Sit down with a marked paper at least once a term. A ten-minute look at real scripts is worth more than an hour of worry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606?

0580 is the core Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics course, with Core and Extended tiers, taken by almost all students. 0606 is Additional Mathematics, a separate harder course with no tiers, two calculator-allowed papers and grades A* to E, covering topics such as calculus, logarithms and series. Students who take 0606 usually sit it alongside 0580 Extended and use it as preparation for IB AA HL or A Level Mathematics.

### Does my child have to take Additional Mathematics 0606?

No. 0606 is optional, and most IGCSE students do not take it. It is worth considering for students who are strong in 0580 Extended, enjoy algebra and plan to study maths at a higher level. It adds a real workload, so weigh it against sport, other subjects and rest. Ask the school whether it is offered, and what teacher support comes with it.

### What does the Core tier mean for my child's grade?

Core candidates sit Papers 1 and 3 and cannot get above grade C. Extended candidates sit Papers 2 and 4 and can reach A*. Core suits students for whom a C is a realistic target and who will not need higher-level maths. If there is any chance your child wants IB DP maths, A Level maths or a maths-heavy degree, ask the school about Extended first.

### Can my child switch from Core to Extended mid-course?

Sometimes, but the earlier the better, and the school has to agree. Extended content builds on Core in sequence, so a late switch needs real catch-up on topics the student has not met. Ask the teacher for a topic checklist, and consider a short diagnostic to see how large the gap is. A switch in Grade 10 after mocks is much harder than in Grade 9.

### How many hours a week should a student spend on IGCSE maths?

A steady target is about two to three hours across the week, spread over short sessions, with a longer review. In Grade 10, add a timed section every two weeks. Consistency matters more than duration. Three focused thirty-minute sessions plus a weekly review beat one marathon on Sunday, because memory needs repetition across days.

### My child understands in class but loses marks in tests. Why?

The usual causes are missing working, rushing, misreading the question, calculator mistakes or unfamiliar question styles. Look at a marked script and sort the lost marks into "did not know", "slipped" and "misread". If most are slips and misreads, the fix is technique and habit, not more content. Timed practice and an error log tend to help here.

### Should we start past papers early?

Start with topic-based questions as soon as a topic is taught, and move to full papers in Grade 10. Use official past papers for the right syllabus code and tier, and mark them with the mark scheme. Doing papers early without fixing the errors only repeats the mistakes. The method is covered in the guide on past papers linked above.

### Is a tutor necessary for IGCSE maths?

Not always. A student who is steady, working regularly and seeing marks improve may not need one. A tutor helps when marks are stuck, errors repeat, a new course like 0606 needs a specialist, or the school's pace is too fast. No tutor can guarantee a grade, so judge a tutor on their diagnosis and plan, not on promises.

### How do I know if the tutor is working?

Look for evidence beyond scores. After a month, the student should be able to explain what they are working on, repeated errors should be shifting in kind, working should be clearer and a written plan should exist. If none of that is true after four to six weeks, speak to the tutor, and if nothing changes, ask for a different match.

### What should I check with the school before choosing support?

Ask five things: the syllabus code and board, the tier your child is entered for, the exam series, whether 0606 is offered and the dates of the school's mock examinations. With those answers, any tutor or plan can be built to the actual course. Without them, support risks being aimed at the wrong target.
