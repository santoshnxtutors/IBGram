MYP maths criteria explained for parents comes down to one idea: your child is not graded on a percentage of questions answered correctly, but on four separate skills, each scored from 0 to 8. Criterion A is Knowing and understanding, B is Investigating patterns, C is Communicating and D is Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. The four scores add up to a total out of 32, and that total converts to the final 1-7 grade on your child's report.

That is why a student who "understands the maths" can still bring home a grade that surprises everyone. Criterion A may be strong while B, C or D is quietly pulling the total down, and a worksheet-heavy routine at home trains only A.

This guide is for parents of students in the International Baccalaureate (IB) Middle Years Programme (MYP), across MYP 1 to MYP 5, in Gurgaon and elsewhere. It shows how to read a criterion-based report, how scores turn into a grade, what each criterion looks like in a child's work, what to ask the teacher in a parent meeting and how to support each criterion at home without doing the work for your child. It is meant as MYP maths criteria explained for parents without jargon. It follows the method of Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, who starts from a diagnosis of where a student's marks are really going.

For a deep dive on each criterion's strands, task types and top-band work, the hub guide on [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/) goes further. This post stays on the parent's job: reading, asking and supporting, which is what MYP maths criteria explained for parents should mean in practice.

## What are the four MYP maths criteria?

The four MYP maths criteria are A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. Every MYP mathematics assessment task is marked against one or more of them, each criterion is scored from 0 to 8, and the criterion total out of 32 converts to a final 1-7 grade through IB grade boundaries.

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
32 | Maximum criterion total
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

Each criterion looks at a different thing.

| Criterion | The question it asks | What it looks like in a child's work |
|---|---|---|
| A: Knowing and understanding | Does the student know the maths and use it correctly? | Accurate methods, correct vocabulary, working that holds up |
| B: Investigating patterns | Can the student explore, find a rule and justify it? | Tables, a general rule, a test of that rule, a proof or verification |
| C: Communicating | Can the student express the maths clearly? | Notation, structure, diagrams, a line of reasoning a reader can follow |
| D: Applying mathematics in real-life contexts | Can the student use maths to solve a realistic problem and judge the answer? | Choosing a model, solving, checking against the situation, stating limits |

Not every task assesses every criterion. A unit test usually concentrates on A, sometimes with C; an investigation task concentrates on B and C; a real-life modelling task concentrates on D. So a term's report is built from several different kinds of evidence, and the mix matters when you read it.

## How do criterion levels become a final MYP grade?

Teachers award each criterion a level from 0 to 8, add the four levels to get a total out of 32, and the total converts to a final grade from 1 to 7 using grade boundaries set by the IB. The conversion is not a simple percentage, which is why a total of 20 out of 32 does not mean 62% and a grade of 4.

The levels themselves are grouped into bands, which helps when reading a report:

- **Levels 1-2:** limited achievement.
- **Levels 3-4:** an adequate or partial achievement.
- **Levels 5-6:** a substantial achievement.
- **Levels 7-8:** an excellent achievement.

For the conversion, the IB publishes boundaries for the final grade. At the time of writing, the commonly published conversion is shown below. Boundaries and reporting practice can be updated, and each school decides how and when it reports a 1-7 grade across the year, so confirm the current version with your child's school before relying on it.

| Criterion total (out of 32) | Final grade (1-7) |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | 1 |
| 6 to 9 | 2 |
| 10 to 14 | 3 |
| 15 to 18 | 4 |
| 19 to 23 | 5 |
| 24 to 27 | 6 |
| 28 to 32 | 7 |

:::bars Criterion total needed for each final grade (lower boundary)
Grade 3 | 31 | 10
Grade 4 | 47 | 15
Grade 5 | 59 | 19
Grade 6 | 75 | 24
Grade 7 | 88 | 28
Bar length is the lower boundary out of 32; confirm current boundaries with the school.
:::

Two practical lessons follow. First, the boundaries are not equally spaced, and the step from a 6 to a 7 depends on only a few extra levels across four criteria. Second, a weak criterion has a bigger effect than it seems, because it costs up to eight points of a total of 32.

### A worked example of a report

Consider a Grade 8 student whose year-end levels are A 7, B 4, C 6 and D 5. The total is 22, which falls in the grade 5 band. The parent's first reaction might be "that is average" or "maths is not the problem since A is strong". Neither is accurate.

If B had been 6 instead of 4, the total would have been 24, a grade 6. The difference between a 5 and a 6 here is two levels in one criterion, and that criterion is not about knowing the maths at all. It is about exploring patterns and justifying a general rule. So the conversation changes from "more practice" to "which skills are we missing".

This is an illustration of how the arithmetic works, not a real report. Your child's pattern will differ.

## Why can a child who understands the maths still get a middling grade?

A child can understand the maths and still get a middling grade because the grade measures four skills, and understanding covers only one of them. Strong Criterion A with weaker B, C or D is one of the most common profiles in MYP mathematics, and the reasons are usually in how the child practises, not in ability.

The common causes:

- **Practice is all procedure.** Pages of routine questions build A and little else.
- **Working is thin.** The child gets answers right in their head and writes a few lines, which costs C.
- **Investigation tasks feel unfamiliar.** A child used to being told the method may stall when asked to find a rule, which costs B.
- **Real-life tasks are rushed.** The child solves the problem and does not check whether the answer makes sense in the situation, which costs D.
- **Feedback is not turned into action.** The criterion comments come back, the grade is noted and nothing changes before the next task.

A parent can see this by comparing the four criterion levels across several tasks, not just the final grade. A consistent gap in one criterion across the term is a signal. A single low score on one task is not.

## Criterion A: Knowing and understanding

Criterion A measures whether a student knows the mathematics for their level and can select and apply it correctly, using appropriate vocabulary, in familiar and unfamiliar situations. It is the criterion most parents recognise, because it resembles traditional testing: correct methods, accurate working and understanding of terms.

What strong Criterion A looks like: the student chooses a suitable method, carries it out accurately, uses terms such as "coefficient", "gradient" or "expression" properly, and can check whether an answer is reasonable. What weak Criterion A looks like: formulas confused, calculation slips, vocabulary missing, and gaps from earlier years surfacing in new topics.

**What to do at home.** Short, regular retrieval beats long sessions. Ask your child to explain, without notes, how to solve a type of problem and why each step works. Ask them to write the three most common mistakes in a unit, and to check an answer by substituting back. If a gap from earlier years keeps appearing, say so to the teacher early, because it is much easier to repair in MYP 2 than in MYP 5.

**What to ask the teacher.** "Is the loss in A about content gaps, accuracy, or vocabulary? Is it a few topics or a pattern?"

## Criterion B: Investigating patterns

Criterion B measures whether a student can investigate a pattern: choose and apply problem-solving techniques, describe the pattern as a general rule and verify or justify that rule. It is where MYP most clearly departs from traditional mathematics, because the task is to explore, not to be told the method.

Take the sequence 2, 5, 10, 17, 26. A student who notes the differences 3, 5, 7, 9 has started. A student who then writes a general rule such as n squared plus 1, tests it with a new term, and explains why it works has done the investigation. Marks follow the whole chain: the exploration, the rule and the justification.

What weak Criterion B looks like: a table of values with no rule, a rule that is stated but not tested, or a claim without a reason. What strong B looks like: organised data, a general rule in words and symbols, a check with a new case and a short justification.

**What to do at home.** Give your child open questions that are safe to be wrong about. "What happens to the area of a square when the side doubles? Triples? Can you say why?" Ask "How do you know it will always work?" and accept a messy first answer. Do not rush to supply the rule.

**What to ask the teacher.** "In the last investigation, where did the levels slip: finding the pattern, stating the rule or justifying it?" The reasoning side of this criterion is explored further in [IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/).

## Criterion C: Communicating

Criterion C measures how clearly a student communicates mathematics: the use of mathematical language, appropriate forms of representation such as diagrams, tables and graphs, and a logical, well-organised line of reasoning. It rewards what is on the page, so a correct answer with no working scores low.

Many parents are surprised by how much this criterion turns on habits that look small. Writing each step on its own line. Defining a variable before using it. Labelling a diagram. Using an equals sign correctly, rather than chaining unrelated calculations. Giving a short statement of the result in words.

What weak Criterion C looks like: jumps from question to answer, notation mixed up, diagrams unlabelled, conclusions missing. What strong C looks like: working that a reader can follow without asking the student what they meant.

**What to do at home.** Ask your child to explain a solved problem aloud, then write it so that a classmate could follow. Use a "swap and read" game: the child reads your written solution and says where they lost the thread. Check for the same habit in your child's own work. Ask the teacher whether presentation or reasoning is costing the levels.

**What to ask the teacher.** "Can you show me one piece of my child's work where C was lost, and what a higher-level version would look like?"

## Criterion D: Applying mathematics in real-life contexts

Criterion D measures whether a student can use mathematics to solve a realistic problem: identify the relevant mathematics, choose a strategy, apply it, explain the degree of accuracy and judge whether the result makes sense in the situation. It rewards thinking about the context, not just solving.

A typical task might involve planning a school event budget, modelling the cost of a taxi ride, or analysing a set of data from a real situation. The student must decide what to include, solve, and then comment: "This answer suggests a negative number of tickets, so my model fails for small values."

What weak D looks like: a solution that ignores the situation, no check against reality, no comment on accuracy or limits. What strong D looks like: a clear statement of assumptions, a method matched to the problem, an answer with units and a sensible judgement about it.

**What to do at home.** Use everyday decisions. "Which phone plan is cheaper over a year, and for what usage does the answer change?" Ask your child to say whether the answer is realistic, and what they assumed. Encourage the sentence: "This makes sense because..."

**What to ask the teacher.** "Is D being lost in choosing the model, in solving, or in the final evaluation?" The routines for unfamiliar real-life problems are described in [MYP maths problem solving](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/).

## How do you read an MYP maths report card?

Read it in four steps: find the criterion levels, compare them across tasks, look at the final grade last and read any written comments for the verbs the teacher uses. The grade tells you where your child stands. The criterion pattern tells you what to do next. Most parents read only the first, which hides the useful information.

Follow this numbered process the next time a report arrives:

1. **Write down the four levels.** A, B, C, D for each reported period, not just the final grade.
2. **Find the lowest and highest criterion.** The gap between them is the first clue.
3. **Check consistency across tasks.** A criterion that is low in every task is a pattern. One low result is not.
4. **Convert the total.** Add the four levels, and compare the total with the school's boundaries to see how close the next grade is.
5. **Read the comments for verbs.** "Justify", "explain", "organise" and "check" point to the exact skill.
6. **Decide on two priorities.** Not eight. Two criteria or skills to work on before the next report.

Be careful with early-year reports. Some schools report criterion levels only, others report a grade at the end of the year, and the rules differ across MYP 1 to MYP 5. Ask the school how and when it reports, and whether a mid-year grade is an estimate or a final judgement.

### Why final grades are not an average of tests

Teachers use their professional judgement across the whole body of evidence, looking for the level that best describes the student's consistent performance, with attention to recent work in a criterion. So a poor first term does not permanently cap the grade, and a strong last test does not erase a weak year. Ask the teacher how the school applies this, because practice differs.

:::steps From a task to a grade
Task | Teacher marks it against criteria
Levels | Each criterion gets 0 to 8
Total | A, B, C and D add to 32
Boundary | Total maps to a 1-7 grade
:::

## What questions should you ask the maths teacher?

Ask specific questions about criteria, not general ones about effort. A parent meeting is short, and "how is my child doing?" gets a general answer. Questions about which criterion is lowest, which task showed it and what a higher level would look like get actionable answers.

| Question | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Which criterion is lowest this term, and across how many tasks? | Separates a pattern from a one-off |
| Can I see one task where that criterion was lost? | Makes the feedback concrete |
| What would a level higher look like on the same task? | Shows the next step, not just the gap |
| Is the issue knowledge, method, communication or application? | Points to the right kind of practice |
| How are the grade boundaries applied here, and when is the final grade set? | Clarifies how close the next grade is |
| Which upcoming tasks assess which criteria? | Lets you plan practice |
| Is my child in standard or extended mathematics, and how is that decided? | Matters for MYP 4 and 5 |
| What can my child do this week to move one level? | Turns the meeting into a plan |

Two more questions are worth asking at the right times. In MYP 5, ask whether the school offers on-screen eAssessment, which is optional and decided by each school; if it does, [MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/) covers the format. In MYP 4 and 5, ask about the split into standard and extended mathematics. Schools often split mathematics in these years, and extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches. [MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics tutoring](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) explains the topic map and the next step.

## How can you support each criterion at home without doing the work?

Support each criterion with a habit, not a worksheet: retrieval for A, open exploration for B, explaining aloud for C and everyday modelling for D. The aim is to make your child's thinking visible, then ask a question that nudges it, not to supply the method. If you find yourself writing the solution, step back.

| Criterion | A home habit | A question you can ask |
|---|---|---|
| A | Five-minute retrieval at the start of homework | "Without notes, how would you solve this and why does it work?" |
| B | A weekly open puzzle, with a rule to find and test | "Will your rule work for the next case? How do you know?" |
| C | Explain a solution aloud, then write it for a reader | "Could a classmate follow this without you?" |
| D | A real decision turned into a maths question | "Does that answer make sense in real life? What did you assume?" |

Keep sessions short. A child with a school day, homework and activities does not need another hour of maths; they need ten minutes of the right kind. And let them be wrong. A student who gets a pattern wrong, then finds out why, learns more than one who is handed the rule.

### The parent's role in homework

Parents with strong maths backgrounds sometimes find it harder to hold back. Resist showing a shortcut when a child is partway through an investigation. Ask: "What have you tried? What did you notice? What would you try next?" If your own maths is rusty, this is an advantage, because your questions are genuine.

For help with confidence and test nerves, a separate set of habits applies, covered in [MYP maths exam confidence](/blog/ajay-vatsyayan-sir-ib-myp-mathematics-exam-confidence-gurgaon/).

## What does a criterion profile tell you about the right kind of help?

Any set of MYP maths criteria explained for parents should end in a choice of support, so here is how to make it. The shape of the four levels tells you which kind of support to look for. Match the help to the profile, not just to the grade. A student with a 5 made of 6, 6, 4, 3 needs different work from a student with a 5 made of 4, 5, 5, 6.

| Profile (illustrative) | Likely issue | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| A high, B low | Skilled at methods, weak at exploring | Open investigations, justification practice |
| A high, C low | Knows the maths, writes too little | Layout, notation, explain-then-write routines |
| A high, D low | Solves but does not judge | Modelling tasks, "does it make sense" checks |
| A low, others moderate | Foundational gaps | Targeted repair of earlier content |
| All even and middling | Steady but not yet fluent | Retrieval plus timed mixed work |
| Volatile across tasks | Inconsistent habits or anxiety | Routines, error log, calmer test practice |

These are illustrations of patterns, not diagnoses. A tutor or teacher looking at actual work will see more. ## How is MYP maths different from the maths many parents grew up with?

MYP maths differs in what it rewards: exploration, communication and application count alongside accuracy, where traditional board maths often leans on a fixed answer key and memorised procedures. That does not make MYP easier or harder; it changes what practice is useful. A child moving from another board often feels the shift most in B and D.

Typical differences a switching student meets are criterion-referenced marking instead of a fixed answer key, tasks that carry real weight beyond end-of-year tests, wider reading and independent research, and less emphasis on memorised derivations. The comparison is explored further in [IB MYP maths vs CBSE maths](/blog/ib-myp-maths-vs-cbse-maths-teaching-approach/), and the conceptual side of MYP, including key and related concepts, in [concept-first MYP maths tutoring](/blog/concept-first-ib-myp-mathematics-tutoring-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/).

For parents who have experience mostly with traditional schooling, a useful mental shift is this: a mark in MYP is attached to a skill, not to a question. When your child loses a level, ask which skill it was.

## When is a tutor useful for criterion problems, and when is it not?

In the light of MYP maths criteria explained for parents, a tutor is useful when the child's criterion pattern shows a persistent gap that class feedback has not closed, when the parent cannot tell which skill is missing or when confidence is low enough to hold performance down. A tutor is not needed if the child is progressing, using feedback and has no persistent gap, and may be the wrong answer if the real issue is sleep, workload or a foundational gap that the school can address.

What a tutor should do, in this context:

1. **Read the report with you.** Name the weak criterion and the evidence for it.
2. **Run a short diagnostic.** Look at the child's actual work on tasks of each type.
3. **Set two priorities.** Not a long list.
4. **Teach the habit that prevents the loss.** Then retest it on a new task.
5. **Review after four weeks.** Compare criterion feedback on new tasks, not just a test mark.

What a tutor must not do is write or complete assessed work. MYP tasks must be the student's own, and the school's integrity rules apply. A tutor can teach the skill, ask questions and point to the criterion; the student writes. If a tutor offers to complete a task, treat it as a serious red flag.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is MYP maths graded?

Each task is marked against one or more of four criteria, each scored from 0 to 8. The four criterion levels are added to give a total out of 32, and that total converts to a final grade from 1 to 7 using grade boundaries set by the IB. Teachers use professional judgement across the evidence, so the grade is not a simple average of tests.

### What do the MYP maths criteria A, B, C and D stand for?

A is Knowing and understanding, B is Investigating patterns, C is Communicating and D is Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. Each looks at a different skill. A covers knowledge and correct use of methods, B covers exploring and justifying patterns, C covers clear expression, and D covers using mathematics to solve realistic problems and judging whether answers make sense.

### Why does my child understand maths but still get a middling grade?

Understanding is one criterion of four. A child may be strong in A and lose levels in B, C or D, because those reward investigation, clear communication and application. Look at the four criterion levels across several tasks to find the pattern. A consistent gap in one criterion points to the skill to build, and more routine worksheets rarely close it.

### How many criterion points make a 7 in MYP maths?

The commonly published conversion maps a total of 28 to 32 out of 32 to a grade 7, but boundaries and reporting practice can change, and each school decides how it reports. Confirm the current boundaries with your child's school. The more useful point is that a grade 7 requires strong levels across all four criteria, not just one.

### Do all MYP maths tasks assess all four criteria?

No. A unit test often assesses Criterion A and sometimes C, an investigation focuses on B and C, and a real-life modelling task focuses on D. Over a term, the mix of tasks covers all four. When reading a report, check which tasks produced each criterion level, because a level from one task is a thin basis for a conclusion.

### What should I ask at an MYP maths parent-teacher meeting?

Ask which criterion is lowest and across how many tasks, to see one piece of work where that criterion was lost, what a higher level would look like and how the school applies grade boundaries. Also ask about upcoming tasks and whether your child is in standard or extended mathematics. Specific questions get specific, usable answers.

### Can I help my child with MYP maths if I am not a maths person?

Yes. Your best tools are questions, not answers: "What have you tried?", "How do you know it works?", "Does that answer make sense?" These build criteria B, C and D, and being genuinely curious works better than showing a shortcut. For gaps in content, ask the teacher or consider a tutor, but you do not need to solve the problems yourself.

### Is a tutor worth it for MYP maths criteria?

Sometimes. A tutor helps when a criterion gap persists after teacher feedback, when you cannot tell which skill is missing or when confidence is low. A tutor adds little if your child is progressing and using feedback. Try a short trial with a clear question: which criterion is lowest, and what will change in four weeks?

### What if my child's criterion levels vary a lot between tasks?

Look for patterns in task type and conditions. Large swings often come from test anxiety, inconsistent habits or uneven topic knowledge. Check sleep and workload, then work on routines such as an error log, timed practice and a fixed method for reading questions. If swings persist, discuss them with the teacher and consider short diagnostic support.

### Can a tutor guarantee a higher MYP grade?

No tutor can guarantee a grade, and any promise of one deserves caution. A good tutor can diagnose the criterion pattern, teach the habits behind each skill and give faster feedback. Results depend on your child's effort, the school's teaching and the tasks themselves. Judge progress by criterion feedback on new tasks over several weeks.
