The honest answer to when to start IB tutoring is: when a specific, repeating problem has survived two or three weeks of the student's own effort and the teacher's help, and not before. The calendar matters less than the evidence. That said, the International Baccalaureate (IB) is built in a way that makes some moments much cheaper to fix a problem in than others, and a lot of families in Gurgaon discover that only after the cheap moment has gone.

This guide is for parents and students in Gurgaon and Gurugram who are in the Middle Years Programme (MYP) or the Diploma Programme (DP) and are asking whether to begin now, wait a term, or skip tutoring altogether. It goes stage by stage, from MYP 3 to the final months of DP2, and it says what a tutor can realistically change at each point, which signals justify starting immediately, and which situations are better handled by the school, a conversation, or simple patience.

It does not tell you that every student needs a tutor. Plenty do not. It also does not tell you to wait for a crisis, because in IB the crisis arrives late and the repair is slow.

## The short answer: a decision rule you can use today

Start tutoring when you can name the problem in one sentence, you have seen it in at least two pieces of assessed work, and the student's own corrections have not shifted it. "He loses marks in Criterion C because he does not define variables" qualifies. "She seems anxious about maths" does not yet, though it may deserve a conversation with the teacher.

The reverse also holds. If a student is scoring at or above the level the family expects, is meeting deadlines without a fight and still has spare evening hours, an outside tutor mostly adds cost and pressure. The point of a tutor is to remove a constraint, and a student with no constraint has nothing to remove.

:::steps A five-minute check before you book anything
Name it | Write the problem in one specific sentence
Evidence | Find it in two separate pieces of work
Try first | Let the student fix it for two weeks
Ask school | Speak to the subject teacher once
Decide | Still there? Start. Gone? Wait.
:::

That sequence sounds slow. It is faster than the alternative, which is hiring a tutor on a vague worry and spending the first month discovering what the actual problem is. A diagnostic should confirm a problem you already half understand, not invent one.

## What changes about timing in the IB compared with other boards

Families coming from CBSE or ICSE often carry a reasonable assumption into the IB: the real pressure is in the last year, so support can wait for the board exam. In the IB Diploma Programme that assumption fails in two ways.

First, the Internal Assessment (IA) for most subjects is written and submitted during the course, and it is worth 20% or more of a subject grade in many subjects. A weak IA habit formed in DP1 is not repaired by revision in DP2. Second, the DP is cumulative. The mathematics in the second year assumes the first year was absorbed, and a student who got through DP1 on short-term memorisation arrives at DP2 with a foundation that cannot carry the load.

In the MYP the problem is different. There is no external exam for most students in India, so nothing forces a reckoning. Grades come from criteria marked by the school, and a student can drift for two years inside a comfortable 5 or 6 while the habits that the DP will punish quietly set. If you want the criteria themselves explained in plain language, the guide to [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/) is a good place to start.

So the real timing question has two halves: when is a problem cheapest to fix, and when is the student ready to be helped. They are not always the same moment.

## How IB timing differs by stage

| Stage | What is at stake | Typical tutoring need | Urgency of a weak signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| MYP 1 and 2 | Habits, curiosity, reading and writing | Rarely needed; school and home routines usually suffice | Low |
| MYP 3 | First serious criterion-based work; algebra begins in earnest | Targeted help on a gap, short blocks | Medium |
| MYP 4 | Foundation for MYP 5 and for DP subject choices | Concept repair; criterion C and D writing | Medium to high |
| MYP 5 | Final MYP grades; Personal Project; DP entry decisions | Structured support, often subject-specific | High |
| DP1 start | Core content, new teaching style, workload jump | Bridging and routine building | High, if the student is new to the DP style |
| DP1 term 2 | Content depth; IA topic choice | Weekly support in the weakest HL subject | High |
| DP2 before mocks | IA completion; mock exam preparation | Exam technique, paper practice, IA feedback conversations | Very high |
| DP2 after mocks | Final push before the May session | Short, intense, targeted | Highest, but returns narrow |

The last row is the one that surprises people. The highest urgency does not mean the best value. By the time mock results arrive, there may be only a few months before the exams, and a tutor can improve technique and plug identified gaps but cannot rebuild a missing year. That is why this guide spends more of its length on the earlier stages than the final one.

## MYP 3: the quiet start of the real work

By MYP 3 most Indian IB schools have moved from exploration to graded, criterion-based tasks, and algebra, graphs and early geometry start to stack. This is where students who coasted on number sense in earlier years first meet work that cannot be done by intuition alone.

**What to watch for.** A student who understands the idea in class but cannot write it up is the classic MYP 3 case. In Mathematics, Criterion C (Communicating) rewards clear mathematical language and structured working, and Criterion B (Investigating patterns) rewards generalising from examples to a rule. If the student reaches the right answer with scrappy working and loses marks on both, that is a writing habit, not a knowledge gap.

**Is tutoring needed?** Usually not as a standing weekly arrangement. A better pattern is a short block of four to six sessions aimed at one named gap, such as fractions and algebraic manipulation, followed by a break. The student then returns to school practice and the family checks, a month later, whether the problem stayed fixed.

**When to wait.** If the student is behind only because a topic was introduced two weeks ago and they have not yet had a chance to practise, wait. MYP teachers often re-teach through tasks, and a student's confusion at the start of a unit is normal.

## MYP 4: where foundations become visible

MYP 4 is the year families most often underrate. Nothing high-stakes is attached to it, so it does not feel urgent, yet it is the year in which the mathematics that MYP 5 and the DP will assume is meant to settle: linear and quadratic functions, simultaneous equations, early trigonometry, algebraic fluency and a first taste of proof-like reasoning.

Many schools split MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics into standard and extended pathways, and extended is built to prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA). A student who is thinking about DP maths at Higher Level (HL) benefits from checking in MYP 4, not MYP 5, whether the foundation is strong enough. If it is not, the repair is much smaller in MYP 4.

:::stats MYP mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

**What to watch for.** Three patterns: grades that slip a band between MYP 3 and MYP 4 without a clear reason; strong Criterion A (Knowing and understanding) with weak Criteria B and C, which suggests the student can follow a method but not generalise or communicate it; and weak Criterion D (Applying mathematics in real-life contexts), which usually shows up as difficulty turning a word problem into a model.

**When to start.** If any of these patterns persists across two assessments, MYP 4 is a good time to begin weekly support for a limited period, for example one term. A useful tutor at this stage does not simply do more questions. They look at how the student reads a problem and where the working breaks. Parents wanting more on this transition can read [the difference between MYP maths and CBSE maths teaching](/blog/ib-myp-maths-vs-cbse-maths-teaching-approach/), which is the most common reason a student new to the IB falls behind in MYP 4.

## MYP 5: the last chance to arrive at the DP ready

MYP 5 has the clearest deadline of the MYP years. Final MYP grades are produced, the Personal Project is completed, the school makes recommendations for DP subjects and levels, and some schools run the optional MYP eAssessment. There is no single national MYP exam in India, so the preparation depends on what your school offers; confirm with the MYP coordinator.

In practice MYP 5 is when families who waited start asking about when to start IB tutoring, because the school conversation about DP subject levels brings the question into focus. A student told that Mathematics AA HL "might be a stretch" is being given early warning that the foundation needs work.

**Two goals, not one.** The first goal is the MYP 5 grade itself. The second, which matters more for DP success, is entering DP1 with a solid algebra, functions and trigonometry base and with a working routine for independent practice and error correction. Tutoring in MYP 5 that only chases the grade can leave the second goal untouched.

**A sensible shape.** Many families choose weekly sessions through the first half of the year, a lighter pattern during the Personal Project crunch so the student is not overloaded, and a tune-up block before any school examinations. The details are for the family and tutor to agree on; the important point is to avoid starting in the last few weeks, because the first sessions of any engagement are spent on diagnosis. For a fuller picture of how a good tutor approaches this year, see [MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics tutoring in Gurgaon](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/).

## When is the best time to start IB tutoring for the Diploma Programme?

For most DP students the best time to start is the first term of DP1, and specifically in the first four to eight weeks, if there is a clear need. For students who are new to the IB or are moving up from an IGCSE or CBSE background, that is the point where a tutor can build routines while the content is still fresh. A student with no gaps can reasonably wait until the first reports arrive.

The DP is a two-year course of six subjects, three or four at HL (240 recommended teaching hours) and the rest at Standard Level (SL, 150 hours), plus Theory of Knowledge (TOK), the Extended Essay (EE) and Creativity, Activity, Service (CAS). Indian schools mostly enter candidates for the May session, and schools set their own mock dates months earlier. Check the exact calendar with your DP coordinator; it varies by school.

The reason the first term of DP1 is such a strong moment is that the DP changes the rules in three ways at once. The workload is heavier and arrives all at once. The assessment style is new, with command terms and markschemes that reward process. And the pace is faster because there are two years to cover a syllabus that would take three in some other systems.

### DP1 start: bridging, routines and the first unit

A new DP1 student has usually just left a year of high pressure, often the IGCSE or a board exam, and then a break. They arrive underestimating how different the teaching is.

If the student is coming from a different board, or from MYP into a school with a demanding DP, a short bridging block of four to eight weeks at the start of DP1 does three jobs. It repairs any prerequisite gap in the subject, especially in maths where AA HL builds directly on advanced algebra and trigonometry. It teaches the student how IB questions and markschemes work. And it sets up a weekly rhythm for practice and error review before the workload peaks.

Students who are coming from the IGCSE side may find [from IGCSE mathematics to IB DP: building a strong foundation](/blog/from-igcse-mathematics-to-ib-dp-building-a-strong-foundation/) useful for seeing which gaps typically appear.

### DP1 term 2: when depth arrives

By the second term of DP1, topics deepen and the first major school tests land. This is also when students have to start committing to an IA topic, in subjects where the IA is topic-led, and it is when the first real signals about HL subject fit arrive.

Consider a DP1 student taking Physics HL, Mathematics AA HL and Chemistry HL who scored well in the first unit tests but is now producing marks well below the first term. A common cause is not a content gap but a time problem: the student is spending evenings on one subject's homework and nothing on the others, and the HL content has grown faster than their routine. A tutor in this situation does two things. They help with the subject, and they help the student audit where time goes. That second job is often the bigger win.

**When to start.** Term 2 is the right point to begin weekly support in the one subject that is consistently under target. Starting in term 2 rather than term 3 matters because in term 3 the student is closer to the DP1 summer examinations, and any fix then has to compete with exam stress.

### DP2: the compression year

DP2 is when IA drafts, mock exams, EE deadlines and university applications all collide. If a student has not needed a tutor until now, the question of when to start IB tutoring becomes really a question of whether any single subject needs rescue before mocks.

There are three distinct DP2 windows.

**Before mocks.** The best DP2 window. The student has time to fix technique, build paper-practice habits and repair a content gap with a margin of several months. A tutor here can do real work.

**Between mocks and the final exams.** The mock results show exactly where marks are being lost. Short, targeted tutoring, often twice a week, can address three or four high-value gaps. A tutor can improve how marks are collected, but cannot rebuild a missing course.

**The last four to six weeks.** A final review and paper rehearsal. This works best for a student who already has a strong base. A student who is meeting a topic for the first time in this window is better served by deciding which marks are realistically recoverable. See the guide to [what toppers do in the last 30 days before IB exams](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/) for a deeper treatment of that stretch.

:::timeline A typical DP2 year and when tutoring pays off
Term 1 | IA drafting; strongest window to fix a gap
Term 2 | Mock preparation; paper practice and technique
Mocks | Results show where marks are being lost
After mocks | Short targeted blocks on the highest-value gaps
Final weeks | Rehearsal; avoid starting new content
:::

## The signals that say start now

Some signals justify immediate action, whatever month it is. They are all things a parent or teacher can observe rather than a feeling.

- **A grade that fell two bands.** A drop from a 6 to a 4 within a term is information. A drop of one band in a single test is not.
- **A repeated error in the teacher's feedback.** The same comment on three pieces of work, such as "show your working" or "no link to the question", means the student is not translating feedback into change.
- **Homework that takes far longer than it should.** If two hours of homework routinely turns into four, the student may be working inefficiently or stuck on something they are embarrassed to ask about.
- **Avoidance.** A student who stops mentioning a subject, hides its worksheets or becomes irritable around it is often protecting themselves from a problem they do not know how to fix.
- **A new school, a new board or a new country.** The first term after a transition is the single most reliable predictor of a problem.
- **An IA that has stalled.** A draft that has not moved for three weeks is not a time-management quirk. It usually signals a topic or method problem.

None of these signals needs a tutor automatically. Each is a reason to find out what is going on, and a tutor is one way to do that.

## The signals that say wait

Waiting is a real option, and often the right one.

- **The problem is only a week old.** Let the student try to repair it. Most small slips correct themselves.
- **The student has not yet used the available school support.** Teachers run clinics and office hours, and some schools have peer tutoring. These are free and should come first.
- **The issue is motivation, sleep or social stress rather than subject.** A tutor cannot fix a student who is exhausted. A conversation, a schedule change and perhaps school counselling should come first.
- **The student is already over-scheduled.** Adding a weekly session to a week already full of sports, language classes and CAS can reduce learning. See the guide to [weekend IB tutoring in Gurgaon](/blog/weekend-ib-tutoring-gurgaon/) if the only real slot is Saturday or Sunday.
- **The student is meeting the goals the family set.** If the target is a 5 and they are producing 5s with room to spare, nothing needs fixing.

### The cost of waiting too long

There is a real asymmetry here. Starting too early wastes a few sessions and some money. Starting too late costs marks that cannot be recovered, because the course has moved on. When you are unsure, the cheaper mistake is an early diagnostic session rather than waiting a term.

## What a tutor can and cannot change, whenever you start

Setting expectations honestly is part of deciding when to start IB tutoring. The table below is a rough guide, not a promise.

| Stage | A tutor can usually change | A tutor cannot change |
|---|---|---|
| MYP 3-4 | Working habits, algebra fluency, criterion C writing | The student's interest in the subject |
| MYP 5 | Final-year technique, DP readiness, project planning conversations | Grades that are set by school-internal moderation |
| DP1 | Prerequisite gaps, routine, command-term fluency | The pace of the school syllabus |
| DP2 before mocks | Technique, paper practice, error patterns | Missing a year of content |
| DP2 after mocks | The highest-value few marks | Anything that needs months |
| IA and EE | Skills: structuring, questioning, reading criteria | The student's own authorship; no tutor may write or edit the submission |

That last row deserves emphasis. The IB's academic integrity rules allow a teacher to comment on one draft of an IA and do not allow anyone, tutor or parent, to write or rewrite the student's work. A good tutor teaches the skill and asks questions; the student writes the words. If a tutor offers more than that, treat it as a warning, not a bonus. More on the IA process is in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

## Subject-specific timing: where the clock runs fastest

Not every subject has the same clock. The ones that need earlier attention are those that build on themselves.

**Mathematics.** The most time-sensitive subject. Both AA and Applications and Interpretation (AI) rely on algebraic fluency developed over years. A student who is shaky in MYP 4 will find the DP1 first term hard. The guide on [choosing between IB Maths AA and AI](/blog/ib-maths-aa-vs-ai-which-one-should-you-choose/) matters here because the earlier a family decides on the right course, the better the preparation.

**Sciences.** Physics, Chemistry and Biology each accumulate content. Chemistry calculations and Physics problem solving reward practice that cannot be crammed. A weak DP1 term 1 in either is worth addressing early. The IA in the sciences is a scientific investigation worth 20%, marked on four criteria of 6 marks each, so the skills it needs, such as research design and data analysis, are built across both years.

**Languages and humanities.** These depend more on reading and writing volume. Starting early can mean building a habit of structured essay writing in MYP 4 and 5 rather than relying on content support. Timing is generally less sharp than in maths and sciences, though the IA, EE and the Individual Oral have their own deadlines.

**Economics and Business.** Rely on understanding and on practice with data-response and extended-response questions. Many students are new to the subject at DP1, so a short bridging block can help.

## The school calendar and when families feel the pressure

Indian IB schools generally follow an April-to-March year, with the DP culminating in May exams, which fits the way the IB session runs. Within that, pressure peaks cluster in predictable places. The details vary by school, so ask your coordinator for the assessment calendar and mark three kinds of date: submission deadlines for the IA and EE, dates for school-set mocks and unit tests, and the months in which the student is also dealing with university applications.

| Period | What typically happens | Tutoring implication |
|---|---|---|
| Start of the school year | New content, new teachers, new routines | Best time for bridging and gap repair |
| Mid-year | Unit tests; DP1 IA topics chosen | Weekly support in weakest subjects |
| Term before mocks | IA drafts due; revision begins | Highest demand on student time; protect schedule |
| Mock period | School-set mocks, usually several months before May | Diagnostic gold; plan targeted blocks |
| Final term | Study leave; May session | Rehearsal only; resist new content |
| Early July | May results released | Planning for retakes or university if needed |

For parents of DP2 students planning for university, the interplay between predicted grades and the application timeline is covered in the [IB university preparation guide](/blog/ib-university-preparation-guide/). The relevant point for timing is that predicted grades are often based on mock performance, so a strong mock can matter beyond the exam itself.

## How early is too early?

Parents of younger students, in the Primary Years Programme (PYP) or MYP 1 and 2, sometimes ask whether to begin tutoring in advance of the Diploma, so that the child is ahead. For most children the answer is no.

At these ages the main things that help later work are reading widely, comfortable number sense, curiosity and sleep. Early tutoring that pushes content ahead of the school can make class feel boring and can create a dependence on being told what to do. Where it does help is in specific cases: a child who is behind in reading, a student with an identified learning need who needs a different approach, or a family that has recently moved between curricula and needs a bridge.

The exception worth considering is mathematics in MYP 3 and 4. A student who is good at the subject and wants to be stretched may benefit from enrichment, but that is a different aim from fixing a problem and should be framed as such.

## A worked example: one family, three timing choices

Consider a student, call her Aanya, who joins DP1 at a Gurgaon school after finishing the IGCSE. She is bright, did well in Extended Mathematics, and chose Mathematics AA HL because a degree in engineering is on the table. This is an illustration, not a real family.

**Scenario A: start in the first month.** Aanya's tutor spends two sessions on a diagnostic. It shows strong calculation skills but weak work with function notation and no habit of writing out justification. They spend six weeks on those two things alongside the school's first unit. By the first test she has a routine and a vocabulary for what the markscheme wants.

**Scenario B: wait for the first test.** Aanya scores lower than she expected, around the level of a 4 or 5. The family starts weekly tutoring in week ten. The first four sessions are used to find out what went wrong. By the time the tutor is repairing, the school is on its third unit. She catches up by December, but with more effort than Scenario A.

**Scenario C: wait until DP2.** Aanya has coasted on memorised methods for a year. At DP2 mocks she gets a 4, with significant gaps in calculus and complex numbers. A tutor can improve technique, and she may improve, but the time left is short and the repair is expensive in effort.

The point is not that Scenario A is always right. If Aanya had arrived with a strong grasp of function notation and a habit of showing working, the family would have been right to wait. The right moment depends on the evidence, which is why the diagnostic comes first. How personalised planning works in practice is covered in [personalised home tuition for IB students](/blog/personalized-home-tuition-ib-students/).

## Starting small: the trial block approach

If you are unsure, a short, defined start is better than an open-ended weekly commitment. A trial block of four to six sessions with an agreed aim, for example "improve Criterion C working in algebra" or "build a paper-practice routine for Physics Paper 2", lets you judge value without long commitment.

At the end of the block, ask three things. Has the specific problem moved in the student's own work? Can the student now do something without the tutor that they could not before? Does the student feel the sessions are useful? If the answers are yes, continue; if not, change the focus or the tutor. IB Gram can arrange a demo or trial session before a family commits, and a different match can be requested if the first one is not right, subject to tutor availability.

The best way to judge a trial is to look at the work, not the mood. A session that felt good but left the student's next test paper unchanged did not do its job. The wider question of how to evaluate fit is covered in [how to choose an IB tutor in Gurgaon](/blog/how-to-choose-ib-tutor-gurgaon/).

## Home, online or hybrid: does mode affect timing?

Mode does not change when to start IB tutoring, but it can change how quickly you can. Online sessions can begin within days and fit around a packed week, which suits a student who needs help before a test in ten days. Home sessions suit younger students and subjects where working on paper side by side matters, and they work best when the student has a quiet space and the commute for the tutor is reasonable at the hours you need. Many families settle on a mix across the year, with home sessions in the quieter months and online sessions in exam season.

If the choice of mode is part of what is holding you back, the guides to [online IB tutoring in India](/blog/online-ib-tutoring-india/) and [IB home tutor vs online tutor in Gurgaon](/blog/ib-home-tutor-vs-online-tutor-gurgaon/) cover that decision directly so it does not need to delay the start.

## Budgeting for the timing you choose

Deciding when to start IB tutoring changes cost in a simple way. An early, short block costs less in total than a long rescue. A DP2 push compressed into a few months means more sessions per week and therefore a higher monthly spend, even if the total is similar. Fees depend on programme, level, subject, the tutor's experience and mode; the drivers are laid out in [what affects IB tutor fees in Gurgaon](/blog/ib-tutor-fees-in-gurgaon-what-affects-the-cost/), and the guide to [budgeting for private IB tutoring over two DP years](/blog/private-ib-tutor-cost-in-gurgaon-full-fee-breakdown/) shows how hours are typically distributed so you can plan rather than react.

A useful rule is to plan the hours first, then the budget. Decide how many weeks and how many sessions the problem needs, then see what that costs, rather than starting from a monthly figure and trying to fit a problem into it.

## Common timing mistakes Gurgaon families make

**Waiting for the school report.** Reports arrive at the end of a term. By then a problem has had ten to twelve weeks to settle.

**Starting in every subject at once.** A student with six subjects, three at HL, cannot absorb six tutors. Pick the subject with the biggest gap, usually an HL subject, and start there.

**Treating DP2 as the time to begin.** Because the exams are in DP2, families focus there, but the course is a two-year build.

**Confusing busyness with progress.** A student attending three classes a week and doing hours of practice, but not reviewing errors, is working hard on the wrong thing. The error log is the cheapest intervention available.

**Switching tutors too fast, or too slowly.** A tutor needs four to six sessions to show results on a defined aim. A student who has had no change after six sessions should be reviewed, not left for a term.

**Ignoring the student's voice.** The student's view of what is stuck is often the most accurate diagnostic information. A tutor imposed on a reluctant student is a slow start. Ask them what they would want help with, and listen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it too late to start IB tutoring in DP2?

No, but the returns depend on the month. Before mocks, a tutor can still repair a gap and build technique. After mocks, tutoring is narrower and aims at the few highest-value marks. In the final weeks the realistic goal is rehearsal and error control, not new content. If the student is in DP2 and unsure, a single diagnostic session usually shows whether the remaining time is enough for the worry in question.

### Should I start tutoring in MYP so my child is ready for the Diploma?

Only if there is a specific gap or a clear reason, such as a switch from another board. For most MYP students, school work plus good habits is enough. The exception is mathematics, where a short block of support in MYP 4 or early MYP 5 can protect a student who wants Mathematics AA HL in the DP. Preparing in advance without a problem to solve often creates boredom more than readiness.

### How many weeks does a tutor need to show results?

Four to six sessions is a fair window for a defined aim. In that time you should see a change in the student's own work, such as cleaner working, fewer repeated errors or a better-structured response. A change in confidence alone is not enough. If after six sessions nothing in the student's marked work has shifted, ask the tutor to explain the plan and adjust it, or consider a different match.

### Can a tutor help with the IA and the Extended Essay?

A tutor can teach the skills, but cannot write or edit the submission. That includes how to read the criteria, how to structure a question, how to analyse data and how to plan time. The student must write every word, and the IB's academic integrity policy allows the teacher to comment on one draft only. A tutor who offers to write or rewrite parts of the work is putting the student at risk.

### My child says they do not need a tutor. What should I do?

Listen first, then test the claim against evidence. Ask what the student thinks is going well and what is stuck. If marks and teacher feedback agree with them, wait. If the marks suggest otherwise, a short trial block agreed as an experiment often lands better than a standing arrangement. A student who has a say in the focus of the sessions is far more likely to use them.

### Is it better to start in the first term or wait for the first test results?

For students who are new to the IB or moving from another board, the first term is better, because the first test result arrives after the habits have formed. For a student who has a strong record and no gaps, waiting for the first test is reasonable. The deciding factor is whether there is a known risk, such as a new curriculum or a weak prerequisite, not the calendar alone.

### How do I know if the problem is the subject or the student's routine?

Compare the student's performance when untimed and when timed, and with and without notes. If the student can do the work slowly but not under time, the problem is fluency and technique. If they cannot do it at all, it is understanding. If they can do it but do not complete it, it is routine. A tutor's diagnostic should be able to tell these apart in a session or two, and the answer changes both the timing and the kind of help.

### Does the mode of tutoring change when I should begin?

Not the decision, but the speed. Online sessions can start within days, which helps when a test is close. Home sessions suit students who benefit from working side by side on paper and families who prefer an in-person routine. Hybrid arrangements are common across a DP year. If mode is the thing delaying a decision, start with whichever mode can begin soonest and revisit it later.

### What if I start and it does not work out?

Change the plan rather than abandon the idea. Ask what the tutor identified, what the agreed aim was and what evidence exists that it has shifted. If the fit is wrong, ask for a different match. IB Gram can arrange a trial before you commit and can shortlist a different tutor if the first is not right, subject to availability. A first tutor that does not fit is a common experience, not a sign that tutoring cannot help.

### Is one session a week enough?

For most defined problems, yes. One session a week combined with the student's practice between sessions is usually more effective than two sessions with no independent work. Increase to two sessions only in specific windows, such as the weeks before mocks, and only if the student is completing the work set. Frequency matters less than whether the student does something with each session before the next.
