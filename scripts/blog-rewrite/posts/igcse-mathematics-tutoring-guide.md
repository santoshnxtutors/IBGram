Why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics is rarely a mystery once you read their scripts. In most cases the cause is one of eight fixable things: shaky algebra from earlier years, not reading the question properly, calculator habits that work against them, sitting the wrong tier, thin or unmarked practice, weak working and presentation, memorised methods with no understanding, and exam anxiety that eats the first twenty minutes. Intelligence is almost never the problem.

This guide is for parents and students in Gurgaon and across India who are dealing with an International General Certificate of Secondary Education (IGCSE) Mathematics student whose marks are stuck, falling or unpredictable. It covers Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1). It explains the eight root causes, gives you a 20-minute diagnostic you can run at home, offers a fix for each cause, and says honestly when a tutor will help and when one will not.

A quick orientation first. Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams) has a Core route and an Extended route. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Core tops out at grade C; Extended reaches A*. Pearson Edexcel 4MA1 is tiered Foundation or Higher, with two calculator papers per tier. Which one your child sits is the school's decision, and it matters more than most families realise, as the section on tiers explains.

## Why do students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics?

Students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics because the course asks for more than the Class 8 and 9 maths they have been doing. It expects fluent algebra, multi-step reasoning, careful reading and clear working across a very wide syllabus, all in timed papers. A student who copes in class can still lose a lot of marks to gaps that only appear under exam conditions.

Three structural features make it harder than it looks.

**The syllabus is wide and cumulative.** Number, algebra, coordinate geometry, geometry, mensuration, trigonometry, transformations and vectors, probability and statistics all feed one another. A weak spot in fractions in Year 8 turns up as a failure to simplify algebraic fractions in Year 10, and again as a wrong answer in a probability tree.

**Papers mix topics and difficulty.** There is no chapter label on an exam question. The student must recognise what a question is about, choose a method, and carry it out, usually in the first minute of reading.

**Marks are earned in steps.** A final answer can be worth one mark of four. A student who "does it in the head" and writes only the answer can lose most of the credit for a correct method.

### The student is rarely "bad at maths"

When a child says "I'm bad at maths", the useful response is: "Which kind of question, and what happened?" The honest answer usually names a specific moment: running out of time, getting a different answer to the one in the book, or not knowing how to begin. Those are specific problems with specific fixes. The sections below are organised around them.

## A 20-minute diagnostic you can run at home

To see why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics in your own house, look at the scripts, not the syllabus. Before choosing a fix, find the cause. This needs one recent paper or test, a pen in a different colour and a markscheme if available.

:::steps The 20-minute diagnostic
Collect | Last two papers or class tests, marked
Sort | Tag every lost mark by cause
Count | Which cause loses the most marks
Pick | Fix the biggest cause first
:::

Tag each lost mark with one letter:

- **A** for algebra or number skill (the method failed because the basic manipulation was wrong).
- **R** for reading (the student answered a different question to the one asked).
- **C** for calculator (wrong mode, rounding too early, or a keying slip).
- **W** for working (the idea was right but the steps were missing or unclear).
- **K** for knowledge (the student did not know the topic).
- **T** for time (the question was left blank or rushed).

Add the letters up. In a typical set of scripts from students who are struggling, the biggest column is rarely K. That one result changes the plan: if most marks are lost to R and W, extra content lessons will not help.

| Dominant tag | What it usually means | Where to go in this guide |
|---|---|---|
| A | Foundation gap | Algebra and number gaps |
| R | Reading and interpretation | Misreading questions |
| C | Calculator habits | The calculator trap |
| W | Presentation and method marks | Weak working |
| K | Content not learned | Memorised methods; tier mismatch |
| T | Pace and anxiety | Time and nerves |

## Root cause 1: algebra and number gaps from earlier years

Among the answers to why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics, the most common deep cause is fragile algebra. A student reaches Year 10 able to solve a basic equation but unable to handle negative numbers inside brackets, fractions in the denominator or rearranging a formula where the subject appears twice. These gaps are invisible in earlier years because the questions were gentle. IGCSE removes the gentleness.

### Signs to look for

- Sign errors when expanding brackets, especially with a minus in front.
- Cancelling terms that cannot be cancelled, such as the 2 in (x + 2)/2.
- Treating fractions as separate numbers instead of one quantity.
- Getting confused by powers, roots and negative indices.
- Stalling on percentage change and ratio questions that need a multiplier.

### The fix

Go back, but only as far as needed, and rebuild with fast, accurate drills rather than re-teaching. Ten minutes a day on a single skill (expanding, factorising, fraction operations, index laws) beats a weekend of catch-up. Mix in questions with a "trap" built in, such as a negative coefficient, so the student learns to check for it. The point is fluency: the student should not have to think about how to expand a bracket, so that thinking is free for the problem.

A student who scored well in earlier school tests but is slipping in 0580 usually has this problem, because earlier tests allowed weaker algebra to pass. The six-week and 12-week revision structures in the [IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/) include algebra blocks for exactly this reason.

## Root cause 2: misreading the question

Reading errors cost more marks than most students and parents believe. The student solves a perfectly good problem, but not the one that was set.

Common patterns:

- Giving an answer to the wrong accuracy (3 significant figures when 2 decimal places were asked).
- Missing the word "exact", so a decimal is given instead of a surd or fraction.
- Answering "find x" when the question wanted the length of a side that depends on x.
- Skipping a condition in the middle of a long question, such as "to the nearest hundred" or "in terms of π".
- Finding the area when asked for the perimeter.

### The fix: a five-second read-back habit

Teach the student to underline the question word and the form of the answer before solving, then to re-read the question after solving and ask: "Did I answer exactly this?" The habit takes five seconds and recovers marks that no amount of extra content can. In practice, it also helps to have the student rewrite the question in their own words in the margin on harder multi-step problems.

Command words in maths are fewer than in other subjects but matter: "show that" requires every step because the answer is given; "write down" means no working needed; "calculate" means show the method; "estimate" means round first. A student who treats them all as "solve" loses marks on each.

## Root cause 3: the calculator trap

IGCSE Mathematics has calculator and non-calculator papers, and students often fail the transition between them. The calculator is a tool that rewards habits, and bad habits are punished by it.

### What goes wrong

- Rounding in the middle of a calculation, so the final answer is off in the third significant figure.
- Wrong angle mode (degrees versus radians) in trigonometry.
- Typing a fraction or a power without brackets, so the order of operations is wrong.
- Relying on the calculator for simple arithmetic and then failing the non-calculator paper.
- Not knowing how to use the fraction, table or statistics functions that would save time.

### The fix

Use the full display value through the calculation and round only at the end. Practise typing expressions with brackets for numerators, denominators and powers. Keep one non-calculator session each week, even if the student has no non-calculator paper that week, because mental and written fluency decays fast. In Core, the non-calculator paper is Paper 1; in Extended, Paper 2. Because those papers carry real weight, a student who dropped marks there needs a separate practice stream for them.

Do check the calculator model the school permits and learn its functions properly: a student who can use the table function to solve a quadratic or the statistical mode for mean and standard deviation (where relevant to the syllabus) saves time on every paper.

## Root cause 4: the wrong tier

Tier mismatch is the least discussed and most costly cause. Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 offers Core (top grade C) and Extended (up to A*); Pearson Edexcel 4MA1 offers Foundation and Higher. Schools decide the entry, often based on a mock result, and sometimes the decision is made too early or too cautiously.

### When a student is placed too low

A student capable of a B or A in Extended, entered for Core, can never exceed grade C. If the student sits Core and gets full marks, the ceiling is still a C. For a student who wants to go on to IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches, A Level Mathematics or Cambridge International AS Level, that ceiling is a problem.

### When a student is placed too high

A student who is consistently scoring low marks in Extended may be better served by a successful Core result than by a poor Extended grade, especially if confidence is suffering and the Extended content is not secure. The Extended paper is not forgiving: much of its marks sit in questions that need confident algebra and multi-step reasoning.

### How to decide

Talk to the school with evidence, not feelings: mock results by paper, topic-by-topic marks, and the pattern of lost marks. Ask what the entry deadline is and whether a change is possible. Do not make the decision alone in a rush; the school confirms entries with the exam board, and the series matters (Cambridge runs February/March in India, May/June and October/November). A tutor can help you read the evidence but cannot make the tier decision for the school.

The wider picture of the two syllabuses and the Additional Mathematics decision is covered in the [IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

## Root cause 5: practice that is thin, unmarked or unbalanced

Many students practise a lot and improve little. Typical forms:

- Doing questions from one topic at a time and never meeting mixed sets.
- Marking by checking only the final answer against the key.
- Redoing questions they already know, because it feels good.
- Starting full papers too late, or never under timed conditions.
- Doing no practice on the non-calculator paper.

### The fix

Move from topic practice to mixed practice by the midpoint of the course, and mark every question against the markscheme, noting the exact mark lost. Use past papers properly, a topic covered in [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/). Keep an error log with a column for the fix and a date for retesting; a fix that has not been retested is a hope.

:::bars Illustrative priorities when marks are being lost
Fix reading and accuracy habits | 85 | Highest
Rebuild algebra fluency | 80 | High
Practise mixed and timed questions | 70 | High
Learn missing topics | 45 | Medium
Learn extra tricks | 10 | Low
This is a typical ordering for a student losing marks to habits, not research data. Your own diagnostic should decide the order.
:::

## Root cause 6: weak working and presentation

IGCSE mathematics is marked in method and accuracy steps. A question worth four marks may award one for setting up, one for a correct step, one for the substitution and one for the answer. A student who writes only the answer, or who writes numbers across the page with no structure, loses marks even when the final value is right, and cannot recover a method mark when the final value is wrong.

### What good working looks like

- One step per line, with the equals signs aligned.
- Formulas written before substitution.
- Units and the required accuracy stated in the final line.
- A diagram sketched, labelled and referred to, for geometry and trigonometry.
- For "show that" questions, every step and the stated final line.

### The fix

Rewrite one corrected solution a day in the full style of the markscheme. After a week, the habit transfers. A simple exercise is to take a solved question and ask the student to explain, in writing, what each line is for; that exposes steps they treated as invisible.

## Root cause 7: memorised methods with no understanding

Some students pass tests by memorising a procedure for each question type. IGCSE papers reward that up to a point and then punish it: a question presented in an unfamiliar way defeats a student who knows only the template.

### Signs

- The student can do the textbook example but not a variation.
- "I haven't seen this type" is a common comment after a paper.
- Formulas are quoted correctly and applied to the wrong situation.
- A change of context, such as a word problem about a tank instead of a bare equation, derails the student.

### The fix

Ask the student to explain *why* a method works, to draw a picture, or to solve the same problem two ways. Use questions that vary the setup: reverse a question (give the answer, ask for the missing value), change the context, remove a step. For geometry, make the student state the theorem before using it. Understanding does not need to be deep or philosophical; it needs to be enough to recognise when a method applies.

## Root cause 8: time pressure and exam nerves

Some students know the material but underperform on the day. Common patterns include spending too long on the first hard question, panicking when a question looks unfamiliar and not returning to skipped questions.

### The fix

Rehearse the exam. Practise full papers in timed conditions, with a rule for moving on after a set time per mark, and a routine for the first two minutes: scan the paper, start with a question that is sure marks, leave a hard one and return. Build a short list of calming moves for when the mind goes blank: write what you know, draw the diagram, try a simpler case. Sleep and regular meals matter more than any trick.

If anxiety is persistent and affects sleep or daily life for weeks, speak to the school counsellor or a doctor; this is a health matter, not a study-skills one.

## How to read the pattern across several papers

One paper is a snapshot. Three papers give a pattern. When you tag lost marks across three papers, look for three things.

| Pattern across papers | Likely meaning | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Same topic loses marks every time | Content or foundation gap | Teach that topic, then retest it |
| Different topics, same cause tag | A habit problem | Fix the habit, not the topics |
| Marks lost mostly in last third of paper | Time or stamina | Timed practice with a pacing plan |
| Calculator paper fine, non-calculator weak | Fluency and mental maths | Weekly non-calculator drills |
| Strong in class tests, weak in papers | Mixed practice or exam technique | Move to mixed and timed work |

A parent can usually run this table without any mathematical knowledge, simply by counting the tags. The result tells you whether a tutor is likely to help, and which skills to look for.

## What does a fix look like week by week?

Whatever the answer to why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics, a change that sticks takes about four to six weeks of consistent small steps, not one dramatic weekend. A realistic pattern for a student whose main causes are reading, working and algebra fluency looks like this.

:::timeline A four-week repair for habit-driven mark loss
Week 1 | Diagnostic, error log started, five-second read-back habit
Week 2 | Algebra drills daily, working rewritten to markscheme style
Week 3 | Mixed questions, first timed half paper
Week 4 | Full timed paper, error log reviewed, plan reset
:::

After four weeks, compare the tags on the latest paper with those from the first. If the largest column has shrunk, the plan is working. If it has not, change one thing, usually the quality of marking or the amount of practice, and review in two weeks.

### A worked example of tagging a single question

Consider a four-mark question: "Solve 3(2x - 5) = 4x + 7, then find the value of 2x - 1." A student writes 6x - 5 = 4x + 7, then 2x = 12, x = 6, and answers 11. They score 2 of 4. What went wrong? The expansion lost a sign: 3 times -5 is -15, not -5. That is an A tag (algebra). The student then answered 2x - 1 = 11, which is correct for x = 6, so the second part earns follow-through marks; had the markscheme not allowed that, the student would also have lost them. The fix is not a lesson on linear equations but a habit of multiplying every term inside a bracket, with a check by substituting back. One line of correction, one mark pattern identified.

## When does a tutor help, and when does one not?

A tutor helps when there is a specific, named cause and the family lacks the time or confidence to fix it, when the student cannot mark their own work honestly, or when the tier or syllabus decision needs an outside view. A tutor does not help if the real problem is volume (too many commitments), if the student will not attempt work between sessions, or if the only goal is a guaranteed grade.

### What a good tutor does in the first month

1. Diagnoses from real scripts, not from a chat.
2. Names two or three targets, not "everything".
3. Gives short, regular practice between sessions.
4. Logs errors and retests them.
5. Reports what changed, in specifics.

If a tutor does not do these, change the approach. For a fair comparison between a tutor and a class, see [IGCSE maths home tutor vs coaching](/blog/igcse-maths-home-tutor-vs-coaching/); for formats, [online vs home tutor for IGCSE maths](/blog/online-vs-home-tutor-for-igcse-maths/).

### What can be done at home without a tutor

Much of what is above can be run by a family: the diagnostic, the error log, the five-second read-back habit, weekly non-calculator work and timed papers. The method is described in [personalised learning in IGCSE](/blog/personalized-learning-igcse-education/). Outside help adds value when the diagnosis is unclear, when motivation or the relationship at home makes parent-led work hard, or when subject depth is needed.

## Special situations

### The student moving from CBSE, ICSE or a state board

A student who switches into IGCSE often finds that questions have less predictable formats, that marks reward method more explicitly and that formula sheets work differently. Gaps in topics not covered in the previous board may exist; the diagnostic will show them. Give it a term before judging.

### The student heading for IB Diploma maths

A student who plans to take IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches usually needs Extended 0580 and often Additional Mathematics 0606. If the foundation is shaky, IB DP maths will expose it within weeks. The [IGCSE to IB DP maths transition](/blog/from-igcse-mathematics-to-ib-dp-building-a-strong-foundation/) guide shows what to repair before the jump.

### The student with a gap year in practice

A child returning after illness or a school change may have missed a block of content. List the topics missed, rank by weight and connection, and teach the high-connection ones first (algebra, fractions, ratio, basic geometry) because they support others.

### The student who is strong in maths but weak in English

Command words and word problems can sink a mathematically able student whose first language is not English. Build a short glossary of the maths-specific phrases ("prove", "show that", "in terms of", "hence") and practise translating a word problem into an equation before solving.

## What parents can do this week

Knowing why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics is only useful if something changes at home.

You do not need to teach the content. You need to create conditions in which the student can find and fix the causes.

- Ask for the last two marked papers, and sit with your child to run the 20-minute diagnostic.
- Agree on one fix and a four-week review date.
- Protect a regular practice slot, short and consistent, over a long weekend session.
- Avoid comparisons with others; they distract from the evidence.
- Ask the school about the tier entry deadline early.

Do not judge progress by a single paper. Look at whether the largest cause column is shrinking, whether the student is finishing papers and whether they can explain their working.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my child doing well in class but poorly in IGCSE maths papers?

Class tests often check one topic at a time with familiar formats, while IGCSE papers mix topics, add time pressure and award marks for method. Run the diagnostic on the last two papers: usually the lost marks are reading, working, calculator or time, not content. Moving to mixed, timed practice with strict marking usually closes the gap.

### Is IGCSE maths harder than CBSE or ICSE maths?

It is different more than harder. IGCSE rewards method marks, unfamiliar question styles and tiered papers, while some boards reward known question types. Students who rely on memorised templates often find the change difficult. Many students adapt within a term if they practise with past-style papers and markschemes. Check the school's choice of Cambridge or Pearson Edexcel, since formats differ.

### Should my child sit Core or Extended?

Sit Extended if the student can realistically reach B or above and plans to take maths further, such as IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches or A Level. Core caps at grade C. The school makes the entry decision, usually from mock results, so bring evidence and ask about the deadline early. A tutor can help interpret results, but cannot change the entry.

### How long does it take to improve IGCSE maths marks?

Habit-driven loss, such as reading, working and calculator errors, can improve within four to six weeks of consistent practice. Foundation gaps in algebra take longer, often a term. No one can guarantee a grade, and progress depends on practice between sessions. Review the error log every two weeks to see whether the largest cause is shrinking.

### My child makes silly mistakes. Is that carelessness?

Usually not. Repeated slips are habits that can be trained out: sign errors, unchecked units, rounding too early. Tag the mistakes, find the pattern and build a specific check for each, such as substituting back to test an equation. Calling it carelessness gives no way to fix it; naming the habit does.

### Does my child need a tutor, or can we fix this at home?

Many causes can be tackled at home with the diagnostic, an error log and timed practice. A tutor adds value when the diagnosis is unclear, when subject depth is needed, when the student cannot mark honestly or when homework conflicts make parent-led work hard. Ask for a trial session, and judge by whether the error log shortens.

### How much practice per day is reasonable?

Thirty to sixty focused minutes a day, five or six days a week, is enough for most students outside the exam run-up, with longer weekend blocks for timed papers. Quality matters more than quantity: marked, corrected and retested work beats three hours of unchecked questions. Reduce volume if the student is exhausted, since sleep affects accuracy.

### Is Additional Mathematics necessary?

Not for every student. Cambridge IGCSE Additional Mathematics 0606 is usually taken alongside 0580 Extended by students heading for IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches HL or A Level Mathematics. If the core course is already a struggle, adding 0606 can overload the student. Discuss with the school and weigh the future course requirements carefully.

### My child is anxious before maths exams. What helps?

Rehearsal helps most: timed papers in exam conditions, a fixed routine for the first two minutes and a plan for blanking, such as writing what is known and sketching a diagram. Good sleep and regular meals matter. If anxiety affects sleep or daily life for weeks, speak to the school counsellor or a doctor.

### Will a tutor promise me a better grade?

No. A tutor can diagnose, teach, keep the student accountable and train exam habits, but the student sits the exam and must practise between sessions. Be cautious of any promise of a particular grade. Judge a tutor by their method, the error log and what changes in the first four weeks, not by outcomes promised in advance.
