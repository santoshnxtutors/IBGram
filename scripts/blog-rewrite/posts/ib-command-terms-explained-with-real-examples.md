IB command terms are the verbs at the start of a question (define, describe, explain, analyse, evaluate, discuss, justify and about forty more) and each one tells you the depth and shape of answer the examiner will mark. Get the verb wrong and you lose marks even when every fact on the page is correct. A student who "explains" when asked to "evaluate" has written a good paragraph that earns a fraction of the marks available.

This guide is for International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) students and their parents, and also for Middle Years Programme (MYP) students who will meet the same vocabulary in MYP 5. It covers what the main terms mean, how they connect to mark allocation, worked answers in the sciences, economics, history, English and mathematics, the confusions that cost the most marks, and a practice routine you can run with past papers. It does not repeat the exam calendar or revision timetable; those live in the [IB exam preparation hub](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/).

A caution before the detail. Each subject guide carries its own glossary of command terms, and the wording differs slightly between subjects. What follows is a practical translation, not a replacement. Treat the glossary in your own current subject guide as the authority, and ask your teacher or DP coordinator when two sources disagree.

## What Are IB Command Terms and Why Do They Decide Marks?

IB command terms are defined words that tell a candidate what kind of response a question wants. The International Baccalaureate publishes them in each subject guide, and examiners are trained to mark against them. "Define" wants a precise meaning. "Explain" wants reasons or causes. "Evaluate" wants a weighed judgement. The term sets the ceiling on what an answer can earn.

The reason this matters so much is that IB markschemes are built from points, not impressions. A 4-mark "explain" question usually has more acceptable points than marks, and each creditable point is a separate decision by the examiner. A 4-mark "state" question can never earn 4 marks from a paragraph, because there is nothing to explain. The command term decides which kind of point the markscheme is looking for.

3 | Broad objective levels they sit in
40+ | Terms across the DP glossaries
1 | Verb that sets the depth of every answer
Subject glossaries differ slightly; use the one in your current subject guide.
:::

### The three levels of demand

Most DP subject guides group command terms under assessment objectives. The grouping is rough but useful. Objective 1 terms ask you to show knowledge: define, state, list, label, draw, write down. Objective 2 terms ask you to apply knowledge: describe, outline, calculate, identify, distinguish, apply. Objective 3 terms ask you to think with knowledge: explain, analyse, compare, discuss, evaluate, justify, predict, suggest, to what extent. The exact placement of a term varies by subject, and a few appear at different levels in different guides.

A rule of thumb follows. Questions worth 1 or 2 marks tend to carry first-level verbs. Questions worth 6 marks and above tend to carry third-level verbs. If you see "state" next to 6 marks, re-read the question, because something is unusual. If you see "evaluate" next to 2 marks, you are probably in a subject where the term is used in a compressed way, and the markscheme will want one strength and one limitation at most.

### Why content knowledge is not enough

Consider two students who both know that enzymes lower activation energy. Asked to "state one function of an enzyme," the first writes a clean line and earns the mark. The second writes six lines, and in the fourth line says that enzymes are used up in the reaction, which is wrong. The extra writing cost nothing in time that mattered but may have cost the mark, because examiners do not credit a correct statement that is contradicted elsewhere in the same answer.

The commonest error is not ignorance. It is a mismatch between what the student knows and what the question asked for. The fix is mechanical, and it is trainable.

## The Command Terms That Ask for Knowledge: Define, State, List, Label

These terms reward precision and brevity. They carry few marks each, and the fastest way to lose them is to write more than the question asked.

**Define** means give the precise meaning of a term. The answer is one sentence with the defining features and nothing else. Example: "Define opportunity cost" in Economics. A mark-earning answer is "the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made." Adding an example is allowed but earns nothing on a 1-mark question. Where a term has a standard definition in the subject guide, examiners look for its key words, so paraphrasing too freely can drop a necessary element.

**State** means give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation. Example: "State one factor that affects the rate of a chemical reaction." Writing "temperature" earns the mark. Writing "temperature, because particles move faster and collide more often" also earns it, but in a longer question that habit leaks time. Writing "temperature and pressure" when one was asked for is the dangerous version: if one of the two is wrong for the reaction in question, you may lose the mark despite the right first answer.

**List** means give a sequence of brief answers with no explanation. The number asked for is the number marked, and extra items beyond that are usually ignored, though a wrong item among them can still hurt.

**Label** and **write down** work the same way: no reasoning is wanted, only the correct item or value. In maths, "write down" often signals that the answer can be read from something already given, such as a value from an equation in a form, and that heavy working is not expected.

### What a one-mark answer looks like

| Question | Weak answer | Full-mark answer |
|---|---|---|
| Define "inflation" | "Prices go up and people have less money" | "A sustained increase in the general price level over time" |
| State a unit of power | "Joules per second, which is watts, and it measures energy transfer" | "Watt" |
| List two products of anaerobic respiration in yeast | "Alcohol and gas" | "Ethanol and carbon dioxide" |
| Label the structure that controls entry to the nucleus | "The edge bit" | "Nuclear pore" |

The pattern is that the full-mark answers are not longer. They use the correct technical word. Vocabulary is the content at this level.

## The Command Terms That Ask for Detail: Describe, Outline, Identify, Distinguish

These sit in the middle. They want accurate detail about what something is or what is happening, and they do not want causes. Students who are strong on understanding often lose marks here by explaining too early.

**Describe** means give a detailed account. For a graph, it asks for the shape, the key values and any change of rate or turning point. For a process, it asks for the sequence. Example: "Describe the trend shown in the graph." "The value increases" is not a description. A mark-earning answer reads something like: "The concentration rises slowly from 0 to 20 minutes, then increases more steeply between 20 and 40 minutes, and levels off at about 80 units after 50 minutes." It uses numbers from the axes, names the stages and mentions the plateau. It does not say why the plateau happens, because that would be "explain."

**Outline** means give a brief account or summary. It sits between a list and a full description: more developed than a sequence of words, shorter than an account with every detail. Example: "Outline two effects of inflation on consumers." Two sentences, each stating an effect and attaching a short consequence, is the right size.

**Identify** means find an answer from several possibilities, usually by naming it. **Distinguish** means make clear the differences between two or more items, and it often appears with a pair of related ideas, such as a hormone and its target or a nominal and a real variable.

### Describe versus explain in practice

A Geography or Environmental Systems student shown a graph of rainfall and river discharge might be asked either of these.

- "Describe the relationship between rainfall and discharge." The answer reports the pattern: discharge peaks about two hours after the rainfall peak, and the rise is steeper than the fall.
- "Explain the relationship between rainfall and discharge." The answer must now give reasons: surface runoff takes time to reach the channel, saturated soil reduces infiltration, and so on.

Same graph, two different answers, two different marks available. If you write the explanation under "describe," you spend time and gain nothing. If you write only the description under "explain," you lose most of the marks.

## What Do "Explain" and "Analyse" Ask For, and How Do They Differ?

"Explain" asks for a detailed account including reasons or causes: how or why something happens. "Analyse" asks you to break information down into parts to bring out the essential elements or structure, and to show how those parts relate. An explanation can be a single chain of cause and effect. An analysis should show more than one part and say how they connect, interact or matter.

That difference decides how long and how structured an answer should be.

### Explain: the single chain

Physics example: "Explain why increasing the resistance in a circuit reduces the current when the potential difference is constant."

A mark-earning answer links the quantities in order: current is the rate of flow of charge; with the potential difference fixed, a larger resistance means less charge is pushed through per second, so by I = V/R the current falls. The marks are for the links, not for the formula alone. A student who writes only "because V = IR" has stated a relationship, not given reasons.

Biology example: "Explain how enzymes affect the rate of reactions." The answer needs a mechanism: the substrate binds at the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, so a larger proportion of collisions lead to product, which raises the rate until the enzyme is saturated. "An enzyme is a biological catalyst" is a definition. It earns at most the opening mark.

### Analyse: the parts and their relationships

Economics example: "Analyse the effect of an increase in interest rates on consumer spending."

An analysis here breaks the effect into channels, because there is more than one route. Higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, which discourages credit-financed purchases. They raise the return on saving, which makes saving more attractive than spending now. For households with variable-rate mortgages, disposable income falls. Each channel is a link, and the analysis shows how the channels combine to reduce consumption and, through that, aggregate demand. The strongest versions also note what shapes the size of the effect, such as how many households carry floating-rate debt.

### A side-by-side comparison

| Feature | Explain | Analyse |
|---|---|---|
| Core question | How or why does this happen? | What are the parts, and how do they relate? |
| Typical shape | One clear chain, link by link | Several elements, plus the relationships between them |
| Judgement needed | None | Usually some sense of significance |
| Typical failure | Stating a fact or formula with no reasons | Describing each part with no connections |
| Useful scaffold | Because... which leads to... so... | Part one... part two... these interact by... |

One useful test: if you could delete any sentence from your answer without breaking the logic of the others, you are probably describing, not analysing. In an analysis, the sentences lean on each other.

:::bars Illustrative depth of response by command term
Define, state, list | 15 | Shortest
Describe, outline | 35 | Detail, no reasons
Explain | 55 | Reasons and causes
Analyse, compare | 70 | Parts and relationships
Discuss, evaluate, to what extent | 95 | Judgement
A teaching illustration of relative depth, not an official scale.
:::

## What Do "Compare", "Contrast" and "Compare and Contrast" Ask For?

"Compare" asks for an account of similarities and differences between two or more items, referring to both throughout. "Contrast" asks for differences only. "Compare and contrast" is the explicit request for both. The words "referring to both throughout" are the part students miss: writing a paragraph on item A and then a paragraph on item B is two descriptions, not a comparison.

Biology example: "Compare mitosis and meiosis." A weak answer describes mitosis for six lines and meiosis for six lines. A strong answer is built around points of comparison: number of divisions, number of daughter cells, chromosome number in the daughters, genetic variation, and where each occurs. For each point it states both sides. "Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid cells, whereas meiosis produces four genetically varied haploid cells" earns more than the two separate paragraphs put together, because the comparison is explicit.

History example: "Compare and contrast the methods used by two authoritarian leaders to consolidate power." A strong plan uses two or three themes, for instance control of the army, use of propaganda, and suppression of opposition, and for each theme weaves in evidence from both leaders before saying what the difference tells you.

### A table is not always allowed, but the logic is

Some subjects accept tables for comparison questions; many do not at higher mark levels. Check your subject's guidance with your teacher. Even when prose is required, mentally build the table first. Rows are the points of comparison, columns are the items. Then write one sentence per row that mentions both. Words that carry the comparison are "whereas", "both", "similarly", "in contrast", "while". If your answer has none of them, you probably described two things.

## What Do "Discuss", "Evaluate" and "To What Extent" Ask For?

"Discuss" asks for a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses, supported by evidence. "Evaluate" asks you to make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations. "To what extent" asks you to consider the merits or otherwise of an argument or concept, which in practice means a judgement about degree. All three are top-tier terms and tend to sit on the largest questions, and all three punish a one-sided answer.

### Discuss versus evaluate

The old post on this topic offered a neat pair of prompts, and they are worth keeping. For "discuss," ask: what are the different sides? For "evaluate," ask: how good, effective or valid is it? The difference is the destination. A discussion can end with the arguments laid out and a reasoned position. An evaluation must end with a judgement about worth, and the body must have weighed the strengths against the limitations to reach it.

In practice, subjects differ on how strictly the conclusion is demanded. Economics and History markschemes usually reward a clear, supported judgement at the end of "evaluate" and "to what extent" answers. Some science markschemes use "evaluate" for a short appraisal of a method or a claim, where one strength and one limitation earn the marks. Read the mark total, then read the verb.

### A worked evaluation: Economics

Question: "Evaluate the effectiveness of a government policy designed to reduce inflation." (A 15-mark style question, illustrative.)

A strong answer is built in blocks. First, say what the policy does and how it should work: for example, a rise in the policy interest rate that lowers borrowing and spending. Second, give the strengths with reasoning: it can reduce demand-pull pressure, and it signals commitment to price stability. Third, give the limitations: effects arrive with a lag, they do little against cost-push inflation caused by import prices, and they can raise unemployment and slow growth. Fourth, name the conditions that decide how well it works: the cause of the inflation, how indebted households are, and the state of the exchange rate. Finally, give a judgement: "The policy is likely to be effective when inflation is driven by excess demand, and much less so when it is driven by supply shocks, so overall its effectiveness is conditional."

The last sentence is the evaluation. It is short, it uses the earlier material, and it commits. A conclusion that merely says "therefore it is effective" with no reasoning is the pattern markers see most often and credit least.

### A worked "to what extent": History

Question: "To what extent was economic instability responsible for the collapse of a democratic government?"

The structure is a weighing. Build the case for economic instability first: unemployment, loss of confidence, extremist parties gaining from hardship. Then build the case for other factors: weak institutions, political deadlock, the actions of key individuals, the role of elites. Then compare their relative importance and state the extent: "Economic instability was a necessary condition but not a sufficient one; it opened space that political choices then filled." An answer that says only "yes, to a large extent" without ranking the factors has not done the work the phrase asks for.

### Judgement words that help

Weak: "This shows it is good." Better: "This suggests the policy works in the short term but its benefits are limited by..." The second form uses hedged, specific language that points to evidence. Examiners reward judgements that are proportionate to the evidence given.

## What Does "Justify" Mean, and How Is It Different From "Explain"?

"Justify" means give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion. The answer must defend a choice that has already been made or stated. "Explain" says why something happens. "Justify" says why a decision or claim is reasonable.

Chemistry example: "Justify the choice of titration rather than gravimetric analysis to find the concentration of the acid." A mark-earning answer names a feature of the situation and links it to the method: titration is rapid, uses small volumes, has a clear endpoint with a suitable indicator for a strong acid and strong base, and does not require the lengthy drying to constant mass that gravimetric methods need. Each reason points back to the choice. An answer that describes how titration works has described, not justified.

Internal assessment (IA) is a place where justification appears without the command word. The methodology section of a science investigation is, in effect, a long "justify": why this range of the independent variable, why this number of repeats, why this control. Students who have practised justification in exam answers tend to write tighter methodology sections. The rules and process for IAs are covered in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/), and subject-by-subject examples are in the [IB IA examples guide](/blog/complete-guide-to-ib-internal-assessment-ia-with-examples/).

### The two-part pattern

A reliable pattern for "justify" is claim, reason, evidence. State the choice. Give the reason that makes it sensible. Support the reason with something from the data, the context or the theory. If the question gives a context, the evidence should come from it. A justification that could be pasted into any context unchanged is probably too generic to earn full marks.

## What Do the Calculation and Reasoning Terms Mean: Calculate, Determine, Deduce, Show That, Hence?

In sciences and mathematics, a family of terms controls how much working you must show and what you may assume. They look similar and are routinely confused.

**Calculate** means obtain a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working. Show the substitution, the arithmetic and the final value with its unit. Method marks and the answer mark are separate, so a correct formula with a slip in the last step usually keeps most of the marks only if the working is visible.

**Determine** means obtain the only possible answer. It signals that the result follows from given information or from a method you must choose, and in mathematics it often accepts working carried out with a graphic display calculator where the paper permits one. Check whether the paper is calculator or non-calculator before deciding how to present it.

**Deduce** means reach a conclusion from the information given. It differs from "calculate" in that the conclusion comes from reasoning with what is on the page, often with little or no arithmetic. Example: "Deduce the relationship between temperature and reaction rate from the data." Use the numbers in the table, say what changes with what, and stop. Do not import theory the data does not support unless the question also asks you to explain.

**Show that** means obtain the required result using the given information, and the result is printed in the question. Because the answer is given, the marks are for the route. Every step must be visible, and a jump to the printed result is the classic way to lose marks. **Hence** means use the result of the previous part. A student who solves a "hence" question by starting from scratch may reach the right answer by a longer route and lose the method marks the intended route offered, and sometimes the mark scheme requires the earlier result to be used. **Hence or otherwise** gives you the choice.

### Calculation terms side by side

| Term | What you must show | Typical slip |
|---|---|---|
| Calculate | Formula, substitution, answer with unit | Correct number, no unit |
| Determine | The only answer that fits, with justification of method | Guessing a value from a graph without stating how |
| Deduce | A conclusion drawn from the given evidence | Bringing in outside theory instead |
| Show that | Every step to the printed result | Skipping the middle line |
| Hence | Use of the previous result | Restarting from scratch |
| Write down | The answer, little or no working | Spending minutes on a one-mark item |

Several of these terms appear in the maths-specific guidance for the [IB Maths AA HL scoring guide](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/), where marks are explicitly split into method and accuracy marks. The same habit of showing the route transfers to physics and chemistry calculations.

## What Do "Predict", "Suggest", "Estimate" and "Sketch" Ask For?

These are the terms that reward sensible reasoning from limited information, and they cause anxiety because there seems to be no single right answer.

**Predict** means give an expected result. The answer states an outcome, and a good answer adds a brief reason when the marks allow. "The rate will increase because higher temperature increases the proportion of collisions with energy above the activation energy." If the question carries one mark, the outcome alone is enough.

**Suggest** means propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer. It tells you there is more than one acceptable response and that the marker will credit any sound one. Example: "Suggest one improvement to the experimental method." A good answer is specific and says what it improves: "Use a data logger rather than a stopwatch to record temperature every ten seconds, which reduces human reaction-time error in the readings." "Be more careful" earns nothing.

**Estimate** means obtain an approximate value. You are allowed to round, use a simplified model or read from a graph. The answer should say what was assumed.

**Sketch** means represent by means of a graph or diagram, labelled and with key features correct. In mathematics it asks for the general shape and the key features, such as intercepts and asymptotes, but not plotted points on exact scales. In sciences it asks for the shape of the relationship with labelled axes. A sketch with unlabelled axes loses marks that the shape alone did not earn.

### A short worked example: suggesting an improvement

An ecology practical measured the effect of light on the number of leaves in a quadrat, and the question reads: "Suggest one limitation of this method and one way to reduce its effect." A mark-earning answer has two clear parts. Limitation: only a small number of quadrats were sampled, so the mean may not represent the whole area. Improvement: increase the number of quadrats and place them using random coordinates, which reduces sampling bias. The structure mirrors the question: one limitation, one improvement, each specific.

## How Do IB Command Terms Map to Mark Allocation?

Command terms and marks work together, and reading both is the single most useful habit in the exam. As a working rule, each mark in a typical IB question corresponds to one creditable point, and the command term tells you what kind of point counts.

For a 2-mark "describe," look for two distinct features. For a 4-mark "explain," look for two or three links in a chain, each with a reason. For a 6-mark "analyse," look for several elements and a statement of how they connect. For a 15-mark "evaluate," look for developed arguments on both sides plus a reasoned judgement. In Language and Literature and in humanities papers, many large questions are marked against level descriptors or criteria rather than counted points, so the verb tells you which criterion is being rewarded: knowledge, analysis, evaluation, structure, or use of evidence.

:::steps A seven-second check before you write
Circle | The command term and the mark total
Translate | What kind of points will count here
Count | Roughly one creditable point per mark
Plan | Two lines of structure, then start
:::

### The marks-per-minute check

A simple pacing logic follows from the verb. Most papers give a little over a minute per mark. A 1-mark "state" should take well under a minute. A 6-mark "explain" deserves about six or seven minutes. If you notice yourself writing for ten minutes on a 4-mark "describe," the likeliest explanation is that you have drifted into "explain." The overall time structure of the May session, and how to rehearse it, is set out in the [IB exam strategy for the last 30 days](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/).

### A mark-allocation table

| Marks | Typical verb | What the examiner looks for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define, state, identify | One precise word, value or phrase |
| 2 | Outline, describe, distinguish | Two distinct, accurate points |
| 3-4 | Explain, calculate, compare | A reasoned chain or a worked method, with a result |
| 6 | Analyse, discuss, suggest | Several developed points and links between them |
| 8-15 | Evaluate, to what extent, discuss | Balance, evidence, structure and a supported judgement |

These are typical patterns. Individual papers vary, so always read the markscheme for the year you are practising. Official markschemes and subject reports come through your school or the IB's own channels, and they show what was actually credited.

## Command Terms Across Subjects: The Same Verb, a Different Shape

The same verb can ask for a different shape of answer depending on the subject. This is the area where a student moving from sciences to humanities, or an MYP student moving into DP, is most likely to be caught.

| Subject | What "explain" or "analyse" usually looks like | What "evaluate" usually looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Biology, Chemistry, Physics | A mechanism or relationship with named processes and quantities | A short appraisal of a method, source or claim: strengths and limitations |
| Economics | A chain of cause and effect with a diagram where useful, applied to the context | Balanced arguments, stakeholders, short and long run, then a judgement |
| History | Causes and consequences supported by specific evidence | Weighing of historians' views or factors, with a ranked conclusion |
| Language and Literature | Effects of language choices linked to meaning | Judgement on how effectively a writer achieves a purpose |
| Mathematics | A reasoned argument or proof with logical connectives | Rare; typically a comment on a model's validity |

A few specifics are worth knowing. In Economics, diagrams are part of the answer for many "explain" and "analyse" questions, and unlabelled diagrams earn little. In History, "evaluate" often attaches to a source, and the criteria are origin, purpose, value and limitation. In literature papers the command words matter less than the guiding question, so read for what the passage is doing and write about the writer's choices.

### Moving from MYP to DP

MYP students meet a lighter version of the same vocabulary, and the criteria language (for example, "justify" in Criterion D of Mathematics, where students are asked to justify the degree of accuracy of a result and whether it makes sense in context) trains the habit early. The step up in DP is that the marks are tied to the verb more tightly and the answers are longer. A student who is comfortable with "describe" and "explain" in MYP often needs deliberate practice on "evaluate" and "to what extent." The way the MYP criteria feed into DP maths is in the [IGCSE to IB DP maths transition guide](/blog/from-igcse-mathematics-to-ib-dp-building-a-strong-foundation/).

## The Command-Term Mistakes That Cost the Most Marks

After reading many practice scripts, the same few errors account for most of the lost marks. They are all fixable in a fortnight of deliberate practice.

1. **Over-answering low-level terms.** A "state" answer that grows into a paragraph risks contradiction and wastes time.
2. **Explaining when asked to describe.** The reasons earn nothing and displace the detail that would.
3. **Describing when asked to explain.** The commonest loss in sciences. The student reports the pattern correctly and never says why.
4. **Two separate descriptions for "compare."** There is no comparison until both items appear in the same sentence.
5. **No judgement in "evaluate."** The answer lists strengths and weaknesses and stops. The examiner has nothing to credit under judgement.
6. **A one-sided "discuss."** Even when the student holds a strong view, the marks sit with the range of arguments.
7. **Unsupported conclusions.** "Therefore it is effective" with no weighing. Evidence and reasoning must lead to the verdict.
8. **Ignoring the context.** "Analyse the effect on Indian consumers" is not the same as the generic textbook question.
9. **Missing units, labels and notation.** Calculation and sketch marks depend on these.
10. **Answering a different question.** A topic that looks familiar triggers a memorised answer. Re-read the verb first.

:::bars Where marks typically go missing (illustrative, from practice scripts)
Wrong depth for the verb | 80 | Most common
Missing judgement | 60 | Evaluate and discuss
Unsupported claims | 50 | No evidence cited
Missing units and labels | 35 | Calculate and sketch
Time lost on low-mark items | 30 | Over-writing
Teacher-style judgement from practice papers, not a published statistic.
:::

### An error log that works

Keep a short log of lost marks sorted by command term, not by topic. After each practice paper, write the verb, the marks lost, and the reason in one line: "Explain, 3 of 4, gave the pattern but no mechanism." After three papers, patterns appear, and you will find that you are strong on one verb and consistently weak on another. That is a far more targeted revision list than "revise topic 6." The same error-log idea, with a different focus, is a strong habit in maths and is laid out in the [score-a-7 guide for IB Maths AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/).

## A Method for Reading Any Question: The Command Term First

A short routine, practised until it is automatic, removes most command-term errors. The routine takes seconds in the exam and is worth rehearsing in every practice session.

1. Read the whole question once, then circle the command term.
2. Write the mark total next to it. Decide how many separate points that implies.
3. Underline the context words: the data, the case, the named country, the diagram.
4. Decide the shape: single chain, parts and links, two-sided argument, or judgement.
5. Jot a two-line plan in the margin or on the rough paper.
6. Write, and keep the verb in view. Tick off each point as you make it.
7. In the last half minute, check that the final sentence answers the verb, not the topic.

The routine reads as slow on paper. In practice, once the pattern is familiar, steps one to five take under twenty seconds for a long question, and under five for a short one.

### Building the plan from the verb

For each family of verbs, a plan template is useful.

- **Explain:** Cause, mechanism, result. "Because... which means... so..."
- **Analyse:** Elements, links between them, significance.
- **Compare:** Points of comparison, with both items in each sentence.
- **Discuss:** Argument for, argument against, further factor, reasoned position.
- **Evaluate:** Strength, limitation, conditions, judgement.
- **To what extent:** Case for, case against, ranking, a degree stated.
- **Justify:** Claim, reason, evidence from the context.

Students who are anxious about long answers often find that holding a template reduces the blank-page problem. The template is a skeleton, not a script, and an answer that follows it mechanically with no engagement with the context will lose marks for being generic.

## How to Practise Command Terms With Past Papers

Learning definitions is the shallow end. The marks come from recognising what a verb requires inside a real question and then checking your answer against the official markscheme. A practical practice cycle follows.

### A four-week practice cycle

:::timeline A four-week command-term practice cycle
Week 1 | Collect and sort questions by verb
Week 2 | Plan answers only, compare with markscheme
Week 3 | Write full answers under time, mark honestly
Week 4 | Mixed paper, update the verb error log
:::

**Week 1: sort.** Take a set of past-paper questions from one subject and group them by command term. Notice which verbs sit with which mark totals. This alone builds the sense of proportion that was described earlier.

**Week 2: plan only.** For ten questions, write only the plan: the number of points, the structure, the evidence you would use. Then read the markscheme and see what was credited. You will find that you often have the right content and the wrong structure, and this is faster to fix when you are not also writing the full answer.

**Week 3: write and mark.** Write answers under a time limit, then mark them against the official markscheme with strict honesty. Where the scheme says "accept any two of the following," do not give yourself a mark for a third. Where marks depend on a word, check that your word, or an accepted equivalent, is on the page.

**Week 4: mix.** Take a full paper section with mixed verbs. This is where the habit is tested, because in a real paper the verbs change every few minutes.

### Using markschemes the right way

A markscheme is the best teacher of command terms, because it lists the points the examiner actually credits. Look for three things. First, the **accepted wording**, because some marks need a precise term. Second, the **marking notes**, such as "do not accept," "allow ECF" (error carried forward) or "must refer to both," which reveal how strict the verb is in that subject. Third, the **balance of the marks**, for example how many go to knowledge and how many to application in a six-mark question. Subject reports, where your school has them, add a layer: they describe the common errors of a real cohort in plain language. The broader revision timetable these cycles fit into is covered in the [IB exam preparation hub](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/).

### What a tutor session on command terms looks like

A session focused on command terms is usually less like teaching and more like script surgery. The student brings two or three recent answers. The tutor and student read the question aloud, circle the verb, and mark the answer against the scheme together. Then the student rewrites the weakest answer at the right depth, without notes, while the tutor watches for the pattern: does the student drift into explanation, forget the second side, or stop before the judgement? The session ends with one rule for the week, for example "every evaluate answer ends with a sentence that uses the word 'depends' and names the condition." A tutor can teach the skill and question the reasoning. A tutor should not write the student's answers, and for internal assessments the limits are stricter still, as the IA guide explains.

## A Worked Walk-Through: One Topic, Five Verbs

The clearest way to see how command terms change an answer is to hold the topic fixed and vary the verb. Take a single Biology topic: the effect of temperature on enzyme activity.

- **State** one effect of high temperature on an enzyme. *Answer:* "It denatures the enzyme." One mark, five words.
- **Describe** the effect of temperature on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction, as shown in a graph. *Answer:* The rate rises with temperature up to an optimum at about 40 on the axis, then falls sharply and reaches near zero at about 60. Two or three data points quoted, with the shape of each stage.
- **Explain** why the rate falls above the optimum temperature. *Answer:* Above the optimum, the increased vibration breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure, so the shape of the active site changes, the substrate no longer fits, and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
- **Compare** the effect of temperature on an enzyme-catalysed reaction with its effect on an uncatalysed reaction. *Answer:* Both speed up as temperature increases because particles have more kinetic energy and collide more often with energy above the activation energy. Whereas the uncatalysed reaction continues to speed up as temperature rises, the enzyme-catalysed reaction peaks and then falls, because the enzyme denatures.
- **Evaluate** the use of a water bath to control temperature in this investigation. *Answer:* Strengths: it holds temperature steady and allows several values to be tested in parallel. Limitations: the temperature inside the test tube may lag behind the bath, heat loss during sampling, the bath's own fluctuations. Judgement: it is a suitable method for comparing temperatures broadly, but a thermometer in the reaction mixture itself would improve validity.

Five questions, one topic, five different answers, and the content knowledge is nearly the same in each. What changes is the verb. A student who can do this exercise for one topic in each subject has understood command terms in the way the examiners mean them.

## Where Command Terms Show Up Outside Exams

The same vocabulary appears in places students often overlook.

**Internal assessment.** Criteria for IAs use the same verbs implicitly. "Evaluation" in a science IA, for example, means weighing strengths and limitations of the method and conclusion, and the commentary in an Economics portfolio is an extended exercise in "analyse" and "evaluate" applied to a news article. The process and the limits of help are in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

**Theory of Knowledge.** TOK essays and exhibitions are not marked on a command-term list, but the habit of reading what a prescribed title is asking is the same discipline. Several prescribed titles are built on "to what extent" or "evaluate" structures, and a student who treats them as invitations to discuss without reaching a position loses marks in the same way. For the calendar that places TOK beside the Extended Essay and CAS, see the [IB DP core timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/).

**The Extended Essay.** The research question should be answerable, and a question whose verb is evaluative ("To what extent...") shapes the whole structure. The reflection sessions with a supervisor are a place to check that the argument is doing the verb's work. The full process is in the guide to [how to write an IB Extended Essay](/blog/how-to-write-a-perfect-ib-extended-essay-ee/).

**University and beyond.** Admissions essays and first-year assessments use the same verbs. The skill is portable, which is one reason strong command-term habits are worth more than a few marks in May. The wider route from DP to university is covered in the [IB university preparation guide](/blog/ib-university-preparation-guide/).

## IB Command Terms Cheat Sheet

This table is a compact working reference. It paraphrases the usual meaning, and the glossary in your subject guide is the authority.

| Term | What to do | Size of answer |
|---|---|---|
| Define | Give the precise meaning | One sentence |
| State | Give a brief answer, no explanation | A word or short phrase |
| List | Give brief items, no explanation | As many as asked |
| Label | Name parts on a diagram | Words on the diagram |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of features or sequence | Several accurate points |
| Outline | Give a brief account or summary | Short, two or three points |
| Identify | Pick out the correct item | Brief |
| Distinguish | Make differences clear | Both items, differences marked |
| Calculate | Show the working to a numerical answer | Method, number, unit |
| Determine | Obtain the only possible answer | Method plus result |
| Deduce | Draw a conclusion from given information | Short reasoned statement |
| Show that | Reach the printed result | Every step shown |
| Predict | Give an expected result | Outcome, usually with a reason |
| Suggest | Propose a possible answer | One or more specific ideas |
| Estimate | Obtain an approximate value | Value plus assumption |
| Sketch | Draw the general shape with key features | Labelled graph or diagram |
| Explain | Give reasons or causes | A chain, link by link |
| Compare | Show similarities and differences, referring to both | Both items in every point |
| Contrast | Show differences only | Both items in every point |
| Analyse | Break down and show relationships | Parts plus links |
| Discuss | Give a balanced review of arguments or factors | Several sides with evidence |
| Justify | Give valid reasons for a choice or conclusion | Claim, reason, evidence |
| Evaluate | Weigh strengths and limitations | Both sides plus a judgement |
| To what extent | Judge how far a claim holds | Case for, case against, a degree |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are IB command terms?

IB command terms are the specific verbs examiners use at the start of exam questions to show the depth and type of answer required. Examples include define, explain, analyse, evaluate and discuss. Each DP subject guide lists them with definitions, and markschemes are written against them. Knowing the term tells you what kind of point earns each mark.

### Where can I find the official list for my subject?

The official list sits in the glossary of command terms in each subject guide, which your school can give you and which the International Baccalaureate publishes for teachers. Use the version for your current syllabus, since wording changes between guide editions and differs between subjects. If a definition in a website or book disagrees with your guide, follow the guide and ask your teacher.

### What is the difference between explain and analyse in the IB?

Explain asks for reasons or causes in a clear chain of how or why something happens. Analyse asks you to break a topic into parts and show how those parts relate, and often how much each matters. A simple test: if your answer would still make sense with one sentence removed, you have probably listed parts rather than analysed them.

### What is the difference between discuss and evaluate?

Discuss asks for a balanced review of several arguments, factors or views. Evaluate asks you to weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement about worth or effectiveness. A discussion can end with a reasoned position; an evaluation must end with a supported verdict. When in doubt, add a final sentence that states a judgement and names the condition it depends on.

### Do I need a conclusion for every evaluate or to what extent question?

In most subjects, yes, for any question with a substantial mark total. The judgement is usually where the final marks sit, and a missing conclusion caps the answer. For short evaluate questions in the sciences worth two or three marks, one strength, one limitation and a brief appraisal can be enough. Read the mark total and check an official markscheme from the same subject.

### How many points should I write for each mark?

As a working rule, aim for one distinct creditable point per mark, adjusted by the verb. A 4-mark describe needs about four accurate observations. A 4-mark explain may need two developed links. The best way to calibrate is to read markschemes for your subject, count the marking points, and notice how they line up with the marks and the command term.

### Can I lose marks for writing too much?

Yes, in two ways. Extra material can contradict a correct statement, and some markschemes say that a wrong point alongside a right one cancels the mark. Writing too much on low-mark items also costs time you need for the large questions. Precision on state, define and list questions is a real skill, and it is worth deliberate practice.

### Do the same command terms apply in MYP and IGCSE?

The vocabulary overlaps heavily, but the definitions and the weight differ. MYP uses command terms inside criterion descriptors and task-specific clarifications, and Cambridge and Pearson Edexcel IGCSE papers publish their own lists of command words. A student moving from IGCSE into DP should expect familiar verbs to ask for more depth. The six-week IGCSE plan in the [IGCSE revision guide](/blog/igcse-revision-six-week-plan/) includes practice with this vocabulary.

### My child knows the content but still loses marks. Is it command terms?

It might be, and a quick audit will show whether it is. Look at three recent marked answers. If the lost marks are mostly on questions where the student wrote correct material but the markscheme wanted something different, such as mechanism instead of description, or judgement instead of lists, the problem is command-term matching. If marks are lost because facts were wrong or missing, the problem is content, and a different plan is needed.

### Will a tutor help with command terms, and what should they do?

A good tutor can help by marking real answers against official schemes, explaining why marks were lost, and having the student rewrite answers at the right depth. Ask for work on your child's actual scripts, not generic worksheets. A tutor should teach the skill but not write answers for assessed work. IB Gram can shortlist a subject tutor and arrange a trial session before you commit.

### Is it worth memorising a list of command terms?

Memorising the list is a start, but it does not change scripts by itself. What changes marks is practice: circling the verb in real questions, planning the answer shape, and checking against the markscheme. Ten minutes of marking one answer for command-term fit is worth more than an hour of re-reading the glossary. Keep the glossary open while practising, and gradually rely on it less.

1. **Take three recent marked answers** and circle the command term in each question. Write beside it the mark total and the kind of point the markscheme credited. Note the gap between what you wrote and what was credited.
