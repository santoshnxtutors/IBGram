If you want to know how to write an IB Extended Essay, the short answer is this: pick a subject you can already work in, shape a question you can answer with evidence you can actually reach, then spend most of your time arguing, not collecting. The Extended Essay (EE) is an independent research essay of up to 4,000 words, about 40 hours of work, supervised by a teacher at your own school. Most weak essays fail in the first month, at the question, not in the last month, at the writing.

This guide is for Diploma Programme (DP) students in DP1 who have just been told to "start thinking about the EE", for DP2 students who are behind, and for parents who want to understand what the supervisor is and is not doing. It covers choosing the subject, building and stress-testing the research question, running the supervisor sessions, structuring the argument, the reflection record, a month-by-month timeline, and the mistakes that cost the most. It does not walk through the EE alongside CAS and TOK in one calendar; that is in the [IB DP core timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/).

One caution before anything else. The EE guide was updated for first assessment in 2027, and schools differ in how they run internal deadlines. So this post never quotes mark allocations for the assessment criteria. Whatever version of the guide your DP coordinator hands you is the one that counts. Everything below is about method, which does not change when the guide does.

## What is the IB Extended Essay?

The Extended Essay is a compulsory part of the International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme: an independent piece of research of up to 4,000 words, written in one DP subject or as an interdisciplinary essay. It takes roughly 40 hours, is supervised by a teacher at your school, and includes recorded reflection sessions with that supervisor. It is graded A to E, and the grade combines with your Theory of Knowledge (TOK) grade to give up to 3 diploma points.

Two consequences follow from that definition, and students tend to miss both.

First, the EE is a research exercise, not a long school essay. A good Class 12 project report describes a topic. An Extended Essay answers a question that has more than one defensible answer, and shows the reader why the author chose the one they did.

Second, the stakes are higher than the word count suggests. An E in either the EE or TOK is a failing condition for the diploma, however well the six subjects go. A strong EE can lift a borderline total; a missing or failing one can sink a good one. The full points picture, including the 24-point minimum, is explained in the [IB exam preparation hub](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/).

:::stats The Extended Essay at a glance
4,000 | Maximum words
~40 | Recommended hours of work
A-E | Grade, combined with TOK
3 | Maximum diploma points from EE and TOK together
:::

## Which subject should you write your Extended Essay in?

Write it in a subject where you already know how a good answer is built, usually one you study at Higher Level (HL) or one you enjoy enough to read around unprompted. The subject sets the rules of evidence your essay will be judged by, so a subject you understand at depth protects you from the commonest failure: spending three months learning the basics instead of researching.

Use this test, in order:

1. **Can you describe what counts as evidence in this subject?** In History it is sources and their provenance. In Economics it is data and a model. In Biology or Chemistry it is your own experiment or a rigorous secondary dataset. In English it is close reading of a text. If you cannot say this in one sentence, the subject is a risk.
2. **Is there a supervisor who can really guide you?** Schools allocate supervisors, and a Mathematics teacher may not be the best guide to a Philosophy question. Ask your coordinator who supervises which subjects before you commit.
3. **Can you reach the evidence from Gurgaon, Delhi or wherever you live?** An essay that depends on archives in another country, or lab equipment your school does not have, fails at the logistics, not at the thinking.
4. **Would you be willing to spend 40 hours on it in a year that also contains IAs and mocks?** Interest is a practical resource here, not a nicety.

Do not choose a subject because you think it is "easy to score". Students pick Global Politics or Business Management for the supposed lower difficulty and then discover that a vague essay in those subjects scores poorly precisely because everyone has opinions. Subjects are not easy or hard; questions are well-shaped or badly shaped.

### A quick comparison of common choices

| Subject | What the evidence usually is | The usual trap |
|---|---|---|
| Economics | Published data plus a model or theory | Describing a policy without testing it against data |
| History | Primary and secondary sources, with provenance | A narrative of events with no argument about them |
| Biology, Chemistry, Physics | Own experiment or a reliable secondary dataset | An experiment with too few trials or an uncontrolled variable |
| Mathematics | A mathematical investigation or proof | Reproducing textbook material at length |
| English A | Close reading of one or two literary works | Plot summary dressed as analysis |
| Business Management | A real organisation, with data and stakeholders | A company description with no decision to evaluate |
| Psychology | Published studies, or a small ethical experiment | Ethics and sampling not thought through in advance |

The table is a rule of thumb, not an official list. The subject-specific guidance for your chosen subject is in the EE guide your school uses.

## How do you write a good Extended Essay research question?

A good research question is narrow enough to answer in 4,000 words, open enough that the answer is an argument, and tied to evidence you can get before the end of the summer term. It names the thing you study, the angle you take, and usually a boundary of time, place or case. If it can be answered with a fact, it is too small. If it needs a book to answer, it is too big.

Here is the sequence that works, from topic to question:

1. **Start with a topic area, not a question.** "Inflation", "the partition of Bengal", "enzyme activity", "Kafka".
2. **Read two or three overviews.** A good textbook chapter and a recent article. You are looking for a disagreement or a gap, not a summary.
3. **Write five candidate questions in different shapes.** Each should start differently: "To what extent", "How far", "Why did", "How does X affect Y in Z", "Which of two models better explains".
4. **Apply the evidence test.** For each, write the three sources or datasets you would use. Cross out any question where you cannot name them.
5. **Apply the so-what test.** If you answer the question, does the answer depend on judgement? If every reader would give the same answer, the question is a fact-check.
6. **Take the best two to your supervisor.** Not one. A supervisor choosing between two is more helpful than one who is only approving or rejecting.

### Weak and stronger questions, with the reason

The old version of this post offered a handful of pairs. Here is the reasoning behind them, plus some new cases, because the reasoning is what transfers.

| Weak version | Why it fails | Stronger version |
|---|---|---|
| What happened during the Cold War? | A topic, not a question. Decades of material. | To what extent did the Cuban Missile Crisis change US-Soviet diplomatic communication between 1962 and 1965? |
| How does temperature affect plants? | No named species, response variable or range. | To what extent does temperature between 15 and 35 degrees Celsius affect the germination rate of mung beans over seven days? |
| Inflation in India | A topic. No argument possible. | To what extent did changes in the repo rate influence consumer price inflation in India between 2019 and 2023? |
| Mathematics in basketball | Vague link, no method. | How accurately can a projectile model with air resistance predict the trajectory of a basketball free throw filmed at 240 frames per second? |
| Is social media bad for teenagers? | Value judgement, impossible to measure in one essay. | What is the relationship between evening screen time and self-reported sleep duration among students aged 16 to 18 in one school? |
| Why is Hamlet a great play? | "Great" is not an analytical term. | How does Shakespeare's use of soliloquy in Hamlet shape the audience's judgement of the protagonist's delay? |

Notice that the stronger versions carry a boundary (dates, a range, one school, one device) and an analytical verb. Boundaries are not a limitation on your ambition; they are what make a question answerable. The second pair also shows something specific to experimental subjects: if your question requires a variable you cannot control with the equipment available to you, change the question, not the equipment.

If your question is in a science, check the one practical issue early: ethics and safety approval. If it is in Psychology or the human sciences, check the same for any survey. Your coordinator will have a process. Skipping it can invalidate months of work.

## What does a supervisor actually do, and what should you bring to each meeting?

A supervisor is a teacher at your school who guides your thinking, checks that the work is your own, and signs off the recorded reflection sessions. They are not your editor. Under IB integrity rules they should not rewrite your paragraphs or hand you a structure to copy, and the amount of feedback they can give on a draft is limited by the guide.

You will typically have three recorded reflection points across the project: an early one when the question is set, an interim one while you research and draft, and a final viva-style conversation after submission. Your school will confirm the exact arrangement and the form it uses.

What makes meetings useful is preparation. A student who arrives saying "I'm stuck" gets general reassurance. A student who arrives with a one-page note gets decisions.

**Bring to meeting one (question stage):**
- Two candidate questions and the three sources you would use for each.
- One paragraph on why you chose this subject.
- The question you are most afraid of: "can I get the data?", "is this too big?".

**Bring to the interim meeting (draft stage):**
- A complete outline with a one-sentence claim for each section.
- One drafted section, not the introduction. The strongest body section.
- A list of what you have discarded and why. This is the raw material for the reflection.

**Bring to the final meeting:**
- The submitted essay and a short list of what you would do differently. Honest, specific reflection reads far better than polished self-praise.

> A supervisor can only improve the essay you show them. Hiding a weak draft until the deadline is the most expensive habit in the project.

## What should the structure of an Extended Essay look like?

The structure is a title page, contents, an introduction that sets out the question and approach, a body organised by argument rather than by source, a conclusion that answers the question, and references. Charts, tables and appendices are used sparingly and never to carry the argument. The details differ by subject, so check your subject's section of the guide, but the logic below holds across almost all of them.

| Section | Job it does | A common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Title page and contents | Show the question and the subject at once | A vague title that is not the research question |
| Introduction | Context, the question, why it matters, how you will answer it | Three pages of background before the question appears |
| Body section 1 | Establish the framework, theory or method you will use | A literature review that never touches the question |
| Body sections 2 to 4 | Each makes one claim, backed by evidence, then evaluated | Sections organised by source, not by claim |
| Conclusion | A direct answer, with the extent and the limits stated | Repeating the introduction, or a sudden new argument |
| References and bibliography | Let a reader trace every claim | Added on the last night, inconsistent format |

### The paragraph engine

Every body paragraph should do five things in a fixed order. Think of it as a unit you can repeat thirty times.

1. **Claim.** A sentence that makes a point relevant to the question.
2. **Evidence.** The datum, quotation, source or result that supports it.
3. **Analysis.** What the evidence shows and why, in your words.
4. **Evaluation.** How reliable it is, what else could explain it, what it cannot tell you.
5. **Link.** One sentence back to the research question.

The step students skip is the fourth. Evaluation is where the essay stops reporting and starts thinking. A claim without evaluation reads as certainty the evidence cannot support.

### Description versus analysis, shown in practice

Consider a student writing on monetary policy.

Descriptive: "The Reserve Bank raised the repo rate several times in 2022. Inflation was also high during that period."

Analytical: "Repo rate increases between May 2022 and February 2023 coincided with a fall in the monthly consumer price inflation reading from its peak. The timing is consistent with the transmission mechanism, but a lag of several months is expected, and food and fuel prices, which the repo rate influences weakly, drove much of the earlier rise. The correlation is therefore suggestive, not conclusive."

The second version commits to a reading, states the mechanism, names a confounder and limits its own claim. That is the pattern examiners reward in every subject, and it takes only one extra sentence per paragraph.

## How to write an IB Extended Essay across a school year

Work backwards from your school's internal deadline, not from the IB's, because your school's deadline is the real one. A workable plan front-loads the thinking: the question and evidence plan in the first two months, research and drafting in the middle, and revision with a buffer at the end. Students who start writing in the last third usually have material but no argument.

Most Indian IB schools run the EE from about the middle or end of DP1 into the first half of DP2, but the dates are the school's choice. Use the plan below as a template and shift every month to match your coordinator's calendar.

:::timeline A typical EE plan, adjusted to your school's calendar
Month 1 | Subject choice, topic area, first reading, two candidate questions
Month 2 | Question agreed with supervisor, evidence plan, reading log started
Months 3-4 | Research or experiment, notes organised by claim not by source
Months 5-6 | Full outline, first complete draft of the strongest section
Month 7 | Complete draft, interim reflection session, supervisor feedback
Month 8 | Revision against the guide, referencing, formatting, final read
:::

The plan hides one real pressure point. DP1 summer holidays are where many students gain or lose the EE. Subject IAs, CAS and TOK all compete for the same autumn term, and a student who has not finished research before school restarts often never catches up. A student who drafts one body section over the holidays walks into term one with momentum. If this is your situation, the interlocking picture of all the core components is in the [IB DP core timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/), and the discipline of doing the Internal Assessment honestly is in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

### What a 40-hour budget looks like

Forty hours sounds small until you divide it. A rough allocation, to be adjusted by subject:

:::bars Illustrative split of 40 EE hours
Reading and notes | 25 | about 10 hours
Research or experiment | 25 | about 10 hours
Drafting | 30 | about 12 hours
Revision and referencing | 20 | about 8 hours
This is a planning guide, not an official split. Experimental essays shift hours towards the research stage.
:::

Under this budget, a student who spends 20 hours reading has only 20 left for everything else. That is the arithmetic behind "stop reading and start drafting" advice, and the reason the reading log below matters.

## How do you research without drowning in sources?

Research for an EE is the process of finding the minimum body of reliable evidence that lets you answer the question, and recording it so you can use it. You are not trying to read everything. You are trying to read enough to know where the argument sits and where the disagreements are.

A reading log solves most problems. For each source, one entry with six fields:

1. Full reference in your school's required format, written once, correctly.
2. One sentence on what the source claims.
3. The specific page, table or figure you may cite.
4. How reliable it is and why (author, date, method, bias).
5. Which of your claims it supports or challenges.
6. A quotation or datum, with page, if you will use it directly.

Fields four and five turn a log from a list into a map of your argument. After about twenty entries you will see that three claims have strong support and two have none. That is the moment to spend your remaining reading hours on the two, not on a twenty-first source for the first three.

### Judging sources

Ask of every source: who produced this, when, for whom, using what evidence, and what does it leave out? A government statistical release and a blog post quoting that release are not equal. Peer-reviewed journal articles, books from academic publishers, official data series and primary documents are strong. Generic blogs, unsourced summaries and text produced by AI tools are weak, and an AI tool can invent references that do not exist. Never cite a source you have not opened and read yourself; a supervisor or examiner who tries to find a fabricated reference will treat it as an integrity problem.

If your school uses similarity checking, as most do, then paraphrasing without credit is detected and handled under the IB's academic integrity policy. Quoting with a clean citation is not a problem. Copying structure from a published essay is.

## How do you handle data, experiments and evidence in different subjects?

The rule is the same everywhere: show the reader how the evidence was gathered, how you know it is reliable, and what it cannot prove. What changes by subject is the form.

**In the sciences.** Control variables, repeat trials, record uncertainty, and say why the sample size is what it is. A small, well-controlled experiment beats a large messy one. Plan so the data can fail: if there is only one possible outcome, the question is not open.

**In Economics and the social sciences.** Use a defined data series with source, dates and units, and name the model before you apply it. A graph with no discussion is decoration. Say what the model assumes and where reality departs from it.

**In History.** Treat sources by provenance: who wrote it, when, for whom, with what purpose. The argument is the interplay of sources, not a chain of quotations.

**In literature and language.** Quote short passages and analyse the language, not the plot. Every quotation needs a sentence explaining what the choice of word or form does to the reader.

**In Mathematics.** The essay explores a question; it does not reproduce a textbook proof. Define notation early, show a genuine line of reasoning and reflect on its limits. Students working in mathematics who also face the Internal Assessment should keep the two clearly separate in topic and scope. The maths-specific skills are covered in the [flagship guide to scoring a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/).

## What are the most common Extended Essay mistakes?

The commonest mistakes are a question that is too broad, description where analysis is wanted, evidence gathered but never evaluated, a bibliography built at the end, and a reflection record written from memory the night before. Each has a cheap fix if you catch it early and an expensive one if you catch it at submission.

| Mistake | How it shows on the page | Cheap fix |
|---|---|---|
| Question too broad | Every section is a survey, nothing is argued | Add a boundary: dates, place, case, range |
| Question too narrow | Answered in 800 words, then padding | Ask "why" or "to what extent" about your finding |
| Description over analysis | Paragraphs of "what happened" with no "so what" | Add an evaluation sentence to every paragraph |
| Source dumping | Twenty sources, none evaluated | Cut to eight strong ones and evaluate each |
| Changing the question late | The introduction no longer fits the body | Lock the question by month two, adjust only wording |
| Ignoring counterarguments | Every source agrees with the thesis | Search deliberately for the best opposing view |
| Late referencing | Missing page numbers, inconsistent style | Write each citation in the reading log the day you read |
| Padding to reach 4,000 | Repetition, long quotations | Cut. Under the limit is fine; over it is penalised |
| Reflection written at the end | Vague, generic statements | Dated notes after every meeting |

The word limit deserves a line. The limit is 4,000 words, and the guide specifies what counts and what does not, so confirm it with your coordinator. Writing under the limit is not penalised for length; writing over it can be penalised by what examiners read beyond it.

## How do you write the introduction, the conclusion and the reflection?

When you plan how to write an IB Extended Essay, write the body first and the introduction and conclusion last, because only then do you know what the essay argues. The introduction states the question, the context needed to understand it and the route the essay will take. The conclusion answers the question directly, says how far the evidence supports that answer, and names what remains uncertain. The reflection is a separate, honest record of your process.

**The introduction** can be done in roughly four moves: a sentence or two of context, the research question stated verbatim, the approach (the sources, method or theory you use), and a line on why the question matters. Keep it short. If your introduction runs past about a tenth of the total, the background is probably doing the body's job.

**The conclusion** has one task: to answer. Open with the answer to the question in plain words. Then state the extent: "largely", "partly", "only under these conditions". Then name one limitation of your evidence and one thing a further study could test. Do not introduce a new argument or a new source. If the evidence was mixed, say so; an essay that forces a confident answer from mixed evidence is less convincing than one that states the mix clearly.

**The reflection** is the record of how your thinking changed. Most students treat it as paperwork. It is better treated as a diary of decisions: why you dropped a source, why you narrowed the question, what a supervisor said that changed your plan, what went wrong in the method and how you adapted. Write two or three sentences within a day of each supervisor meeting. By the final session you will have genuine material, instead of a paragraph invented under time pressure.

## How does the Extended Essay differ from the Internal Assessment, and where does outside help fit?

The EE is a stand-alone research essay graded A to E that contributes to the core, whereas an Internal Assessment (IA) is a subject-specific task marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB, contributing to that subject's 1-7 grade. They are written under the same integrity rules but serve different purposes, and the supervisor model differs. The IA process, criteria and timeline are in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/), with worked comparisons in [IB IA examples](/blog/complete-guide-to-ib-internal-assessment-ia-with-examples/).

Outside help is where families have the most questions, so here is the plain version. The integrity rules say the essay is the student's own work. A tutor, a parent or an older sibling may not write, rewrite or edit the submission. A good tutor may legitimately:

- Teach the skill underneath: how to evaluate a source, how to structure a paragraph, how to read a dataset.
- Ask the student questions that expose a gap in the reasoning.
- Point to the assessment guidance and ask the student to reread their own paragraph against it.
- Help the student plan their time and keep the reading log.

A tutor may not supply a question, a thesis, paragraphs or a bibliography. If a family is unsure whether help has crossed the line, the coordinator is the right person to ask, and asking is always safer than guessing.

## What does a tutoring session for an EE actually look like?

A good EE session is a working session on the student's own material: a half-hour on one problem, with the student doing most of the talking. It is not a lecture on the subject, and it never ends with the tutor handing over text. Consider a typical student at the question stage who brings a draft question about "the impact of fast fashion on India's economy".

The tutor's questions might run like this. Impact on which part of the economy: employment, exports, the environment? Over what period? Measured how? Where would the data come from? The student, by answering, discovers that the question has three possible essays inside it. They leave with homework: choose one and write three alternative versions of the question with a boundary each.

A mid-project session looks different. The student brings one body section. The tutor reads it with them and asks, paragraph by paragraph, "what is the claim here, and where is your evaluation?" The student marks the paragraphs that lack one and rewrites them between sessions.

A late session is about the process: checking that citations are consistent, that every claim in the introduction is supported in the body, and that the conclusion answers the question as worded. The student, not the tutor, does the checking. This is slow and the student sometimes finds it frustrating, because the "quick fix" would be for the tutor to do it. It is also the only version that stays within the rules and leaves the student able to do it alone in the viva.

## When should you ask for help, and when do you not need it?

Ask for help when your question keeps changing, when you have been "researching" for more than a month with no draft section, when feedback from your supervisor is too general to act on, or when time is short and you cannot see a path to the deadline. You do not need outside help if your question is agreed, your reading log is growing and your draft sections are landing with your supervisor.

The signals that a student is drifting:

- The question has been reworded more than twice since the first meeting.
- There are more than ten sources in the log and still no outline.
- The supervisor has asked for the same fix in two meetings running.
- The student describes the essay as "about" a topic rather than "arguing" a claim.
- The word count is climbing but the number of claims is not.

The right response to any of these is a smaller task, not a bigger one: write the one-sentence claim for each planned section, then see which claims have no evidence.

## How to write an IB Extended Essay well in the last four weeks

In the last four weeks you stop adding and start checking. Freeze the question, stop reading new sources, finish a complete draft, use your supervisor feedback, and then run a structured read against the guide. A calm final month usually separates a solid essay from a rushed one.

Here is a four-week sequence that works, assuming your school's deadline is the end of week four.

1. **Week 1: Complete the draft.** Every section written, even roughly. Gaps marked in brackets. No new reading.
2. **Week 2: Cut and sharpen.** Remove paragraphs that do not serve the question. Add the missing evaluation sentences. Check the word count.
3. **Week 3: Check against the guide.** Reread the guide for your subject and the assessment criteria. Mark each criterion against the essay, honestly. Fix the weakest area first.
4. **Week 4: Present and proof.** Consistent referencing, labelled figures, a contents page, page numbers, the title matching the question. Read aloud once. Submit a day early.

Reading aloud catches more errors than any other final check, because the ear finds the clumsy sentence that the eye has stopped seeing. And do not use the last week for anything ambitious. A new chart added on the final night is more likely to contain an error than to add a mark.

## What does a good reflection record look like?

A good reflection is specific, dated and about decisions. It says what you did, what you learned, what you changed, and what you would do differently. It does not praise the essay or repeat the supervisor's comments.

Weak: "I learned a lot about research and I think my essay is quite good. My supervisor was very helpful."

Stronger: "After my second meeting I dropped the survey method because my school could not give me a large enough sample. I switched to published household survey data, which narrowed the question to one state. The change cost two weeks but made the evidence more reliable. If I did this again I would check data availability before choosing the question."

The second version shows a decision, a trade-off and a lesson. That is the whole job of the reflection. If you keep a notebook entry after each meeting, you will have this material without effort.

## How does the EE fit with everything else in DP1 and DP2?

The EE competes for time with subject IAs, CAS, TOK and mock exams. The practical rule is to place its heaviest work where the rest of the diploma is lightest: the DP1 summer holiday and the weeks between mocks and finals are usually less crowded than the first term of DP2.

Two interactions are worth planning for. The first is with the TOK essay and exhibition, which have their own deadlines and which many schools place close together. The second is with exam revision: a student still drafting in the spring of DP2 is trading marks in six subjects for an essay worth a limited number of points. That is why the planning conversation should happen early, with all three core components on one page. The [IB DP core timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/) covers that calendar. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should an IB Extended Essay be?

The limit is 4,000 words, and the recommended effort is about 40 hours. The guide specifies what counts towards the word count, so confirm what is included with your coordinator. Writing a little under the limit is not penalised for length alone, but a thin essay usually reveals a question that was too small. Aim to use the space for argument and evaluation, not background and quotation.

### Can I change my research question after I start?

Yes, but change the wording early, not the substance late. Narrowing a question in the first two months is normal and healthy. Replacing it in the last third usually means throwing away the body, and the essay's introduction and conclusion stop matching. If your evidence is not working, talk to your supervisor, and record the change and the reason in your reflection notes.

### How do I know if my topic is too broad?

If you can imagine a whole book on it, it is too broad. A fast test: can you name the three sources or datasets you would use, and would they fit in 4,000 words of analysis? If not, add a boundary of time, place, case or measurement range. A question with no boundary is usually a topic.

### What should I do if my supervisor is not helpful?

Ask for specific feedback on a specific piece of work, in writing if possible. A one-page note with a question is easier to answer than "can you look at my essay?". If the problem continues, speak to your EE coordinator, who can mediate. Do not wait until the interim deadline to raise it. The supervisor's role is to guide, not to write, so ask for questions and direction, not text.

### Can a tutor help me with my Extended Essay?

A tutor can show you how to write an IB Extended Essay by teaching research and writing skills and challenge your reasoning, but cannot write, rewrite or edit your essay. IB integrity rules require the work to be your own. Useful help includes how to evaluate sources, how to structure an argument and how to plan your time. If you are unsure where the line is, ask your coordinator. No tutor can guarantee a grade.

### Is it better to write the EE in my HL subject?

Often, but not always. A subject you study at depth gives you the vocabulary and the habits of evidence the essay needs, and your teacher may be the right supervisor. A subject you love but do not study can work if a qualified supervisor is available and you can reach the evidence. Choose for knowledge, access to evidence and interest, in that order.

### How many sources do I need?

There is no required number. A focused essay may rest on eight to fifteen well-chosen, evaluated sources. A long list of unevaluated ones is worse than a short list of strong ones. Quality, relevance and the quality of your evaluation matter, and the number only has to match what the question needs.

### What happens if I miss the EE deadline?

Your school sets internal deadlines, and the IB sets the final submission window. Missing the school deadline usually triggers a conversation with the coordinator and may delay feedback. Failing to submit an essay means no grade in the EE, which is a failing condition for the diploma. Contact your coordinator the moment you see a deadline slipping, not afterwards.

### Can I use AI tools to help write my Extended Essay?

Treat AI-generated text as a risk, not a shortcut. The essay has to be your own work, AI tools can invent facts and references, and your school's similarity checking and the IB's integrity policy apply. Using a tool to explain a concept you then research properly is different from pasting its output. Follow your school's stated policy and cite any permitted use as the guidance requires.

### How is the Extended Essay graded?

It is graded A to E against assessment criteria in the EE guide, and the grade combines with TOK for up to 3 diploma points. The criteria and their mark allocations changed with the version of the guide first assessed in 2027, so use the document your coordinator provides. In practice, examiners look for a focused question, relevant evidence, sound analysis, a clear structure and honest reflection.

### Should I start the Extended Essay in DP1 or DP2?

Start the thinking in DP1. Most schools begin the formal process in DP1 for exactly this reason: the first two months, choosing the subject and shaping the question, cost little time and save a great deal later. Waiting until DP2 to choose a subject leaves too little time for research alongside mocks and IAs.
