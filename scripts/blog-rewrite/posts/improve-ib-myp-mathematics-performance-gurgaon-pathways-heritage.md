To improve IB MYP mathematics performance, stop asking how much practice a student is doing and ask which of the four criteria is costing marks. A student who has been given more worksheets for six weeks and whose result has not moved is usually practising the skill they already have, not the one they lack. The fix is a diagnosis, a target for each criterion, and a short cycle of practice and review that is aimed at the gap.

This guide is a 12-week plan for parents and students in the Middle Years Programme (MYP), written with MYP 4 and MYP 5 in mind, and with Gurgaon families in particular. It explains how MYP maths is scored, how to find the real weakness, how to set targets that add up, what to do in each phase of the 12 weeks, how to measure progress without guessing, and what to do at the end. It uses clearly illustrative students and worked examples, and makes no promise about a grade. The plan works whether or not a tutor is involved, and the sections on tutoring explain where one adds most.

## Why Does More Practice Often Not Improve MYP Maths Results?

More practice often fails in MYP maths because the assessment rewards four different skills, and routine worksheets train only one of them. A student who is already competent at straightforward questions can complete a hundred more and be no better at investigating a pattern, explaining reasoning or modelling a real situation, which is where the marks are being lost.

Think of a typical student at the end of a term. Their quizzes on algebra are fine. Their assessed investigation scored low, and the report comment says "needs to justify conclusions." The family reads this as "weak at maths" and buys more practice questions. The practice questions are all of the kind the student already answers well. Six weeks later, the quiz scores are marginally higher and the investigation score has not moved.

The reason is structural. Each MYP maths criterion rewards something different, and the way to improve a criterion is to practise the thing it measures. That means the first job is to find out which criterion is the real bottleneck, and the second is to build practice around it.

## How MYP Mathematics Is Scored

Every MYP subject is assessed against four criteria, A to D, each scored 0 to 8, so the criterion total is out of 32. The total is converted to a final 1 to 7 grade through grade boundaries set by the International Baccalaureate (IB). For mathematics the four criteria are:

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
32 | Maximum criterion total
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

| Criterion | Name | What it rewards | Typical way marks are lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Knowing and understanding | Correct use of concepts, procedures and terminology | Gaps in foundations, slips in procedure, wrong terms |
| B | Investigating patterns | Exploring, spotting a pattern, generalising, justifying | Stopping at examples, no general rule, no proof or check |
| C | Communicating | Clear, organised mathematical presentation and reasoning | Missing steps, undefined symbols, unlabelled graphs |
| D | Applying mathematics in real-life contexts | Modelling a situation, solving it, interpreting and evaluating | No model, no interpretation, no comment on limits |

Schools often split MYP 4 and 5 mathematics into standard and extended, with extended covering additional topics that prepare a student for the Diploma Programme (DP) subject Mathematics Analysis and Approaches. The final grade boundaries, and how a school reports them, are the school's and the IB's to confirm. There is no single national MYP examination in India, and MYP eAssessment in MYP 5, with its on-screen examinations and ePortfolios, is optional, each school deciding whether to offer it. Ask the school which tasks count toward the final grade for your child.

## The Plan at a Glance

To improve IB MYP mathematics performance in a way you can measure, the plan has four phases across twelve weeks. It is deliberately simple, because a plan that cannot be explained on one page is rarely followed.

:::timeline The 12-week improvement plan
Weeks 1-2 | Diagnose: gather evidence, find the weak criterion
Weeks 3-6 | Repair: targeted practice on the lowest two criteria
Weeks 7-10 | Integrate: mixed tasks, timed work, unfamiliar contexts
Weeks 11-12 | Review: reassess, compare, plan the next cycle
:::

The reason for twelve weeks is practical. It is long enough for a habit to settle and for at least one assessed task to show whether something has changed. It is short enough that a student can see an end. Many MYP terms run for roughly this length, but confirm the school's assessment dates and fit the plan around them, not the other way round.

## Weeks 1 and 2: Diagnose Before You Prescribe

The first two weeks are about evidence, not teaching. The aim is to know, by the end of week 2, which criterion is the bottleneck and what kind of difficulty sits inside it.

### What to collect

Gather the last term's marked work, with the criterion scores and teacher comments, and sort it.

- **Assessed tasks** with the criterion levels, from the school.
- **Unit tests or quizzes**, with the actual scripts, not just the marks.
- **An investigation or real-life task**, if one has been set.
- **Homework**, particularly where the same error appears more than once.
- **The student's own account**: which questions felt impossible, which felt easy, where time ran out.

Three questions guide the sorting. Which criterion has the lowest level? Is the pattern consistent across tasks or does it come from one bad task? And is the loss at the start of a response (not knowing what to do), in the middle (execution) or at the end (not interpreting)?

### The four illustrative profiles

Students are not identical, and the same grade can hide different problems. Consider four hypothetical MYP students, each with a criterion total of about 18 out of 32.

| Profile | Pattern across criteria | Likely root cause | First priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student One | A 6, B 4, C 4, D 4 | Competent at procedures; stuck on open and applied tasks | B and D |
| Student Two | A 5, B 5, C 3, D 5 | Understands, but writes too little to earn marks | C |
| Student Three | A 3, B 5, C 5, D 5 | Foundation gaps with decent reasoning | A |
| Student Four | A 5, B 5, C 4, D 4 | Even, slightly weak under time pressure | Timed work and D |

The totals are the same, but the plan for each is different. Giving all four the same worksheet is the common mistake.

### A diagnostic session

In a good diagnostic, whether run by a parent, a teacher or a tutor, the student does three things on paper, out loud:

1. **Solve two routine questions** and explain each first step. This checks Criterion A and shows how the student thinks.
2. **Attempt a short pattern task**, such as continuing a sequence and describing the rule. This checks Criterion B.
3. **Work through a short contextual problem** and write the solution in full. This checks C and D together.

The adult watches and writes down where the student hesitates, which words they avoid, and what they do when stuck. A transcript of the reasoning is more useful than a score.

## Setting Targets That Add Up

A target of "get a 6" is not actionable. A target for each criterion is. Here is how to build one.

Take the most recent criterion levels. Suppose a student is at A 5, B 4, C 4, D 3, a total of 16. The question is where the next points come from most cheaply. Moving D from 3 to 5 is usually more achievable than moving A from 5 to 7, because the weak criterion has more obvious room and the habits needed are learnable. A plan that adds two points to D, one to B and one to C raises the total to 20.

| Criterion | Current | Target after 12 weeks | Points to find |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 5 or 6 | 0 to 1 |
| B | 4 | 5 | 1 |
| C | 4 | 5 | 1 |
| D | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| Total | 16 | 20 to 21 | 4 to 5 |

How many points of total are needed to move up a final grade depends on the IB boundaries for the session or the school's conversion, so ask the teacher. For the grade-band mechanics and which criterion moves fastest, see [how can an MYP maths student move from a 5 to a 6 or 7](/blog/how-can-an-ib-myp-maths-student-move-from-5-to-6-or-7/). The targets above are illustrations of the method. They are not predictions, and no tutor can guarantee a grade.

## Which Criterion Should You Fix First?

Fix the criterion where the student's level is lowest and the cause is something a student can change within weeks. For most students this is C or D, because communication and application habits respond quickly to practice, while a weak foundation in A needs a longer repair. If A is the lowest, start there, because the others depend on it.

A useful ordering rule:

- **If A is two or more levels below the others**, repair foundations first. Reasoning and application will not hold up on shaky procedure.
- **If C is lowest**, start there. It is the quickest to improve, since it is mostly habit.
- **If D is lowest**, start with D and keep A ticking over. D needs the most varied practice and benefits from the longest runway.
- **If B is lowest**, begin pattern work early, since investigation skills build slowly.

Where levels are even, choose the one the next assessed task will test most.

## Weeks 3 and 4: Repairing Criterion A and Building the Error Log

Whatever the weak criterion, the first repair block deals with foundations and sets up the error log, because both will be needed later.

**Foundations.** List the topics from the diagnostic where procedure broke down: fractions, negative numbers, linear equations, ratio, angle facts, whatever it is. Spend short, regular sessions on each, using three or four varied questions, not forty identical ones. After each, ask the student to explain why the method works. A student who can say why can adapt when the question changes.

**The error log.** From week 3, every mistake in practice goes into a log with its cause and a fix. This is the single habit that most reliably separates students whose results improve from those whose results do not.

| Mistake | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sign error when expanding 3(x − 2) | Rushed, did not write the middle line | Always write the expanded line |
| Answer "x = 11" with no check | Habit of stopping at the answer | Substitute back and write the check |
| Graph with no axis labels | Treated as decoration | Label axes and units every time |
| Real-life answer with no sentence | Treated the number as the answer | End with a sentence in the context's words |
| Pattern described as "it goes up" | No rule attempted | State the rule in words, then in algebra |

Review the log weekly. An entry that appears three times is not carelessness. It is an untrained habit, and it deserves a short dedicated drill.

## Weeks 5 and 6: Criterion B, Learning to Investigate

Criterion B asks students to investigate patterns: generate examples, notice a relationship, state a general rule and justify it. Many students were taught to ask "which formula?" and are lost when there is none. The aim of this block is to replace that question with "what is changing, and what stays the same?"

### A worked example

Consider the sequence 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. A student asked to investigate it should be taken through a sequence of moves, not handed a formula.

First, generate more terms and look at the differences: 2, 3, 4, 5. The differences grow by one each time. That is a pattern about the pattern. Second, make a conjecture: the nth term is the sum 1 + 2 + … + n. Third, test it: for n = 5, 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15, which matches. Fourth, find a general rule. Pairing the first and last terms gives n/2 pairs each summing to n + 1, so the rule is n(n + 1)/2. Fifth, check it against a term not yet used: for n = 6, 6 × 7 / 2 = 21, and the next difference would be 6, so 15 + 6 = 21. It works. Finally, justify why: the pairing argument is the reason, not just the check.

Notice that the work sits in the moves, not in the formula. A student who can narrate those moves on a new sequence, say 2, 5, 10, 17, has acquired the Criterion B habit. (Differences 3, 5, 7 grow by 2, suggesting a quadratic, and the rule n² + 1 fits: 1 + 1 = 2, 4 + 1 = 5, 9 + 1 = 10, 16 + 1 = 17.)

### Practice that builds it

- **Do one open investigation a week**, for 30 to 40 minutes, and write it up as a short report.
- **Always include a general rule and a check**, even if the rule is partly wrong. The attempt earns more than silence.
- **Ask the student to explain a pattern aloud** before writing. If the speech is vague, the writing will be too.

## Weeks 7 and 8: Criterion C, Making Reasoning Visible

Communication is the quickest criterion to improve and the one students most underestimate. A student who solves a problem in their head and writes only the answer has not demonstrated anything the assessor can credit.

### A before and after

Take the equation 3(x − 2) = 2x + 5.

*A low-scoring response:* "x = 11."

*A stronger response:*

"Expand the bracket: 3x − 6 = 2x + 5. Subtract 2x from both sides: x − 6 = 5. Add 6 to both sides: x = 11. Check: left side 3(11 − 2) = 27; right side 2(11) + 5 = 27. Both sides equal, so x = 11 is correct."

The mathematics is identical. The second response lets an assessor see every decision, and the check shows the student has taken responsibility for the answer. The habits the stronger response shows are: define what is being done at each step, use correct notation, and verify.

### Habits to train

- **Speak before writing.** Explain the next step aloud, then write it.
- **Define variables** at the start of any applied problem.
- **Label** graphs, tables and diagrams.
- **Use a consistent layout**: one idea per line, equals signs aligned.
- **State conclusions** in a sentence.

Within a few weeks, the habit becomes automatic, and the effect on Criterion C is usually visible on the next assessed task. ## Weeks 9 and 10: Criterion D, Applying Mathematics to Real Situations

Criterion D asks the student to take a real situation, choose a mathematical model, solve, interpret the result and evaluate it. Students who are secure on procedure often lose marks here because they jump straight to calculating.

### A worked example

Two taxi firms charge differently. Firm X charges a fixed fee of 40 units plus 12 units per kilometre. Firm Y charges 100 units plus 7 units per kilometre. When is Firm X cheaper?

A strong response follows a chain.

1. **Define:** let k be the distance in kilometres. Cost X = 40 + 12k, Cost Y = 100 + 7k.
2. **Model and solve:** the firms cost the same when 40 + 12k = 100 + 7k, so 5k = 60 and k = 12.
3. **Interpret:** at 12 km both cost 184 units. For distances shorter than 12 km, Firm X is cheaper, because its lower fixed fee outweighs its higher rate. For longer distances, Firm Y is cheaper.
4. **Evaluate:** the model assumes the charge is exactly linear in distance and ignores waiting time, tolls and minimum fares. A real fare may differ.

The fourth step is the one most students omit, and it is the step that distinguishes a high-level response. It requires the student to say something about the limits of the model.

### Practice that builds it

- **Rotate contexts**: finance, sport, transport, environment, technology.
- **Insist on the chain**: define, model, solve, interpret, evaluate.
- **Vary the representation**: ask the student to show the same situation as a table, a graph and an equation, and to say which is most useful.
- **Allow messy data** once in a while, and discuss what can and cannot be concluded.

The routines for unfamiliar problems, and how a tutor gradually withdraws support, are covered in [MYP maths problem solving](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/).

## A Weekly Rhythm That Fits a School Week

The plan only works if it fits into a week that already has school, sport and other subjects. A realistic rhythm uses about three to four hours of maths a week outside school, of which one or two are with a tutor, if the family uses one.

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Short focus drill on the target criterion | 30 minutes |
| Tuesday | Session with tutor or parent: new idea, student writes | 60 minutes |
| Thursday | Mixed practice, including one unfamiliar question | 40 minutes |
| Friday | Error log review and one written-up solution | 30 minutes |
| Weekend | One investigation or applied task, written up | 40 to 50 minutes |

Short, frequent work beats a long weekend session. If a week is disrupted by exams, drop the weekend task, not the error log.

## Weeks 11 and 12: Review and Reset

The last fortnight is for measuring, not for cramming.

**Reassess.** Repeat the diagnostic from weeks 1 and 2 with fresh tasks of the same type. Compare how the student handles the pattern task, the written solution and the contextual problem against the baseline.

**Compare with school evidence.** If an assessed task has been marked during the 12 weeks, look at the criterion levels and the teacher's comments. One task is not a trend, but it is a data point.

**Update the targets.** Some criteria will have moved and others will not. Reset the plan: continue what works, replace what does not.

**Decide the next cycle.** Options include another 12 weeks aimed at the next weakest criterion, a lighter maintenance schedule, or preparation for a specific assessment, such as MYP 5 tasks and, where the school offers it, the eAssessment.

:::steps The review at week 12
Retest | Repeat the diagnostic tasks
Compare | Baseline against week 12 work
Check | Marked school tasks and teacher comments
Reset | Targets and plan for the next cycle
:::

Be honest if little has changed. A lack of movement in twelve weeks is information. It can mean the diagnosis was off, the practice was not aimed at the gap, the student was not doing the between-lesson work, or the match with a tutor is not right. Each has a different remedy. The worst response is to continue unchanged.

## How Long Does It Take to Improve IB MYP Mathematics Performance?

Families who want to improve IB MYP mathematics performance often ask this first. Visible change in how a student works, such as writing full solutions or attempting open questions, often appears in three to four weeks. Change in assessed criterion levels usually takes one or two assessment cycles, because the school marks a task only now and then. Twelve weeks is a fair window to judge a plan, and no plan can promise a result.

Some changes are quick. Criterion C habits, such as writing steps and checking, can show within a couple of weeks. Others are slower. Criterion B skills build over months, and Criterion A repairs depend on how deep the foundation gap runs. A family that measures at week 4 should look at the student's behaviour, not the grade. Is the student attempting questions they would have skipped? Are the solutions longer and clearer? Is the error log getting shorter? Those are the early signs.

## Standard or Extended: Does It Change the Plan?

The plan is the same, the content differs. A student in extended mathematics meets additional topics, such as more advanced algebra and trigonometry, and the pattern tasks and applications use those topics. The criteria and the way marks are earned do not change.

For students heading toward DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches, the algebraic fluency from Criterion A, the justification habits from B and the communication from C all carry forward. ## Concept First: The Sequence Behind the Plan

The method that helps most students improve IB MYP mathematics performance is a simple order of learning that Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, uses with MYP students: **Concept, Pattern, Application, Expression.** The concept establishes understanding. The pattern builds recognition. The application builds flexibility in new contexts. The expression builds the ability to communicate it. Each of the four criteria lines up with one of these steps, which is why the plan can be run criterion by criterion.

The approach begins with a diagnosis of the individual student, not a fixed worksheet. A student who says "I used this formula because it looked right" is showing a method-selection problem. A student who says "I used this equation because the rate of change is constant" is showing conceptual reasoning to build on. The tutor listens for these differences and designs the next lesson around them, then gradually reduces support so the student can start, plan and check alone. The goal is a student who needs the tutor less over time.

## When a Tutor Adds Value and When a Family Can Run This Alone

A family can run the first two weeks and most of the plan without a tutor if a parent or older sibling is comfortable with MYP-level mathematics and can ask good questions. The diagnostic, the error log and the weekly rhythm do not need an expert.

A tutor adds the most value in three places: finding the real diagnosis when the family is guessing, designing varied practice for Criteria B and D, which are hard to invent from scratch, and giving feedback on written reasoning that is specific enough to act on. A tutor also provides accountability. A weekly meeting with someone who has read last week's work keeps a plan alive.

A tutor adds the least when the main problem is not enough time, since lessons add to a full week, or when the student is not doing the work between sessions. In those cases, fix the schedule first. ## The Parent's Role Across the 12 Weeks

Parents are the plan's project managers, not its teachers.

- **Hold the calendar.** Know the assessment dates and the weekly rhythm.
- **Ask about process.** "How did you decide to start that question?" does more than "did you finish?"
- **Read the error log** once a week, not to criticise but to see what is recurring.
- **Protect the time.** A fixed slot with a clear desk and no phone beats an open-ended promise.
- **Keep a steady tone.** A student who is shown a difficult question and not rescued learns to try. A student who is rescued each time learns to wait.

Resist the temptation to check every answer. If a parent corrects every error, the student never builds the habit of checking their own work, which is the habit that matters most in MYP assessment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I improve my child's IB MYP maths grade quickly?

Find the criterion with the lowest level, practise the skill it measures, and keep an error log. For most students, communication and real-life application respond fastest. Expect early signs in work habits within a few weeks and changes in assessed levels over one or two assessment cycles. No plan can guarantee a particular grade.

### Why is my child strong in tests but weak in MYP investigations?

Tests reward procedure on familiar question types, while investigations reward exploring a pattern, stating a rule and justifying it. A student trained to ask "which formula?" struggles when none is given. Practise one open investigation a week, asking "what is changing, what stays the same?" and require a general rule and a check.

### What is a good Criterion D practice routine?

Use one applied problem a week from a varied context, and require five steps: define variables, build the model, solve, interpret in context, and evaluate the model's limits. The last step is where most marks are lost. Ask the student to say what the model ignores, such as waiting time in a fare problem.

### Should my child take extended mathematics?

It depends on the student, the school and the DP route intended. Extended covers additional topics that prepare students for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches, but the school decides placement. Ask the mathematics teacher whether your child is ready, and look at foundation strength in algebra before pushing for extended.

### How much tutoring does an MYP student need?

Many students benefit from one lesson a week plus short independent practice, with more in the weeks before an assessment. Total time matters less than whether practice targets the weak criterion. Review after four weeks: if nothing in the student's working habits has changed, adjust the plan or the match.

### Is MYP eAssessment compulsory?

No. MYP eAssessment in MYP 5, with on-screen examinations and ePortfolios, is optional, and each school decides whether to offer it. Ask your child's school what applies. Where it is offered, practise the digital environment after the mathematics is secure. The preparation is covered in the [MYP 5 eAssessment preparation guide](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/).

### My child's grade has not changed after a month of tutoring. What now?

Check the diagnosis first: is the practice aimed at the criterion that is costing marks? Then check whether work is happening between sessions, and whether the error log is being used. One month is often too short to see an assessed change, but working habits should have shifted. If nothing has changed, ask for a different match.

### Can online lessons work for MYP maths?

Yes, especially for MYP 4 and 5, with a pen tablet and shared board so the student writes. Younger students need shorter sessions and a parent nearby. If your child drifts on screens, a home lesson may suit better. Many families combine both across the year depending on the week.

### How do I know if the problem is understanding or exam technique?

Ask the student to solve a question aloud with no time pressure. If the reasoning is sound but the written answer is thin, the issue is communication. If the reasoning is unsure, it is understanding. If it is sound untimed but slips under a clock, it is time management. Each needs a different fix.
