A good IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan does three jobs in order: it finds what is broken, repairs it in topic blocks, then converts the repaired knowledge into marks under timed conditions. Twelve weeks is enough for that sequence if the student starts honestly and keeps an error log. It is not enough if the weeks are spent re-reading the textbook and hoping.

This plan is for students sitting Cambridge International Education's International General Certificate of Secondary Education (IGCSE) Mathematics 0580, at either the Core or Extended tier, who have about three months before the exam series. It is also written for parents who want to know what a sensible revision structure looks like so they can check it is happening. This IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan covers Mathematics only. If you need a plan across several subjects, the [six-week IGCSE revision plan](/blog/igcse-revision-six-week-plan/) is built for that.

The plan is organised the way a good tutor would organise it: a diagnostic week, four weeks of topic blocks, four weeks that move toward past papers, two weeks of mixed timed work, and a final fortnight that is deliberately lighter on new material. Each stage explains what to do, how to know it worked, and what to change if it did not. No plan guarantees a grade; a plan only makes the work you do count for more.

Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, shapes the approach described in the section on how the plan is coached: diagnosis first, an error-correction system, and exam-technique training. Everything below can be run at home without a tutor, and the section near the end says when outside help genuinely adds value.

## What Does the 0580 Exam Actually Look Like?

Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 is taken at two tiers. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Core grades top out at C, and Extended reaches A*. Cambridge examines in three series: February/March (a series offered only in India), May/June and October/November. Schools decide which series a student enters, so your child's date comes from the school.

Cambridge revises its syllabuses, so confirm the current version and the exact paper durations and weightings from [Cambridge International Education](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/) or the school before you fix any timing. The syllabus content areas are stable enough to plan around: Number, Algebra and graphs, Coordinate geometry, Geometry, Mensuration, Trigonometry, Transformations and vectors, Probability and Statistics.

:::stats IGCSE Maths 0580 at a glance
2 | Tiers: Core and Extended
2 | Papers per student, one without a calculator
A* | Top grade, Extended tier only
:::

Notice what the paper pairing means for a plan. Every student sits one paper without a calculator and one with it. Roughly half of the preparation must therefore be done by hand: exact fractions, surds in Extended, algebra, and mental estimation. Students who revise entirely with a calculator in reach arrive at the non-calculator paper unprepared.

## Who Should Use a Twelve-Week Plan, and Who Should Not?

A twelve-week plan fits a student who has covered most of the syllabus at school and now needs to consolidate, or a student who is roughly one grade below where they want to finish. It works because twelve weeks is long enough to repair gaps and short enough that the exam feels real.

It fits less well in two cases. A student who has covered only half the syllabus needs a content-first plan, with the plan below stretched or the first four weeks used to teach new material. A student already scoring comfortably near their target needs less structure and more targeted practice on whatever the error log shows. If you are in the first case, the [IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/) explains the tiers and the decision about Additional Mathematics.

Before beginning, settle the tier. The wrong tier is the largest single threat to a plan. A student entered for Extended who is working at the Core boundary needs a conversation with the teacher, not just more practice; [why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics](/blog/igcse-mathematics-tutoring-guide/) covers tier mismatch and its signs.

## Week 1: Diagnose Before You Plan

The first week produces no progress in marks and all of the progress in direction. A student who skips it spends the first month revising what they already know.

1. Print the syllabus topic list from the current guide, or ask the school for it.
2. Mark each subtopic honestly as green (can do it cold), amber (can do it with a prompt) or red (cannot start).
3. Sit one past paper of the paper type the student will sit, under timed conditions, without help.
4. Mark it using the official markscheme and sort every lost mark into a reason: did not know it, knew it but chose the wrong method, calculation slip, did not show working, or ran out of time.
5. Write down the three red topics that carry the most marks. They go first.

Official past papers and markschemes are available through the school or Cambridge's own channels. Use papers matched to the student's current syllabus version; older syllabus papers can include content that is no longer examined or omit content that now is.

### What Honest Grading Looks Like

Students overrate topics they have seen and underrate topics they have not. A useful test: choose a topic and ask the student to explain it to someone, with an example, in two minutes. If they cannot, it is amber at best. A tutor or parent with the markscheme can run this test in ten minutes per topic.

## Weeks 2 to 5: Topic Blocks and the Order That Works

The topic blocks are four weeks, each focused on a cluster of content. The order matters. Algebra comes first because it contaminates everything else: a weak algebra base shows up in graphs, trigonometry, coordinate geometry and even statistics.

| Week | Block | Why this order |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Number and algebra basics | Fractions, percentages, indices, linear equations underlie everything |
| 3 | Algebra and graphs | Quadratics, simultaneous equations, sequences, graph sketching |
| 4 | Geometry, mensuration, trigonometry | Shape facts, area and volume, right-angled and non-right-angled trigonometry |
| 5 | Probability, statistics, vectors and transformations | Often shorter blocks, and a source of cheap marks |

Adjust the blocks to the diagnostic. If the first paper showed that geometry is the real weakness, move it earlier and shrink something safe.

### How a Single Topic Block Runs

Each topic follows the same mini-cycle, spread over three or four study sessions in the week:

- **Session one: understand.** Work two or three examples with every step written. Do not hurry.
- **Session two: practise.** Ten to fifteen questions, graded from easy to exam-style, without notes.
- **Session three: mark and log.** Mark against the markscheme and record each lost mark in the error log with a reason.
- **Session four: retest.** A short quiz of five or six questions on the same topic, including the types that went wrong.

The retest is the step students skip, and it is the one that proves repair has happened.

### Worked Example: Understanding a Linear Equation

Take 3x + 8 = 23. The student should understand that the same operation is applied to both sides: subtract 8 from each side to get 3x = 15, then divide by 3 to get x = 5. A student who memorises "move the 8 across and change the sign" can solve this and still fail at 3(x + 4) = 2x + 19, where the rule has nothing to attach to. The understanding is what carries.

### Worked Example: A Quadratic Factorised

Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0. The factors of 6 that add to −5 are −2 and −3, so (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, which gives x = 2 or x = 3. The point for revision is the check: substitute back, and see that both values make the original equation zero. A student who does this on every quadratic catches sign errors that otherwise cost marks quietly. On the Extended paper the same quadratic may reappear as a quadratic formula question or as part of a problem about areas, and the student who understands factors recognises it either way.

### Worked Example: Percentages in Five Disguises

Percentage questions appear as a percentage of a quantity, a percentage increase, a percentage decrease, a reverse percentage and repeated percentage change (compound growth). They look different and use one idea: multipliers. An increase of 12% is a multiplier of 1.12; a decrease of 12% is 0.88; a reverse percentage is a division by the multiplier, not a subtraction of the percentage. Students who learn the multiplier method once solve all five. Students who learn five separate methods mix them up under pressure.

## What Goes in the Mistake Notebook?

The mistake notebook records every lost mark as a row: question, topic, what went wrong, why, the correction and the action. It is the quiet engine of the plan. After three weeks, the same two or three entries repeat, and those repeats are the highest-value revision targets the student has.

One row might read: simultaneous equations; sign error when substituting; rushed, did two steps in one line; write each step on its own line; five similar questions on Friday. The "why" column matters most. "Careless" is not a reason. "Wrote the minus sign on the wrong side because I skipped the bracket" is.

The notebook has a second section for formulas that are not on the formula sheet, or that the student confuses. In the final fortnight, this section is the most valuable few pages the student owns. Cambridge's rules on what is provided in the exam can change, so check the current instructions to candidates for what is and is not given.

## Weeks 6 to 8: Mixing Topics and Meeting Full Papers

By week six the topic blocks are done and the danger changes. Students who can solve a quadratic when told "this is a quadratics question" often cannot recognise one inside a mixed paper. The next three weeks teach recognition.

### Week 6: Mixed Sets

Replace topic-by-topic questions with sets of ten to fifteen questions drawn from several topics. A student may do three algebra, three geometry, three number, two statistics. The skill being trained is deciding what a question is asking before choosing a method. A good habit is to write, in the margin, the topic and method in four words before starting: "right-angled triangle, sine ratio".

### Week 7: The First Full Paper

Sit a full paper, untimed or generously timed, and mark it properly. The purpose is to see the whole shape of the exam. Afterwards, update the topic colours and add the new entries to the error log. Compare against the diagnostic: which red topics are now green? If few have changed, the blocks were not working, and it is time to ask why before moving on.

### Week 8: Non-Calculator Week

Spend this week mostly on the non-calculator paper type. Revise exact values, fractions without a calculator, standard form, indices, expanding and factorising, and estimation. A calculator makes students lazy at arithmetic, and Paper 1 or Paper 2 punishes that. Short daily drills of ten minutes, done by hand, build fluency better than one long weekly session.

A student weak in this area can use the [IGCSE Maths 0580 A* strategy guide](/blog/how-to-score-a-star-in-igcse-maths-0580/), which looks at accuracy habits for the top grade, and [the ten commonest IGCSE maths mistakes](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/) for specific errors to look for.

## Weeks 9 and 10: Timed Papers and the Error Log Review

Weeks nine and ten are about conversion. The student now knows enough; the question is how much of that knowledge becomes marks in the time allowed.

### A Timed-Paper Routine

1. Sit the paper at a desk, phone away, within the time limit from the current syllabus.
2. Stop when time is up; mark further work in a different colour if the student wishes to finish.
3. Mark with the official markscheme the same day, with every method mark counted.
4. Record the score, the time spent per section and every lost mark in the error log.
5. Write a three-line summary: biggest loss, main cause, fix for next paper.

Two timed papers a week is a sensible load for most students, one with a calculator and one without. A student who is exhausted should do one and spend the other session on the error log. A paper marked carefully teaches more than two papers marked quickly.

For the question of how many papers and which ones, [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/) goes into marking, examiner reports and how to avoid memorising specific papers.

### Reading the Pattern

After three or four papers, ask:

- Which topics appear most often in the error log?
- Were the lost marks conceptual or careless?
- Did the student run out of time, and on which questions?
- Did working go missing on multi-mark questions?
- Were answers rounded to the stated accuracy, and were units included?

A student whose errors are mostly careless needs a checking routine, not more content. A student whose errors are conceptual needs to go back to the topic block and retest.

## Weeks 11 and 12: The Final Fortnight

The last two weeks are for accuracy and calm, not for new material. The danger is panic: students who sense the exam approaching try to learn everything again and exhaust themselves.

:::steps The final fortnight in four moves
Fix | Only recurring errors from the log
Time | One paper per paper type, early in the week
Check | Rehearse the final checking routine
Rest | Sleep, short sessions, light day before
:::

### Week 11

Run one full paper of each type under timed conditions, then spend the remaining sessions on the three or four topics that still appear in the error log. Revise the formula section of the mistake notebook. Do short mixed sets of ten questions in the evenings, not long marathons.

### Week 12

Keep sessions short, around an hour. Redo wrong questions from the error log without looking at the corrections, and see whether the method now comes automatically. Check the practical details: the exam date and time from the school, permitted equipment, calculator readiness and how the paper types are ordered. In the last two days, do no new papers. A rested student scores better than a worn-out one who did one more paper.

### A Final Checking Routine

Rehearse this until it is automatic, because it recovers marks that students otherwise give away:

- Check each calculation with a different method where possible, or estimate to see if the answer is sensible.
- Check signs.
- Check units and the requested degree of accuracy.
- Check that every question part has an answer.
- Check the final answer matches what was asked, such as "area" rather than "perimeter".

## Weekly Rhythm: What a Normal Week Looks Like

An IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan fails if it cannot survive school. This is a realistic weekly shape for a student who has several hours free a week alongside school and activities.

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | New or repair topic, worked examples | 60 minutes |
| Tuesday | Practice set on the topic | 45 minutes |
| Wednesday | Mark, log errors, correct | 45 minutes |
| Thursday | Retest and mixed set | 45 minutes |
| Saturday | Timed paper or half paper | 60-90 minutes |
| Sunday | Mark paper, review error log | 45 minutes |

Adjust the days to the school week; the pattern is what matters: learn, practise, mark, retest, then test under time. A student with less time should shorten each session and keep the order, rather than drop the marking step.

## How Does the Plan Change for Core and Extended Students?

The structure is identical; the content and the pace differ. Core students aim at grades up to C, so the plan emphasises accuracy on the core topics: number, basic algebra, shape and statistics. Extended students need the same base plus harder algebra, functions, further trigonometry, vectors and more demanding problem-solving, where the top grades are decided.

For Extended students aiming high, the topic blocks can be shortened and the mixed and timed phases lengthened, because the content is harder to recognise in a mixed paper. For Core students who may be moving up, spend more of weeks 2 to 5 on fluency in algebra, which usually decides whether a higher grade is realistic. A student unsure about the tier should ask the teacher before week 1, not in week 10.

Students who take Additional Mathematics 0606 alongside 0580 Extended, usually heading for IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches or A Level Mathematics, should keep the two syllabuses on separate schedules and let the 0606 content wait until 0580 is secure. The plan above is for 0580 alone.

## When Does a Tutor Add Value to a Plan Like This?

A student with a stable routine, a patient parent and access to past papers can run this plan alone. A tutor adds value in four places: the diagnostic, where an experienced eye finds in one paper what a student might take weeks to notice; the error log, where the question "why" needs a second pair of eyes; mixed practice, where choice of method is the skill; and timed-paper review, where marks-to-minutes habits are formed.

A tutor does not add value by doing the work. If the student is passive in sessions, the plan has failed regardless of the fee. ## What Goes Wrong With Plans Like This

Most failures of an IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan are predictable, and visible by week four.

**Skipping the diagnostic.** The student begins at the start of the textbook, spends a month on topics they know, and reaches week 9 with the weak topics untouched.

**Topic blocks without retests.** Practice without a retest feels like progress and proves nothing.

**Calculator dependence.** A plan that never practises the non-calculator paper by hand produces a student who is strong on one paper and weak on the other.

**Papers without marking.** Doing ten papers and marking none loses the learning. One marked paper beats three unmarked ones.

**Over-planning.** A fifteen-line timetable that cannot survive one school event will be abandoned by week three. Keep it simple enough to restart after a missed day.

**Starting new content in the final fortnight.** New content in the last two weeks usually displaces secure content. Revise; do not expand.

## What if There Are Fewer Than Twelve Weeks?

Compress honestly and keep the order. With eight weeks, cut the topic blocks to two or three weeks, concentrating on red topics only, and keep the mixed and timed phases at the same length, because those turn knowledge into marks. With six weeks, run a diagnostic in week one, one topic block of two weeks on the reddest areas, then three weeks of mixed and timed work and a short finish. The [six-week IGCSE revision plan](/blog/igcse-revision-six-week-plan/) is built for that scenario.

What cannot be compressed away is the error log and the marking. A shortened plan without them is just a shorter version of hoping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is twelve weeks enough to prepare for IGCSE Maths 0580?

Twelve weeks is enough for a student who has covered most of the syllabus and needs to consolidate, or one who is about a grade below target. It is not enough to learn the whole syllabus from scratch. The plan works because every week has a job: diagnose, repair, mix, time, and finish.

### How many hours a week does the plan need?

Most students need about five to seven hours a week alongside school: three or four short sessions for topic work and marking, and one longer session for a timed paper. Quality matters more than volume. A student who marks and logs every paper in five hours will progress faster than one who does ten unmarked hours.

### Should I start with past papers or with the topics?

Start with one past paper as a diagnostic in week one, then go to topics, then return to papers in weeks seven to ten. A paper at the start tells you where the gaps are; papers at the end convert repaired knowledge into marks. Doing papers without topic repair mostly records the same weaknesses repeatedly.

### How many past papers should my child do?

Quality beats quantity, but a typical plan includes roughly one full paper in week seven, then two a week through weeks nine to eleven, split between calculator and non-calculator types. Each must be marked with the official markscheme and logged. Ten carefully marked papers are worth more than twenty skimmed ones.

### What if my child is still weak in algebra by week five?

Extend the algebra work and shorten something that is already secure. Algebra underlies graphs, geometry and trigonometry, so leaving it weak limits every other topic. Use short daily hand-worked drills on expanding, factorising and solving, and retest weekly. If progress stalls, a tutor's diagnostic can find the specific habit causing the errors.

### Does the plan work for the Core tier?

Yes. The structure is the same, but the emphasis shifts to accuracy and fluency on core topics: number, basic algebra, shape, statistics and probability. Core grades top out at C, so the aim is to secure every mark in reach. If the student may be able to move up to Extended, speak to the teacher early.

### Should I use a calculator for revision?

Use it only on the calculator paper type and learn it properly, since fluent use saves time. Practise the non-calculator paper type by hand, with exact fractions, standard form and algebra. Students who revise everything with a calculator lose marks on the other paper. Check the current Cambridge rules for approved calculators.

### How do I stop my child panicking in the last fortnight?

Keep sessions short, avoid new material and give the final checking routine plenty of rehearsal. Panic usually comes from vague worry about the unknown, so a clear list of what has been fixed helps. The error log, with its crossed-off entries, is evidence of progress a student can see.

### Can this plan be used for the February/March series?

Yes, the plan is series-independent. Count back twelve weeks from the school's confirmed exam date and begin there. Schools decide which series a student sits, so confirm the date with the school. If the gap is shorter, compress using the guidance above and keep the marking and error log intact.

### What if my child stops following the plan in the middle?

Restart from where you are, not from the beginning. Re-sort the topic colours, check the error log and choose the next block. A broken plan is usually a plan that was too heavy for the week. Shorten sessions and keep the order of learn, practise, mark and retest, which matters more than the exact days.
