Concept-first MYP maths tutoring starts from a simple rule: a student should be able to say what an idea means, draw it, and test it, before they are drilled on the method that uses it. A child who knows only the procedure for solving a quadratic can solve the quadratics they have seen. A child who understands what a quadratic describes can handle one that arrives in a new shape, a new context or a new question.

This guide is for parents and students in Gurgaon, including families whose children attend schools such as Pathways and Heritage, who have noticed the pattern: "She understands in class but struggles when the question is unfamiliar." It explains the idea in plain terms, ties it to the Middle Years Programme (MYP) key and related concepts, shows through worked contrasts what a procedural approach and a concept-first approach actually do to the same problem, describes how a session is structured, and traces the pay-off into the Diploma Programme (DP). It also says honestly when drilling is the right call, because concept-first does not mean practice-free.

## What is concept-first MYP maths tutoring?

Concept-first MYP maths tutoring is a teaching approach in which the tutor establishes what a mathematical idea means, how it can be represented and why it works, and only then practises the procedure that uses it. The student moves from idea to representation to method to application. The aim is a student who can choose and adapt methods for problems they have not seen before, not just repeat methods for problems they have.

Three habits mark it out in a session.

1. **The tutor asks before telling.** "What do you notice?" comes before "Here is the rule."
2. **Every idea is shown in more than one form.** A relationship is seen as a table, a graph, an equation and a sentence, and the student moves between them.
3. **Methods are earned.** The student sees why a method is natural before being asked to memorise it.

None of this replaces practice. A student who understands what a fraction division means still needs to do forty of them to be fluent. The point is the order. Understanding first makes practice faster and more durable, because there is something for the practice to attach to.

### A short definition worth quoting

Procedural learning is knowing steps. Conceptual learning is knowing why the steps work and when they apply. A student needs both, and the weaker partner usually decides the grade.

## Why does MYP maths reward understanding over procedure?

MYP maths rewards understanding because it is assessed against four criteria, three of which cannot be met by recalling a procedure. Each criterion is scored 0 to 8, the total out of 32 converts to a final grade from 1 to 7, and only Criterion A (Knowing and understanding) resembles a conventional test of method. Criterion B (Investigating patterns), Criterion C (Communicating) and Criterion D (Applying mathematics in real-life contexts) ask the student to think, explain and decide.

That shape explains a common report. A child scores comfortably on routine tests, then gets a lower level on an investigation, because the investigation asks for a conjecture, a test of it and a justification, none of which a memorised procedure supplies.

## How do the MYP key and related concepts fit in?

The MYP organises each subject around key concepts and related concepts, which give a unit its conceptual focus. In mathematics the key concepts are form, logic and relationships. Related concepts are narrower ideas that a unit draws on, and commonly seen examples include equivalence, generalization, models, patterns, quantity, representation, simplification, space and systems. Each school chooses which ones its units emphasise and records them in the unit planner, so check your child's planner for the exact list.

A tutor working concept-first uses these as a map of what to teach and how to link ideas, not as vocabulary to recite.

| Key concept | What it asks the student to see | A small example |
|---|---|---|
| Form | The shape or structure of a mathematical object | A quadratic is a curve with a turning point, not just "x squared" |
| Logic | Why a conclusion must follow | If two angles on a line add to 180, the missing one is forced |
| Relationships | How quantities depend on each other | Doubling the radius changes area by a factor of four, not two |

### Related concepts in practice

A related concept such as **equivalence** shows up when a student learns that 3(x + 2) and 3x + 6 are the same thing written two ways, and that the choice between them depends on the task. **Generalization** shows up when a student moves from "the 10th term is 31" to "the nth term is 3n + 1". **Models** appear when a real situation is described by an equation, and **representation** when the same relationship is shown as a graph and a table.

The practical point for a parent: if the unit planner says the related concept is generalization, expect the next assessment to ask for a rule, an explanation of why it works, and a test on a new case. A tutor who knows that can prepare the student for exactly that.

## Procedure versus concept: five worked contrasts

The difference is easiest to see in the same problem handled two ways. In each contrast below, the procedural student gets a correct answer to the familiar question and stalls on the variation.

### Contrast 1: dividing by a fraction

**The procedure.** "To divide by a fraction, flip it and multiply." So 6 ÷ 1/2 becomes 6 × 2 = 12.

**The concept.** Dividing 6 by 1/2 asks: how many halves fit into 6? Each whole holds two halves, so six wholes hold twelve. The student can see that dividing by a number smaller than 1 makes the result bigger, and why flipping works: dividing by 1/2 is the same as asking how many pieces of size 1/2 fit.

**The variation.** "A ribbon 3/4 m long is cut into pieces of 3/16 m. How many pieces?" The procedural student sometimes multiplies by mistake because the numbers look similar. The concept-first student asks how many 3/16s fit into 3/4, estimates that 3/16 is a quarter of a metre or a bit less, so the answer should be a bit more than 3 and finds 4. The estimate catches the error before it is written.

### Contrast 2: area and perimeter of a scaled shape

**The procedure.** Area of a rectangle is length times width; perimeter is twice the sum.

**The concept.** Area measures covering, perimeter measures the boundary, and they scale differently. If every side doubles, the perimeter doubles, but the area grows by 2 × 2 = 4 because the shape now covers four copies of the original.

**The variation.** "A garden's sides are all scaled by 3. By what factor does the area change?" The procedural student tries numbers. The concept-first student says nine, because area scales with the square of the scale factor, and can draw a 3 by 3 grid of copies to show why.

### Contrast 3: solving a quadratic

**The procedure.** Substitute into the formula, simplify, get two values.

**The concept.** Solving x² - 5x + 6 = 0 asks: where does the graph of y = x² - 5x + 6 cross the x-axis? The expression factorises as (x - 2)(x - 3), so the graph crosses at 2 and 3. If a product is zero, one factor must be zero, which is the logic behind the method. The turning point sits midway between the roots, at x = 2.5, which gives the student a way to check the work.

**The variation.** "The path of a ball is h = -t² + 4t. When is the ball at ground level?" The procedural student may stall because there is no constant term. The concept-first student sees that h = 0 gives t(4 - t) = 0, so t = 0 (launch) and t = 4 (landing), and knows that the peak is at t = 2 by symmetry. The same understanding answers three questions.

### Contrast 4: linear relationships

**The procedure.** y = mx + c, so find m and c.

**The concept.** m is the change in y for each step of 1 in x, and c is the value of y when x is 0. In a taxi fare of a fixed charge plus a rate per kilometre, c is the fixed charge and m the per-kilometre rate. The student can read both off a table without a formula.

**The variation.** "A table shows 2 km costs 70 and 5 km costs 130." The procedural student plugs two points into simultaneous equations, correctly but slowly. The concept-first student notes that 3 extra kilometres cost 60 more, so each kilometre costs 20, and 2 km at 20 is 40 so the fixed charge is 30. The rule is y = 20x + 30, found in a few seconds, and the student can explain what each part means in context, which is exactly what Criterion D rewards.

### Contrast 5: percentage change

**The procedure.** Multiply by 1.2 to increase by 20%, by 0.8 to decrease by 20%.

**The concept.** A percentage change is a multiplier because the new amount is a fraction of the old one. An increase of 20% means the new value is 120% of the old one, or 1.2 times it. This also explains why a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the start: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, so there is a 4% net fall.

**The variation.** "A price rises by 25%. By what percentage must it fall to return to the original?" The procedural student often says 25%. The concept-first student reasons: the multiplier is 1.25, so the reverse multiplier is 1 ÷ 1.25 = 0.8, a fall of 20%.

## What does a concept-first session actually look like?

A concept-first session usually runs through four moves, and the proportion of each shifts with the student's stage.

:::steps A concept-first session in four moves
Probe | Ask what the student notices or expects
Represent | Show the idea as table, graph, words, symbols
Method | Derive the method from the representation
Apply | Variations, new contexts, written explanation
:::

### An example session: introducing the idea of a nth-term rule

The student is given the sequence 5, 8, 11, 14 and asked to continue it, then to say what changes and what stays the same. The tutor asks for the 10th term without writing the whole list. The student notices the constant difference of 3, builds a table, and plots the points to see they lie on a straight line. Only then does the tutor write "term = 3n + 2" and ask where the 3 and the 2 come from in the table and the graph.

Next come variations: a sequence whose differences are not constant (1, 4, 9, 16), where the student notices the differences themselves change by a constant, a first look at quadratic structure. The session ends with a short written explanation: "Why does the rule 3n + 2 work for every term?" That sentence is Criterion B and C work in miniature.

### Where drilling still belongs

Fluency needs repetition. Number facts, algebraic manipulation, fraction arithmetic and index laws all need regular, short practice until they are automatic. A student who is still slow at expanding brackets cannot spend working memory on the idea in a harder problem. The sensible pattern is a short fluency drill at the start or end of a session, with the main time given to understanding and application.

A concept-first tutor who never drills is as limited as a drill-only tutor who never explains. For the fuller discussion of what a good plan looks like, see [how to improve IB MYP mathematics performance](/blog/improve-ib-myp-mathematics-performance-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/).

## How does concept-first teaching help each criterion?

The approach is not a general philosophy; it targets each criterion in a particular way.

| Criterion | Concept-first move | What changes in the student's work |
|---|---|---|
| A: Knowing and understanding | Link each method to its meaning; estimate before calculating | Fewer wrong-method errors; answers checked for sense |
| B: Investigating patterns | Observe, conjecture, test, explain | Rules stated and justified, not just found |
| C: Communicating | Verbalise reasoning, define variables, state conclusions | Working that another reader can follow |
| D: Applying in real-life contexts | Decide what matters, model, interpret, check | Choice of mathematics is explained, results are judged |

Criterion B is where concept-first is most visible. The reasoning skills for investigations are covered in depth in the [guide to MYP reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/), and Criterion D in the [problem solving guide](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/).

### An illustration of one task across the criteria

Consider a task: "Investigate how the number of handshakes changes as more people join a meeting." A procedural student lists the cases for 2, 3, 4 and 5 people, finds 1, 3, 6, 10, and guesses the next. A concept-first student sees that each new person shakes hands with everyone already there, so the number added each time is one more than before, builds the rule n(n - 1)/2 and justifies it by saying each of n people shakes hands with n - 1 others, but each handshake is counted twice. Criterion B is the pattern and rule; Criterion C is the clear explanation; Criterion A is the correct use of the formula; Criterion D could extend to a seating or networking context. One understanding serves all four.

## What does the pay-off look like in the Diploma Programme?

The pay-off appears in DP1, when the pace and abstraction rise and procedural habits start to fail. DP Mathematics comes in two subjects: Analysis and Approaches (AA), which is algebraic and proof-heavy, and Applications and Interpretation (AI), which is modelling and statistics-heavy with technology throughout. Both reward a student who can choose a method, justify it and notice when an answer is unreasonable.

### What carries over

- **Estimation and sense-checking** become the habit that saves marks in a no-calculator paper.
- **Multiple representations** become fluency in moving between a function, its graph and its table, essential in AA calculus and AI modelling.
- **Justification** becomes the proof and reasoning marks in AA, and the interpretation in context marks in AI.
- **Conjecture and test** becomes the skill behind unfamiliar-question training and the longer Paper 3 problems at Higher Level.
- **Communication** becomes method marks and the Mathematical communication criterion in the internal exploration.

The mathematics exploration is the same task for AA and AI, SL and HL: 20% of the final grade, 12 to 20 pages, marked out of 20 on five criteria (Presentation, Mathematical communication, Personal engagement, Reflection and Use of mathematics). A student who has practised explaining and justifying since MYP 3 finds it more natural to write.

### What does not carry over

A habit of expecting marks for effort does not carry over; DP papers mark steps and accuracy. A student also has to learn to be fast, which MYP rarely demands. The bridge is a deliberate step in MYP 5 and the summer after it. The [MYP 4 and MYP 5 tutoring guide](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) covers standard and extended pathways and the AA or AI decision, and the guide on [choosing one tutor from MYP into DP](/blog/how-to-choose-ib-maths-tutor-pathways-student-gurgaon/) deals with continuity across the boundary.

DP2 | Paper 3 style problems and a confident exploration

## Is concept-first right for every student?

Mostly, but the balance changes. Some students need more of one element than another, and a good tutor adjusts.

### The student with weak foundations

A student who cannot multiply fractions or expand brackets accurately needs fluency work before conceptual extension. Start with the concept for each foundation skill (what a fraction product means, what expanding does) and then drill. Skipping the drill leaves the idea floating.

### The student who is strong and bored

A student who is strong on routine questions benefits most from conceptual depth, because it exposes the thinking behind the method and prepares for the DP. Give harder investigations and ask for explanations in words.

### The student close to an assessment

Two weeks before a test, the balance moves toward practice on the unit's likely tasks, with concept used to repair specific errors. A tutor should say so openly. Concept-first is a long-run strategy that also shows results in the short run, but it is not a substitute for rehearsing the task type.

### The student with anxiety

Understanding reduces anxiety, because there is less to memorise and more to reason from. But a worried student also needs short, achievable wins early on. Start with a concept they can master in one session. For the confidence side of the work, see the [guide to MYP maths exam confidence](/blog/ajay-vatsyayan-sir-ib-myp-mathematics-exam-confidence-gurgaon/).

## Common parent questions about the approach

### "Will it slow my child down?"

Slightly at first, then it speeds up. A student who understands why a method works needs fewer repetitions and makes fewer silly errors. The early weeks can feel slower because the tutor asks questions and waits, but the habit pays back within a unit or two.

### "How is this different from what school teaches?"

The MYP is itself concept-driven, so good schools teach this way. The difference in tutoring is the focus on the individual student: finding the specific idea that did not land, reteaching it in a different form, and checking by variation. Class time often cannot do that for each child.

### "How will I know it is working?"

Look at three signs: your child can explain a method in their own words, they can handle a variation of a question they have seen, and the errors in their error log change from "did not know" to smaller slips. If after four weeks none of these has moved, change the plan.

## A short plan a family can try this month

You can test the approach at home, even before booking a tutor.

1. Choose one idea from your child's current unit, for example linear relationships.
2. Ask your child to show it three ways: a table, a graph, a sentence.
3. Ask them to explain why the rule works and to invent a question about it.
4. Give a variation with a new context and see whether they adapt.
5. Note where they hesitate: that is the idea to reteach.

If the hesitation is frequent or the gap is hard to name, that is the moment a tutor adds value.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is concept-first MYP maths tutoring?

It is a teaching approach that builds understanding of an idea, its meaning and its representations, before drilling the method that uses it. The student moves from idea to representation to method to application. The goal is the ability to handle unfamiliar questions, which is what MYP Criteria B, C and D reward. Practice still matters; it follows understanding.

### Is concept-first the same as no drilling?

No. Fluency in number, algebra and fractions needs regular repetition, and a student who is slow at basics cannot think about the harder idea. The sensible pattern is short fluency drills plus the main session time on understanding and application. A tutor who never drills, or one who only drills, will each leave a gap.

### How do the MYP key concepts relate to what my child is tested on?

The key concepts for mathematics are form, logic and relationships, and related concepts such as equivalence, generalization and models give each unit its focus. Tasks are assessed on Criteria A to D. Check your child's unit planner for the concepts the school emphasises, since each school chooses them, and ask the tutor to teach to those.

### Will concept-first help with MYP eAssessment?

Where a school offers MYP eAssessment in MYP 5 (it is optional, and each school decides), the on-screen tasks include unfamiliar problems where understanding matters. The approach helps, but a student should also practise the digital format and timing. The [MYP 5 eAssessment preparation guide](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/) covers format and planning.

### My child is strong at routine questions but loses marks on investigations. Why?

Routine questions reward remembered procedures, while investigations ask for observation, a conjecture, a test and a justification. A student who has only practised procedures can find that unfamiliar. Concept-first work trains the missing steps: noticing, generalising and explaining. The [guide to moving from a 5 to a 6 or 7](/blog/how-can-an-ib-myp-maths-student-move-from-5-to-6-or-7/) shows which criteria move fastest.

### Does concept-first teaching help with the move to DP maths?

Yes, because DP rewards choosing a method, justifying it and noticing unreasonable answers. Estimation, multiple representations and justification carry directly into AA and AI. It does not replace the speed and accuracy DP papers demand, so the MYP 5 year and the summer after it should include timed practice as well.

### How is this different from CBSE-style maths teaching?

The two differ in emphasis: MYP assesses investigation, communication and application as well as knowledge, and reports criterion levels. A student switching from a more procedural system needs time to adapt. The comparison is covered in [IB MYP maths vs CBSE maths](/blog/ib-myp-maths-vs-cbse-maths-teaching-approach/).

### How long before I see a change?

Habit-level changes, such as estimating before calculating or explaining a step in words, often appear within three or four weeks. Conceptual gaps in a topic take longer, sometimes a unit. Review the error log and your child's explanations after four weeks; if nothing has moved, ask the tutor to change the plan.

### Can a tutor guarantee a higher criterion level?

No tutor can guarantee a grade or a criterion level. A tutor can diagnose, teach and train habits, but the student does the work and the school assesses it. Judge a tutor by method: diagnosis from real tasks, a clear plan, variations to test understanding and honest reporting of what changed.

### Do I need a tutor, or can I do this at home?

You can try the plan above at home. A tutor adds value when the gap is hard to name, when you lack the time or the maths, or when your child responds better to someone outside the family. If your child is coping and their explanations are improving, extra sessions may add little.
