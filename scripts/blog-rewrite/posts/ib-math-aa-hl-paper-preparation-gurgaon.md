IB Math AA HL paper preparation works when you stop treating the three papers as one big exam and give each its own habits. Paper 1 is about hand fluency with no calculator. Paper 2 is about choosing between algebra and technology, then interpreting the result. Paper 3 is about staying calm inside an unfamiliar, scaffolded problem. A student who revises "everything" the same way for all three usually under-trains the paper that decides the grade.

This guide is for Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches (AA) Higher Level (HL) students in DP1 and DP2, and for Gurgaon parents trying to understand where marks are really lost. It gives a question-style profile, a timing plan, a set of drills and a weekly preparation block for each paper, then shows how to combine them in the last months. The overall system for a 7 sits in the flagship guide on [how to score a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/). This post goes deeper on the papers themselves. Topic-by-topic content is in the [topic playbook for AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-a-7-in-ib-maths-aa-hl-step-by-step-strategy/), and the two-year calendar is in [how to prepare for IB DP Mathematics AA HL](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-ib-dp-mathematics-aa-hl/).

## How is IB Math AA HL assessed across the three papers?

IB Math AA HL is assessed by three external papers and one internal assessment. Paper 1 (no calculator) and Paper 2 (graphic display calculator, GDC, required) are 30% each and run 120 minutes for 110 marks. Paper 3 is 20% and consists of extended, scaffolded problems in unfamiliar settings. The mathematics exploration, the Internal Assessment (IA), is 20%. The exams together are 80% of the grade.

:::bars Weightings in IB Math AA HL
Paper 1 (no calculator) | 30 | 30%
Paper 2 (GDC required) | 30 | 30%
Paper 3 (extended problems) | 20 | 20%
Internal Assessment | 20 | 20%
The exams are 80% of the grade. Check the current subject guide for exact durations and mark totals.
:::

Notice the arithmetic. Paper 3 is the smallest exam but carries a fifth of the grade, and it is the one with the fewest past papers to practise on and the least resemblance to anything taught in class. That combination is why it gets neglected, and why the neglect is expensive. The fraction of the course examined on each paper also overlaps: calculus, algebra, functions and vectors can appear in all three. What differs is the *demand* each paper places on you.

| Paper | Tool | What it really tests | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | No calculator | Exact manipulation, written method, stamina | Arithmetic slips and skipped working |
| Paper 2 | GDC | Modelling, technology judgement, interpretation | Giving numbers without meaning, or over-using the GDC |
| Paper 3 | GDC | Reasoning through an unfamiliar scaffold | Abandoning later parts when an early part is missed |

The question "which paper is my weakest?" is therefore the first question for any IB Math AA HL paper preparation plan, and it can only be answered with timed, marked evidence, not with a feeling.

## What should come first in IB Math AA HL paper preparation?

Before paper-specific practice, run one baseline: sit a full past paper under exam conditions, mark it strictly against the official markscheme, and log every lost mark with a cause. Without that evidence, you are guessing whether your weakness is content, speed, notation or nerves, and the fix for each is different.

This is the step most students skip, because it is uncomfortable. They keep doing topic questions where they feel competent. A baseline takes one three-hour afternoon and saves weeks of misdirected effort.

1. **Choose a recent past paper** from your school's resources or the IB's official channels. Use the correct tool: no calculator for Paper 1, your exam GDC for Paper 2.
2. **Sit it timed**, in one go, at a desk, with only the permitted items. Do not pause the clock for a hard question.
3. **Mark it only against the markscheme.** Award a mark only where the scheme lists it. Write the mark you earned, not the mark you "nearly" earned.
4. **Classify every lost mark** into one of five causes: did not know it, knew it but could not start, started but made an algebra slip, lost the method marks through missing working, or ran out of time.
5. **Count the causes.** The largest bucket is your first training target for that paper.

The five-bucket log is the same instrument described in the flagship guide's error-log system. The difference here is that you keep one log *per paper*, because the dominant cause often differs. A student whose Paper 1 losses are mostly algebra slips may find that Paper 3 losses are mostly "could not start", and these need opposite training.

## How do you prepare for IB Math AA HL Paper 1 without a calculator?

Prepare for Paper 1 by building exact, written, step-by-step fluency: surds, logs, trigonometric exact values, differentiation and integration rules, and algebraic manipulation that fire without a calculator. Practise in timed sets, show every method line, and keep answers exact until the question asks for a decimal. The paper is a test of control, so the training is repetition under mild time pressure.

### Question styles you will meet

Paper 1 questions fall into a few recurring shapes, and naming them helps you recognise them in the hall.

- **Short, multi-step algebra.** Solve an equation or inequality involving logs, exponentials or a modulus, with an exact answer. The question looks small and hides three steps.
- **Exact trigonometry.** Use identities, special angles and the unit circle to evaluate or solve, then give solutions in a stated interval.
- **Sequences, series and the binomial theorem.** Find a term, a coefficient or a sum, sometimes with a condition for convergence.
- **Complex numbers.** Polar and Cartesian form, De Moivre's theorem, roots, sometimes with a geometric reading.
- **Calculus by hand.** Differentiate or integrate using chain rule, product rule, parts or substitution; find a stationary point; compute an area exactly.
- **Vectors.** Lines, planes, scalar and vector products, with a geometric conclusion.
- **Proof.** Induction, direct proof or contradiction, where the layout and logic are marked as well as the algebra.
- **Longer structured questions.** Several connected parts that build towards one result, where a slip in part (a) can echo downstream.

### A timing plan that matches the marks

Both Paper 1 and Paper 2 give 120 minutes for 110 marks, about 1.1 minutes a mark. That is a *budget*, not a pace to hold on every question. Use it as three passes.

:::steps A three-pass approach to Paper 1
Pass 1 | Bank every question you can start at once
Pass 2 | Return to longer, partly-solved questions
Pass 3 | Attack the hardest, then check signs and exact form
Reserve | Last 8 to 10 minutes for checks only
:::

In pass one, do not "get stuck in". If a question does not yield a first line within about a minute, mark it and move on. The marks you bank in pass one are secured, and they lower the pressure for pass two. Pass two is where partial credit lives: write the formula, the first substitution, the set-up, even when you cannot finish. In pass three, with the safe marks already secured, you can afford to think.

### Drills that work for Paper 1

Paper 1 is trained, not revised. These drills are short, repeatable and measurable.

1. **Ten-minute exact-value sets.** Ten quick questions, no calculator, on one skill: surds, log laws, trig exact values or index rules. Mark immediately. Aim for 100% before moving up.
2. **Rule recall, written cold.** Once a week, write from memory every differentiation and integration rule, every log law, the sum formulae, and the identities, then check against the formula booklet. Gaps are your revision list for the week.
3. **One-page hand derivations.** Pick a result you normally use (the quadratic formula, the sum of a geometric series) and derive it. This builds the structural fluency that lets you adapt when a question looks unfamiliar.
4. **Silent-solution rewriting.** Take a solution you got right but wrote briefly. Rewrite it as if a stranger must follow every step. This trains the written-method habit that earns method marks.
5. **Two-column checking.** After any Paper 1 question, check with a different method or a quick estimate (sign, size, special case). Habitual checking catches the arithmetic slips that decide the paper.

### A worked illustration of lost method marks

Consider a student asked to find the exact value of an integral that needs substitution. The student does the substitution mentally, writes a correct final answer with one sign error, and receives only the final-answer mark, which is wrong, so zero. A second student writes the substitution line, the new limits, the antiderivative and the evaluation. The second student makes the *same* sign error but keeps three of four marks. The mathematics is identical; the writing is not. Paper 1 is where this difference is largest, because there is no calculator to rescue a missing line.

## How do you prepare for IB Math AA HL Paper 2 with a GDC?

Prepare for Paper 2 by pairing mathematical judgement with GDC speed: decide first whether a question wants algebra, a graph or a numerical method, then use the GDC efficiently, then write an interpretation in context with units and the required accuracy. The mathematics is often lighter than Paper 1; the marks are lost in setting up, reading the context and communicating the result.

### Question styles you will meet

- **Modelling with a function.** A scenario gives a formula or data; you find parameters, rates, maxima or turning points, and interpret them.
- **Statistics and probability.** Distributions (binomial, normal), expected values, conditional probability and sometimes hypothesis-style reasoning, with the GDC doing the heavy computation.
- **Calculus in context.** Rates of change, areas and volumes from a described situation, often with a numerical integration step.
- **Equations without exact solutions.** Solve using the GDC, then justify or interpret.
- **Vectors and kinematics in context.** Position, velocity and motion problems where the interpretation matters as much as the number.
- **Longer, connected questions** in which several parts reuse earlier results.

### The decision at the heart of Paper 2

Every Paper 2 question puts one question to you first: *by hand, by graph or by numerical tool?* Students who default to the GDC for everything lose time in menus and sometimes lose marks for no working. Students who refuse the GDC lose time on messy algebra. The efficient habit is to sketch the situation, choose the tool, state what you are about to do in one line, and then use it. The techniques themselves (solver, graph analysis, numerical calculus and statistics menus) are covered in detail in the guide to [the GDC advantage in IB DP Mathematics AA HL](/blog/the-gdc-advantage-in-ib-dp-mathematics-aa-hl/), including when the GDC hurts.

### The three marks hiding in every Paper 2 answer

Paper 2 answers carry three kinds of mark that students often forget in order.

1. **Set-up.** The equation or model you are solving. Write it before pressing a key.
2. **Result.** The number from the GDC, to the stated accuracy, usually three significant figures unless the question says otherwise.
3. **Interpretation.** A sentence in context, with units. "The gradient is 4.2" earns less than "the cost rises by about 4.2 rupees per extra unit".

Missing the third is the commonest Paper 2 loss, because the number *feels* like the answer. A habit that fixes it: after every result, ask "what does this mean for the person in the question?" and write one sentence.

### Timing for Paper 2

The same 120 minutes for 110 marks applies, but Paper 2 has a distinct risk: the long final questions, which are often connected and heavy on context, take a lot of reading. Plan for it.

:::timeline Illustrative Paper 2 time plan
Minutes 0-5 | Scan the whole paper, note which questions are quick wins
Minutes 5-60 | Shorter questions, in order, no stalling
Minutes 60-105 | Longer questions, writing set-up and interpretation each time
Minutes 105-120 | Check accuracy, units and blank spaces
This is a planning aid, not an official allocation. Adjust to your own pace after a timed paper.
:::

### Drills that work for Paper 2

1. **Context translation.** Take a worded question and write only the mathematical model, without solving. Do ten of these in a sitting. The skill of translating from words to an equation is where many students stall.
2. **Sketch-first sets.** For every question in a set, draw the graph or diagram before touching the GDC. Compare your sketch with the screen.
3. **Interpretation sentences.** Write one in-context sentence for the result of each question in a past paper, and have it checked against the markscheme's wording.
4. **GDC speed rounds.** Time yourself to reach a regression, an intersection, a numerical integral and a distribution probability. Knowing menu paths cold is worth several minutes.
5. **Accuracy audit.** Re-mark a completed paper looking only for significant-figure and unit errors. The total is often surprising.

## How do you prepare for IB Math AA HL Paper 3?

Prepare for Paper 3 by practising scaffolded investigations: read the whole problem, treat each part as its own question, carry results forward, and state an assumption when you cannot finish an earlier part. Paper 3 rewards persistence, pattern spotting and clear communication more than new content, and it improves fastest through regular, short, untimed exposure followed by a timed run.

### What Paper 3 actually looks like

Paper 3 gives extended problems set in unfamiliar territory. A typical question opens with a definition or a scenario you have not seen, asks you to compute a few simple cases, asks you to spot a pattern, then asks you to prove or generalise, and finishes with an extension or a link to another area of the syllabus. The early parts are accessible by design. The later parts reward the structure you built in the earlier ones.

That scaffold is the key to the whole paper. The setters want you to *discover* a result through small cases, not to recall it. So the preparation is to build the habit of exploring.

### The exploration routine

1. **Read the whole problem first.** Later parts explain the earlier ones and reveal where the question is heading.
2. **Do the small cases by hand.** If a question defines something for general n, compute n equal to 1, 2 and 3 before anything else.
3. **Look for the pattern, then say it.** Write the conjecture in words before trying to prove it.
4. **Prove or justify.** Choose the tool the syllabus gives you: induction, algebraic manipulation, a geometric argument or a calculus argument.
5. **Use results forward.** If a part says "hence" or "using your answer to (b)", look for the exact thing from (b) to use.
6. **State assumptions when stuck.** If you cannot finish part (c), write what you would have obtained, label it as an assumed value, and continue. Follow-through marks exist for this reason.

### Why Paper 3 is trainable

Many students believe Paper 3 is a talent test. It is not. It is a *routine* test. A student who has practised the six moves above on ten different scaffolds meets an eleventh with a plan. A student who has never seen the format spends the first twenty minutes working out how to start.

### A drill block for Paper 3

- **Small-case drill.** Take any extended question. Spend ten minutes only on specific numerical cases and writing the pattern. Do not prove anything.
- **Prove-it drill.** Take a conjecture from a previous question and spend fifteen minutes proving it by two methods.
- **Fill-the-gap drill.** Take a completed solution and delete the middle part. Try to reconstruct the logic that links the parts.
- **Never-blank drill.** For each part of a past Paper 3, write something: a definition, a formula, a small case. Compare it with the markscheme to see how often something scores.
- **Timed run.** Once a fortnight, do a whole Paper 3 under timed conditions and compare with the markscheme.

### Scenario: the student who stops at part (c)

Consider a student who is comfortable in class and scores well in topic tests. In a mock, Paper 3 part (a) goes fine, part (b) goes fine, part (c) needs a proof by induction she cannot see. She writes nothing for (c) and leaves (d), (e) and (f) blank because "they depended on (c)". She loses roughly half the paper. In a review session, she is shown that (d) only needed the *statement* of (c)'s result, and that (e) could be attempted from small cases. The fix is not more content. It is a rule: *no part is ever blank, and no part is ever abandoned because an earlier part was missed.*

## How do you split time across the three papers in a revision week?

Split the week by paper and by purpose: short daily fluency work for Paper 1, one longer Paper 2 session built around context and the GDC, one Paper 3 exploration session, and one review session where the markscheme and the error log are updated. A sustainable plan is eight to ten hours a week, increasing from January of DP2, and review is the session you must not skip.

| Day | Block | Paper focus | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Exact-value set plus topic questions, no calculator | Paper 1 | 75 min |
| Tuesday | Context translation plus a GDC speed round | Paper 2 | 75 min |
| Wednesday | Rest or light reading of last week's log | None | 20 min |
| Thursday | Mixed Paper 1 half-paper, timed | Paper 1 | 60 min |
| Friday | Paper 3 exploration, untimed | Paper 3 | 90 min |
| Saturday | Full or half past paper, timed | Alternating | 90 to 180 min |
| Sunday | Mark Saturday's paper, update the error log | All | 60 min |

The review session is where the marks are converted. A paper that is done and not marked is practice without learning. A paper that is marked and logged becomes next week's training plan. This is why the Sunday block comes with the same weight as the Saturday one.

The weekly shape changes across the two years. In DP1, the aim is to keep every paper on the radar with light, regular exposure so none of them is a shock in DP2. In the first half of DP2, shift to topic-wide mixed sets with Paper 3 style questions added. From January, move towards full papers under timed conditions, with the review session getting longer, not shorter.

## What is the right order of papers in the final months?

For IB Math AA HL paper preparation in the final months, rotate the papers: do a timed full paper roughly weekly, alternating Paper 1 and Paper 2, with a Paper 3 timed run every two to three weeks, and use the gaps for targeted drills based on your logs. Do not leave Paper 3 to the final fortnight: its skills need months to settle.

A common pattern of the last ten weeks looks like this.

:::timeline A final-months paper rotation
Weeks 10-8 | Topic gaps closed using the logs, short timed sets
Weeks 7-5 | One full paper a week, rotating Paper 1 and Paper 2
Weeks 4-3 | Paper 3 timed run each fortnight, review in depth
Weeks 2-1 | Light, mixed practice, formulas, notation, sleep
This is one workable pattern. Your school's mock dates and the May session calendar should set your own.
:::

The calendar is the school's. Your school sets its own mock dates, usually several months before the May session. A mock is the best data you will get, so treat it as a rehearsal: same tools, same timing, same breakfast. Then analyse it as described below.

## How do you analyse a mock or practice paper properly?

Analyse a paper by turning each lost mark into one of five causes, then act on the largest cause first. The process takes about an hour per paper, and it is worth more than another paper attempted in that hour, because it tells you what to change.

1. **Rewrite the question you lost marks on**, in full, without looking at the solution.
2. **Compare with the markscheme line by line**, noting which marks were available at which step.
3. **Name the cause**: content gap, no start, algebra slip, missing working, or time.
4. **Write the specific fix**, not a vague one: "write the inner derivative as a separate line" instead of "be careful".
5. **Schedule the fix**: pick the practice that will test it next week.

### Reading your pattern

If most lost marks fall in "algebra slip", your training target is Paper 1-style fluency drills and checking habits. If most are "no start", the target is exploration and recognition: more mixed, unfamiliar questions. If most are "missing working", the target is presentation; it needs no extra content at all. If "time", the target is the three-pass method and calculator speed. If "content gap", it is back to the topic, with a short list of topics rather than the whole syllabus.

This diagnostic is the same one an experienced tutor runs in the first sessions, and it is the best test of a tutor's method: do they diagnose from your scripts, or do they start with "let us revise chapters"? ## What mistakes cost the most marks on each paper?

The costliest mistakes are paper-specific: arithmetic and missing working on Paper 1, uninterpreted results and accuracy slips on Paper 2, and abandoned parts on Paper 3. Fix them with a rule per mistake, not with general advice to be careful.

| Paper | Mistake | Rule that fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | Decimalising too early | Keep surds, logs and π exact until the last line |
| Paper 1 | Sign error in a chain-rule or integration step | Write the inner function and the substitution on separate lines |
| Paper 1 | Skipping algebra "because it is obvious" | Show every step, even when it feels trivial |
| Paper 2 | Giving a number without meaning | End each context question with a sentence including units |
| Paper 2 | Wrong accuracy | Default to three significant figures unless told otherwise |
| Paper 2 | Calculator answer with no set-up | Write the equation or model before using the GDC |
| Paper 3 | Leaving later parts blank | Write something in every part, assume an earlier result if needed |
| Paper 3 | Not reading the whole problem first | Read to the end, then plan |
| All | Spending ten minutes on a three-mark question | Use the marks-to-minutes budget and move on |

Another quiet loss is notation: missing dx, no constant of integration, undefined variables. Every one of these is a mark the examiner is entitled to withhold, and each is cured in a week of deliberate habit.

## How does the Internal Assessment fit with paper preparation?

The IA is 20% of the grade, independent of the exam papers, and it should be planned alongside paper preparation, not squeezed in at the end. A rushed IA throws away marks that the student controls, while a well-timed IA often improves exam fluency because it requires sustained mathematical writing.

The mathematics exploration is 12 to 20 pages, marked out of 20 on five criteria: Presentation (4), Mathematical communication (4), Personal engagement (3), Reflection (3) and Use of mathematics (6). Higher Level work is judged against a higher standard on the Use of mathematics criterion. The teacher may comment on one draft, and the work must be the student's own. Put the IA draft deadline on the same calendar as your mocks, so you never face both in the same fortnight. The integrity rules and process are explained in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

## What does paper-focused tutoring look like in practice?

Good paper-focused tutoring is a loop of diagnosis, targeted drill, timed attempt and review, with the student doing most of the writing. A session typically starts from the student's own marked paper, picks one or two causes of lost marks, practises the fix on fresh questions, and sets a short independent task for the next session.

An illustrative first fortnight with a DP2 student might run like this. Session one: the tutor reviews a recent mock, builds the five-bucket log with the student, and agrees the two largest causes. Session two: a Paper 1 fluency block on the first cause, with silent-solution rewriting, and a Paper 2 context-translation task. Session three: a Paper 3 exploration, where the tutor asks questions and does not give the pattern away. Session four: a timed half paper, marked together. The student leaves each session with a task they can do alone.

Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, describes the approach in terms of personalised learning (starting from a real diagnosis of the student's script), seven-level thinking (training pattern recognition and multi-step reasoning on unfamiliar scenarios) and exam strategy training (timed drills, marks-to-minutes discipline, paper-specific tactics and presentation habits). The principle is the same whichever tutor you work with: the plan must come from the student's own errors.

## When is it too late to change your paper preparation?

It is almost never too late to change the *method*, but the later you are, the more you must narrow the target. With three or more months left, a full rebuild is possible. With four to six weeks, focus on the single largest cause of lost marks and on the paper that has the most marks to give. In the final fortnight, protect sleep and consolidate: no new methods.

A realistic example: a student with eight weeks left and a mock total around the 5 to 6 band might find that two-thirds of lost marks are "missing working" and "algebra slip". Those are fixable in weeks. A student whose lost marks are mostly "content gap" in three topics needs a different plan, with topic work first. The point is that the logs tell you which situation you are in. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best way to start IB Math AA HL paper preparation?

Start by sitting one past paper under exam conditions and marking it strictly against the official markscheme. Then classify every lost mark by cause: content gap, no start, algebra slip, missing working or time. This takes an afternoon and tells you which paper and which habit to train first. Without it, most revision effort goes to topics you already handle.

### How many past papers should I do?

There is no magic number, but quality matters more than quantity. One paper marked and logged thoroughly teaches more than three attempted and glanced at. A common plan is one timed full paper a week in the last ten weeks, alternating Paper 1 and Paper 2, with a Paper 3 timed run every two to three weeks. Increase frequency only if you are still reviewing each one properly.

### Is Paper 3 really that different from Papers 1 and 2?

Yes, in demand if not in content. Paper 3 uses the same syllabus but gives extended, scaffolded problems in unfamiliar settings, so it rewards exploration, pattern spotting and communication. Students who only drill textbook-style questions find it unrecognisable. It improves through regular exposure to scaffolded problems and a fixed routine for reading, small cases, conjecture and proof.

### How should I manage time in Paper 1 and Paper 2?

Use the marks-to-minutes rule: about one minute per mark, with a reserve of eight to ten minutes at the end. Work in three passes: bank the questions you can start immediately, return to partly solved ones, then attempt the hardest. Do not stay on a question that has not yielded a first line within about a minute during pass one.

### Should I use the calculator for everything on Paper 2?

No. Decide first whether the question wants algebra, a graph or a numerical tool. Sketching first prevents whole categories of error, and writing the set-up before using the GDC protects method marks. Use the GDC where it saves time, such as intersections, numerical integrals and distribution probabilities, and do simple algebra by hand when it is faster.

### What if I cannot do part (a) or (b) of a Paper 3 question?

Move on and use an assumed value. Later parts often say "hence" or give a result you can use even if you could not derive it. State what you are assuming and continue, because follow-through marks reward correct reasoning from an assumed result. Never leave a part blank: a definition, a formula or a small case often earns marks.

### How many hours a week does AA HL paper practice need?

Roughly eight to ten hours a week, spread across the week, is a sustainable load alongside five other subjects. Lighter in DP1, heavier from January of DP2. Five ninety-minute sessions beat one long weekend block. What you do with the hours matters more than the number, and marking plus the error log must be part of them.

### Can a tutor help with paper preparation, and what should they do?

A tutor can diagnose your scripts, teach the missing habits and run timed drills with feedback. They should start from your marked papers, not from the textbook, and should give you tasks to complete alone. They should not do the work for you. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and the value of a tutor lies in how quickly they find and fix your specific losses.

### When should I start preparing for Paper 3 specifically?

Start in DP1 with light exposure, such as one extended question a fortnight, and increase through DP2. Paper 3 skills need months to settle because they are habits of thinking, not facts to memorise. Leaving it until March is the most common planning error, since it is a fifth of the grade and the least like day-to-day classwork.

### Does AA HL paper preparation differ for AI HL?

Yes. AA is algebraic and proof-heavy, while Applications and Interpretation (AI) is modelling and statistics-heavy with technology throughout, so the drills and the balance between papers differ. If you are still deciding between them, read the [AA versus AI decision guide](/blog/ib-maths-aa-vs-ai-which-one-should-you-choose/) and confirm placement with your school's DP coordinator.

### Is it normal to score much lower on Paper 1 than Paper 2?

Yes, and it usually points to hand fluency or missing working, not intelligence. Without a calculator, small arithmetic slips and skipped steps cost more marks. The remedy is exact-value drills, rule recall from memory, silent-solution rewriting and the habit of showing every line. If the gap persists after a month of drills, bring the marked paper to a teacher or tutor.
