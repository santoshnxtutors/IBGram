IB exam preparation in Dwarka comes down to a calendar and a commute. The calendar is the one every International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) student in India faces: school mocks in the winter of DP2, written papers in May, results in early July. The commute is local: a Dwarka family has to decide whether a tutor arrives at the flat, whether the student travels, or whether the sessions happen on a screen. Get both right and the last six months are calm. Get either wrong and they are a scramble.

This guide is for DP2 students and their parents in Dwarka, Delhi, and for DP1 families who want to know what the next year should look like. It works backwards from the May session, with a month-by-month countdown, a recovery plan for a weak mock, a rhythm for past-paper practice, an honest view of home versus online tutoring in Dwarka's sectors, and the questions to ask before bringing a tutor in. The general system for any city is in [the IB exam preparation guide](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/); this page is the local, countdown-driven version.

## What does IB exam preparation in Dwarka look like in practice?

IB exam preparation in Dwarka looks like any serious DP revision plan with two local adjustments: protected study blocks that survive a long school day and an honest choice about where tutoring happens. Most Dwarka students are in the same position as students across Delhi and the NCR: a demanding timetable, coursework deadlines that collide with mocks, and a limited number of specialist tutors per subject nearby.

Dwarka is a planned sub-city in south-west Delhi, laid out in numbered sectors, with a large share of housing in group-housing societies and apartment blocks and a metro line running through it. That layout shapes study life in three practical ways:

- **Societies with gates and visitor registers.** Home tutoring means a tutor will need to be cleared at the gate. Setting this up in advance, with a regular name on the guard's list, avoids the ten-minute delay that eats into a one-hour lesson.
- **Flats, not large houses.** A quiet study corner is the scarce resource, particularly if younger siblings are at home. A student who shares a room should plan lesson times for when the room is free.
- **Commutes that depend on school.** Students at schools elsewhere in Delhi or across the Gurgaon border may travel a long way, so evenings start late. A plan that assumes a free 4 p.m. will fail.

None of this is specific to Dwarka alone, but all of it is more visible there than in a district where schools sit round the corner. What follows converts that reality into a plan.

## What does the countdown to the May session look like?

The countdown to the May session runs in four stages: the autumn build, the winter mock window, the spring rehearsal and the final month. Each stage has one job, and the common failure is doing the wrong stage's job. A student who starts timed full papers in September is not ready; one who is still learning new content in April has run out of time.

:::timeline Countdown for a DP2 student in Dwarka
August-October | Finish IA drafts; keep a weekly timed section
November-January | Core submissions; prepare for school mocks
After the mocks | Lost-mark analysis; a three-week fix plan
March-April | Full timed papers; rotate subjects
Final month | Rehearse, rest, check logistics
:::

Dates of school mocks and submission deadlines differ by school, so treat the stages as a sequence, not as fixed calendar months. Your school's coordinator publishes the actual dates.

### August to October: protect the base

DP2 begins with a rush of coursework. Internal Assessments (IA) are due across subjects, the Extended Essay (EE) needs finishing, and Theory of Knowledge (TOK) work begins to bite. The danger in this period is that exam preparation disappears. Keep one fixed weekly block, 40 to 60 minutes per subject, of timed past-paper questions. It is small, but it keeps exam skills warm and starts the error log that will be the main asset later.

### November to January: finish the core, prepare for the mocks

Aim to have the Extended Essay and subject IAs essentially complete before the school's mock window. Every hour that coursework spills into mocks costs preparation time. The sequence of what is due when, and how the three parts of the core interact, is mapped in [the IB DP core timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/).

During this stage, shift revision towards the mocks: topic lists per paper, a list of command terms that matter in each subject, and at least two timed practice sessions in the hardest subjects.

### After the mocks: the most valuable three weeks

This is the pivot of the whole year. The following section covers it in detail.

### March to April: rehearsal

New learning should be rare. Use full timed papers, alternating subjects so that the student practises the rhythm of changing between them, as the exam timetable does. Keep the error log open, and use it before every paper.

### The final month

The last 30 days have their own day-by-day logic: paper rotation, triage of weak topics, sleep and logistics. That plan is in [what toppers do in the last 30 days before IB exams](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/). Read it early in the spring so you can fit it to your own timetable.

## How do you recover from a bad IB mock?

Recover from a bad IB mock by treating it as a diagnostic: mark the script yourself, list every lost mark with its cause, fix the largest cause first, and retest after two weeks. A weak mock in January or February is the cheapest failure available, because there are still months to act.

A simple recovery process:

1. **Wait one day, then look.** The first reaction is emotional. Open the script after a night's sleep.
2. **Re-mark against the markscheme.** Teachers' marks are a start; your own re-marking shows you how marks are awarded.
3. **Write every lost mark in a table**: question, marks lost, one-line cause.
4. **Sort the causes** into knowledge gaps, method errors, command-term or presentation errors, and time pressure.
5. **Pick the top two causes** and build the next three weeks around them.
6. **Retest** with fresh questions after two weeks and compare.

:::steps Mock recovery loop
Re-mark | Against the markscheme, same week
Log | Every lost mark with a cause
Fix | Top two causes, three weeks
Retest | Fresh questions after two weeks
:::

Consider a typical Dwarka DP2 student, described here as an illustration. She walks out of her Economics mock with a result well below target and assumes she does not know enough. The log tells another story: of the marks lost, most come from description where evaluation was asked, and a smaller share from a missed diagram. Her knowledge was fine; her answers stopped one step short. Her fix is not more notes. It is writing the evaluative sentence first in every extended answer and rehearsing it on six questions a week. That is a two-week repair, and it moves marks.

The command-term logic behind this particular error is covered in [IB command terms explained with real examples](/blog/ib-command-terms-explained-with-real-examples/).

### When a mock goes badly in several subjects

If the shortfall is broad, resist the urge to rebuild everything. Rank the subjects by marks recoverable per hour of work. A subject where the log shows two repeating errors is a better investment than one where the student simply has not covered half the syllabus. Cover the cheap marks first, then the structural gaps.

### When the mock goes well

Do not coast. A good mock shows which habits are working. Keep them, and use the free time to rehearse the paper you fear most.

## How often should you practise past papers?

Practise past papers on a cadence that rises through the year: one timed section a week in the autumn, one full paper a week in the spring, and a daily light paper routine only in the final fortnight. The cadence matters more than the volume, because each paper must be followed by marking, correction and logging, which takes as long as writing it.

:::bars Past-paper effort across DP2 (typical share of weekly study time, illustrative)
Autumn | 20 | about 20%
Winter, around mocks | 35 | about 35%
Spring | 55 | about 55%
Final fortnight | 70 | about 70%
These shares are an illustrative guide to how the balance shifts, not a rule or research finding.
:::

The shape is what counts: more content early, more rehearsal late. Each stage has a "paper day" that follows a simple routine.

1. **Sit the paper cold**, under the real time limit, in a quiet space.
2. **Mark with the markscheme** the same day, while the memory is fresh.
3. **Rewrite two lost-mark answers** in full.
4. **Log the causes**, one line each.
5. **Retest one weak question** two or three days later.

Past papers, markschemes and subject reports reach students through the school or the IB's official channels. Subject reports are the most underused: they say in plain language what candidates did wrong in each question. Read the report for each paper you attempt.

A practical point for Dwarka families: set the paper practice in a window when the home is quiet. Weekend mornings usually work. A paper attempted with a television in the next room is a different exercise from one done in silence, and the real exam will be silent.

## Which subjects usually need most attention in the final months?

The subject that needs most attention is the one with the largest recoverable loss, which is rarely the one a student worries about most. Even so, patterns recur, and knowing them helps you read your own log.

| Subject | Where marks often go | A focused fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics (AA or AI) | Skipped working, the last paper's extended problems, notation | Timed mixed papers; method written line by line |
| Physics, Chemistry, Biology | Data-based questions, units, extended responses | Unseen data practice; definitions; six-mark answer drills |
| Economics | Evaluation missing; diagrams unexplained | Evaluation-first paragraphs; labelled diagrams |
| English A | Weak planning; thin evidence | Five-minute plan; paragraph-level practice |
| Language B | Listening and writing neglected | Separate sessions for receptive and productive skills |

The DP sciences, first assessed in 2025, have Paper 1 in two parts (1A multiple choice and 1B data-based questions) and Paper 2 of short-answer and extended-response questions. Students who rehearse the data-based section from unfamiliar contexts, not from the textbook, usually gain more than those who re-read notes.

## Should a Dwarka student choose home tutoring or online?

Choose the mode that the student will keep up for twelve weeks without cancellations. Home tutoring suits students who concentrate better with a person in the room and families whose flat has a quiet corner. Online suits students who need a specialist who does not live nearby, or whose week has no room for a visitor. A hybrid combines both.

| Factor | Home | Online |
|---|---|---|
| Specialist availability | Limited to tutors who can reach the sector | Wider, including tutors in other cities |
| Concentration | Often better for students who drift on screens | Fine for self-directed students |
| Society gate and visitors | Needs advance clearance | Not an issue |
| Evening travel | Falls on the tutor | None for either side |
| Maths and diagrams | Paper and pen, natural | Needs a tablet or shared board |
| Exam-season flexibility | Fixed slot | Easy to add a short review |

The honest trade-off is specialist depth against presence. If your child needs a rare combination, such as HL Physics with strong data-analysis coaching, online widens the pool. If your child fades on a screen after forty minutes, a home tutor for the hardest subject is a better bet. Many families end up with home sessions for one demanding subject and online sessions for the others.

## How do you make home tutoring work in a Dwarka society?

Home tutoring works when the logistics are settled before the first session: a gate pass or standing instruction, a fixed day and time, a quiet table, and a clear rule about phones. Most failed home-tuition arrangements fail on logistics, not teaching.

A checklist for the first week:

- **Gate and visitor entry.** Give the society's security the tutor's name and a standing instruction for the weekly slot.
- **Time of day.** Pick a slot when the student is fresh and the home is calm. Immediately after a long school day is the worst time for hard subjects.
- **Study space.** A clear table, a lamp and a door that closes beat a nice desk in a shared hall.
- **Materials ready.** Past paper, markscheme, error log and a pencil case on the table before the tutor arrives.
- **Phones away.** For both student and tutor, for the length of the session.
- **A buffer.** If one lesson is lost to traffic or a delay, agree in advance how it is made up.

Where a tutor lives some distance away, weekday evening travel can make punctuality unreliable. If you notice repeated late arrivals, move that tutor's lessons online, or to a weekend slot, rather than letting the pattern settle.

## How do you make online sessions work from Dwarka?

Online sessions work with three ingredients: a shared writing surface, a stable connection and a quiet room. Without the first, a maths or science session turns into a lecture. A tablet or a phone on a stand pointed at paper is enough, as long as the tutor can see the student's working.

Practical points:

1. **Test the set-up before the first lesson.** Camera angle, audio, screen sharing and the writing method.
2. **Keep a backup.** If the connection drops, switch to a phone call and photographed working.
3. **Save every board or page.** A folder per subject becomes the revision file.
4. **Keep sessions active.** The student writes and speaks for most of the time; the tutor questions and corrects.
5. **Close each session with a one-line takeaway** that goes into the error log.

Online also helps in the final weeks, when short, frequent sessions on a single paper are more useful than a long weekly one. A 30-minute review of a marked paper is hard to schedule if a tutor has to travel; online makes it routine.

## How does coursework affect exam preparation here?

Coursework matters because deadlines collide with revision. The DP's Internal Assessments, the Extended Essay (up to 4,000 words and roughly 40 hours of work) and the TOK essay and exhibition all fall due before the written papers, and a student who is still writing in January is losing mock preparation time.

The firm rules are the same everywhere. Each IB internal assessment is the student's own work. A teacher may give feedback on one draft; no tutor or parent may write, rewrite or edit the submission. A tutor may teach the underlying skill, question the reasoning, point to a criterion and ask the student to reread their own paragraph against it. Details and criteria logic are in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/), and the Extended Essay process is in [how to write an IB Extended Essay](/blog/how-to-write-a-perfect-ib-extended-essay-ee/).

For revision, the point is timing: set the last working date for each piece, protect it, and then stop. Time spent polishing a 20% component beyond that point comes at the cost of the written papers.

## What should a Dwarka parent do, and not do?

For IB exam preparation in Dwarka, a parent's best contributions are logistics, calm and questions about the plan. The least useful is a daily question about marks. Students in the final months already know the scores; what they need is a home where the plan can happen.

Helpful:

- **Keep evenings predictable.** Same dinner time, same quiet hour.
- **Take a weekly plan check.** "What is this week's focus, and what do you need from me?"
- **Handle logistics.** Gate arrangements, printing, transport on mock days.
- **Watch sleep.** Late nights before mocks lose more than they gain.
- **Notice when it is too much.** If a student has been low or sleepless for more than a couple of weeks, talk to the school counsellor or a doctor.

Unhelpful:

- Comparing with a cousin or classmate.
- Adding tutors in every subject at once, so the week has no room left.
- Pushing a tutor to "finish the syllabus faster" when the log says the problem is technique.

## When is a tutor worth adding in the final six months?

A tutor is worth adding when the lost-mark list shows a specific, fixable pattern that school time does not address. Examples: a topic that never clicked, a repeated technique error, a subject where the class has moved on too fast, or a Paper 3-type problem that the student cannot start.

Ask these questions before booking:

1. **What do I want fixed?** A one-line answer, from the log.
2. **Is it knowledge, technique or time?** Tutoring is strongest on the first two.
3. **What will it replace?** A new session must displace a lower-value block, not stack on a full week.
4. **How will we know in three weeks?** A defined review point, with a repeated task.

For IB exam preparation in Dwarka, timing is everything: starting a tutor in April for a vague "extra help" is a poor use of limited weeks. Starting in January with a defined list is a strong one. For timing across the whole programme, see [when to start IB tutoring](/blog/when-to-start-ib-tutoring-gurgaon/), and for judging candidates see [how to choose an IB tutor in Gurgaon](/blog/how-to-choose-ib-tutor-gurgaon/), whose questions apply equally in Delhi.

No tutor can guarantee a grade. Treat any promise of one as a reason to look elsewhere.

## What does a realistic weekly timetable look like for a DP2 student in Dwarka?

A realistic timetable assumes a long school day and builds around fixed commitments first. Here is an illustrative spring-term week for a student with six subjects, one tutor session and school finishing mid-afternoon.

| Day | Block | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Evening, 2 hours | Subject A: topic review plus past-paper questions |
| Tuesday | Evening, 2 hours | Subject B: timed section, then marking |
| Wednesday | Evening, 90 minutes | Tutor session in the weakest subject (home or online) |
| Thursday | Evening, 2 hours | Subject C: error-log review and rewrites |
| Friday | Evening, 60 minutes | Light: flashcards, reading, one core task |
| Saturday | Morning, 3 hours | Full timed paper, then marking |
| Sunday | Short block | Plan next week; rest |

Three features keep it realistic. Subjects rotate rather than block, so recall stays active. The weekend morning carries the full paper because that is when the home is quiet. And Friday is deliberately light, which protects the weekend and gives the student a pressure valve.

Adjust it for school activities, travel and the exam timetable. A student with a sport or an instrument keeps it in the plan, since removing everything that is not study usually makes the study worse.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should a Dwarka student start IB exam preparation?

Start building habits in DP1 and begin structured revision in the autumn of DP2. In DP1 the work is understanding and keeping an error log; in DP2 it shifts to timed practice and retrieval. Starting intense revision in March leaves too little time to act on mock feedback. A steady weekly block from August of DP2 is usually enough to keep exam skills warm.

### Is home tutoring or online better for IB students in Dwarka?

For IB exam preparation in Dwarka, neither mode is better for everyone. Home suits students who concentrate better with a person in the room and have a quiet space; online suits those who need a specialist not available nearby and whose week is tight. Many families combine them. Judge by what the student will sustain for twelve weeks, and use a trial session to test the fit.

### How many past papers should I do before May?

Quality matters more than count. Do a timed section weekly in the autumn, a full paper weekly in the spring and a lighter daily routine in the final fortnight, marking each against the markscheme and rewriting weak answers. A student who does fewer papers and corrects them thoroughly usually gains more than one who attempts many and never reviews.

### What should I do if my mock result is far below target?

Mark the script yourself, list every lost mark with its cause, and tag each as knowledge, method, command term or time. Fix the largest cause first and retest after two weeks. A mock in winter is a diagnostic, not a forecast. Speak to your teacher and, if useful, a tutor, with the list in hand.

### Can a tutor help with the Extended Essay or IA?

A tutor can teach skills, question the student's reasoning and point to criteria, but cannot write or edit the submission. The IB allows teacher feedback on one draft and requires the work to be the student's own. A tutor who offers to write or tidy a draft puts the student at risk of a malpractice finding.

### Do Dwarka societies allow tutors to come in?

Usually yes, with gate clearance. Practices vary by society, so give security the tutor's name and agree a standing instruction for the weekly slot. Sorting this before the first lesson prevents delays that cut into the session. If entry is a recurring problem, consider moving that tutor's sessions online.

### How long should a tutoring session be in exam season?

Forty-five to sixty minutes works for most students, with a short retrieval opening, live work and a closing summary. In the final weeks, shorter, more frequent sessions on one marked paper can be more useful than a long weekly one. Adjust to the student's energy and avoid late-night sessions that cost sleep.

### What if my child studies at a school in Gurgaon but lives in Dwarka?

Plan around the commute. Tutoring slots should avoid the hardest part of the evening, and online sessions can remove one more journey. The Dwarka Expressway links the two areas, and [IB tutors on the Dwarka Expressway in Gurugram](/ib-tutors/gurugram/areas/dwarka-expressway/) lists tutor pages for the Gurgaon side. Confirm the school calendar and mock dates with the school.

### Will an IB tutor promise a 7?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and outcomes depend on the student's starting level, practice, school assessment and many other factors. A good tutor defines a process, shows evidence of progress and changes the plan when evidence demands it. Be cautious about promises of a particular result.
