To move from a 5 to a 7 in MYP maths, a student has to add about nine to thirteen points to a criterion total that sits somewhere between 19 and 23 out of 32, and the fastest way to find those points is to read the four criterion levels behind the grade, not the grade itself. A final 5 is not a vague "good but not great" result. It is a specific sum, built from four numbers, and each of the four can be raised by a different kind of work. Most families spend months on "more maths" when the missing points sit in one or two criteria that need something quite specific.

This guide is for parents and students in the Middle Years Programme (MYP), usually in MYP 3 to 5, who are stuck at a 5 and want to understand the mechanics: how the Mathematics criteria add up, which grade band a given total falls into, which criterion tends to move fastest, and what a one-level upgrade looks like in each of A, B, C and D. It also covers the consistency problem that holds back strong students, and where a tutor does and does not help. It does not promise a grade. No tutor can guarantee one, and the final judgement belongs to the school's teachers under IB moderation.

For the detailed anatomy of each criterion, see [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/). For reading a report card as a parent, [MYP maths criteria explained for parents](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/) is the companion. This post is about the arithmetic and the upgrades.

## How Is an MYP Maths Grade Calculated From the Criteria?

An MYP maths grade is calculated by adding the student's levels in the four criteria, each scored from 0 to 8, to give a total out of 32, and then converting that total to a final grade from 1 to 7 using the IB's grade boundaries. The four criteria are A (Knowing and understanding), B (Investigating patterns), C (Communicating) and D (Applying mathematics in real-life contexts).

The criterion levels themselves are decided by the teacher, using the IB's level descriptors, from the student's body of work across the year: tests, investigations and applied tasks. They are not a simple average of percentage marks. A task is marked against descriptors, and the teacher then judges which level best describes the student's work in each criterion, giving weight to consistent and recent performance. Schools differ in how they record this, so ask the teacher how final levels are decided in your school.

The conversion from total to grade is the part families rarely see. The published general boundaries for the MYP are:

| Final grade | Criterion total (out of 32) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 to 5 |
| 2 | 6 to 9 |
| 3 | 10 to 14 |
| 4 | 15 to 18 |
| 5 | 19 to 23 |
| 6 | 24 to 27 |
| 7 | 28 to 32 |

Confirm these in your school's MYP handbook or with the MYP coordinator, since the IB can revise boundaries and schools report them in their own formats. In MYP 5, where a school offers the optional eAssessment, the final result also draws on on-screen examinations and ePortfolios, and the school explains how the pieces combine.

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
32 | Maximum criterion total
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

## What Do the Grade Boundaries Look Like in Practice?

The bands are not equal in width, and that shapes strategy. Grade 5 covers five totals (19 to 23), grade 6 covers four (24 to 27) and grade 7 covers five (28 to 32). A student at the bottom of the 5 band needs five more points to reach 24, while a student at the top of the band needs one.

:::bars Criterion totals behind each grade (from the published general boundaries)
Grade 4 starts at | 47 | 15 of 32
Grade 5 starts at | 59 | 19 of 32
Grade 6 starts at | 75 | 24 of 32
Grade 7 starts at | 88 | 28 of 32
Bar length is the starting total as a share of 32; confirm boundaries with your school.
:::

Two consequences follow. First, a "5" can mean a student who is one point from a 6 or five points from one, and the strategy differs. Second, a 7 requires 28 of 32, which means a student can drop no more than four points across all four criteria. That sounds severe, and it is: a single 6 in one criterion, with 8s in the other three, gives 30, which is a 7. Two 6s and two 8s give 28, still a 7. Three 6s and one 8 give 26, a 6. The top grade belongs to the student who is secure in the top band in at least three criteria.

## What Does It Take to Move From a 5 to a 6?

Moving from a 5 to a 6 means raising the total from somewhere between 19 and 23 to at least 24, which is between one and five extra points across four criteria. The practical reading is that a one-level upgrade in just two or three criteria is usually enough, which is a far smaller task than "become a stronger mathematician".

Consider a typical 5 profile with illustrative levels:

| Criterion | Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A Knowing and understanding | 6 | Secure on routine work |
| B Investigating patterns | 5 | Finds patterns, rarely justifies a rule |
| C Communicating | 5 | Right answers, incomplete working |
| D Applying in real-life contexts | 5 | Models, but does not justify or check |
| **Total** | **21** | **Grade 5** |

To reach 24, this student needs three more points. Raising C and D by one level each and B by one gives 24 exactly. Raising only C and D by one gives 23, which is still a 5. Raising A by one and C and D by one gives 24. The point is that the student has several routes, and choosing the cheapest one is the whole game.

Which routes are cheap depends on the type of error. A student who understands the mathematics but writes too little in solutions is one lesson-type away from a C upgrade. A student who cannot yet justify a general rule has a longer road to B. Reading the criterion pattern is the diagnosis.

## What Does It Take to Reach a 7?

Reaching a 7 means a total of 28 or more, which in practice means at least three criteria at level 7 or 8 and no criterion below 6. This is a different kind of target from a 6, because it requires success in unfamiliar problems and not only polish on familiar ones.

The IB's top-band descriptors in the Mathematics criteria are written around the student doing the right thing in situations that were not rehearsed: selecting and applying methods in unfamiliar settings, describing and justifying a general rule, communicating a complete and concise argument, and applying mathematics to a real context with justification of accuracy and fit. Anyone planning to move from a 5 to a 7 in MYP maths has to plan for these descriptors. A student who is excellent on familiar tasks and nervous on new ones will plateau at a 6, and it can look like a stubborn ceiling.

Four routes to 28 or more show how different students get there:

| Route | A | B | C | D | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Even all-rounder | 7 | 7 | 7 | 7 | 28 |
| Strong in application | 8 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 29 |
| Strong in reasoning | 7 | 8 | 7 | 6 | 28 |
| One dominant skill | 8 | 8 | 8 | 4 | 28 |

The last row shows something worth knowing: a weak criterion does not necessarily block a 7 if three others are at the top. It also shows the risk, since four points lost in one criterion leaves no margin elsewhere.

To move from a 5 to a 7 in MYP maths, therefore, the target is seldom "do everything better". It is to find which three criteria can be brought to 7 or 8, and then to remove the weakest one from the danger zone.

## Which Criterion Moves Fastest?

For most students stuck at a 5, Criterion C (Communicating) moves fastest, then A (Knowing and understanding), then D (Applying), and B (Investigating patterns) is the slowest. This is a pattern, not a rule, and it depends on why the marks were lost, but it is a reliable starting assumption.

The reasoning is about what kind of work each criterion needs:

- **C moves fastest** because the missing marks are usually not missing mathematics. The student has the idea but does not show it, or uses notation loosely, or leaves the answer without a conclusion. These are habits, and habits change in weeks with strict feedback.
- **A moves next** because it responds to accuracy, to recall of key methods and to a clear understanding of why a method works. A student who corrects errors systematically and revises strategically sees A move within a term.
- **D moves more slowly** because it requires the student to model a situation, choose a method, apply it and judge the result, which is a sequence with several places to fail. It improves with exposure to varied contexts and with a routine for unfamiliar problems.
- **B moves slowest** because it requires a way of thinking: noticing, conjecturing, generalising and justifying. Students who have been trained to wait for instructions need time to become comfortable with open questions.

:::steps The usual order of upgrades
C first | Habits of working and notation
A next | Accuracy and understanding of why
D then | Model, solve, justify, check
B last | Notice, generalise, prove
:::

This order should not be treated as a reason to ignore B. A student whose B is stuck at 4 or 5 and whose other criteria are strong will find the ceiling at 6 or 7 is set by B. The advice is to start where the points are cheapest, then work on the long-term skills in parallel, because they take longer.

## How Do You Upgrade Criterion C by One Level?

You upgrade Criterion C by making the reasoning visible, complete and concise, using correct notation and a clear conclusion in every solution. The upgrade is usually one of habit, which is why it is the cheapest in the set.

Consider a question: "A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 cm and its length is twice its width. Find its area."

**A level-4-style response** writes the answer only: 50.

**A level-6-style response** writes: "Let width = w. Length = 2w. Perimeter: 2(w + 2w) = 30, so 6w = 30, w = 5. Length = 10. Area = 5 × 10 = 50 cm²."

**A level-8-style response** adds precision and a conclusion: it defines the variables with units ("let the width be w cm"), keeps the algebra in logical order with each line following from the last, checks the perimeter (2 × 15 = 30), and states the conclusion in a sentence: "The area of the rectangle is 50 cm²."

The mathematics is identical. What changes is the definition of variables, the logical sequence, the units and the closing statement. These are the elements the Criterion C descriptors reward.

A short routine that moves C reliably:

1. Define every variable with its unit before using it.
2. Write each step on its own line, with the reason where it is not obvious.
3. Use correct notation: equals signs that mean equal, brackets where needed, and no run-on chains.
4. Label diagrams and axes.
5. End with a sentence that answers the question in context.
6. Reread as if you were the marker, and ask whether anyone could follow it without you.

A tutor can speed this up by marking one piece of work a week for communication only, ignoring correctness, and asking the student to rewrite it. Two or three weeks of that often lifts C by a level.

## How Do You Upgrade Criterion A by One Level?

You upgrade Criterion A by raising accuracy, widening the range of methods the student can select, and building real understanding of why a method works. A student at a 5 or 6 in A typically knows the methods, but makes slips and is hesitant when two methods compete.

The route is a three-step loop. First, keep an error log with causes, so that slips are classified (sign, arithmetic, wrong formula, misread) and trained out. Second, practise selection: take a mixed set of ten questions from different topics and, before solving, write down which method each needs and why. Third, test understanding by asking the student to explain, in one or two sentences, why a method works, for example why completing the square always yields the vertex form.

A short check that exposes the difference between a 5 and a 7 in A: give the student a question in an unfamiliar layout and ask whether they can say what is being asked in their own words. A student who restates it correctly in one sentence tends to choose the right method. A student who cannot restate it is pattern-matching and will fail when the pattern changes.

For students who plan to continue into the Diploma Programme, the mathematics in A is the foundation for later work, and the same habits of accuracy and understanding are what the flagship guide describes for the senior years in [how to score a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/). The foundation built here shows up there.

## How Do You Upgrade Criterion B by One Level?

You upgrade Criterion B by training the full sequence of investigation: notice a pattern, state a rule in words, express it algebraically, test it on new cases and justify why it must hold. Most students at a 5 stop after the first or second step.

Take the sequence 3, 8, 15, 24, 35. A level-4-style response gives the next term, 48. A level-6-style response finds the differences (5, 7, 9, 11), notes that they increase by 2, and proposes a rule. A level-8-style response goes further: it expresses the *n*th term as n² + 2n, checks it on the first five terms, then explains why it works by noting that n² + 2n = (n + 1)² − 1, so each term is one less than a perfect square, and tests the rule for a term not in the list, for example the 10th term, 120.

What separates the levels is the movement from "what comes next" to "why must it be so". A student who can justify a general rule, even informally, is working at the top of B.

A practical routine for B:

1. State what you notice in words, before any algebra.
2. Make a table of at least five cases.
3. Look for a relationship between consecutive terms and between position and value.
4. Write a general rule and test it on a case you have not used.
5. Explain, in a sentence or a diagram, why the rule must hold.
6. Say where the rule might stop working, if anywhere.

## How Do You Upgrade Criterion D by One Level?

You upgrade Criterion D by following a fixed sequence on any real-life task: identify the relevant mathematics, choose a method, apply it, justify the accuracy of the result and decide whether the answer makes sense in the context. Students stuck at a 5 often do the first three well and skip the last two.

Consider a task: a taxi charges a fixed fee plus an amount per kilometre. Two journeys are known: 4 km costs 90 and 10 km costs 180. A level-5-style response finds the linear model: cost = 15 × distance + 30, from the gradient (180 − 90)/(10 − 4) = 15 and the intercept 90 − 15 × 4 = 30, and predicts the cost of a 7 km journey as 135. A level-7 or 8 response also states the assumption (the charge is linear, with no waiting time or minimum fare), checks the model against both given points, rounds the answer sensibly for money, and comments on whether the model would stay valid for very long journeys or traffic delays.

The upgrade is in the last two steps. Justifying accuracy means saying how close the answer is likely to be and why. Judging fit means asking whether the result makes sense in the real situation. Both are short, and both are often omitted.

A routine for D:

1. Restate the problem in your own words.
2. List what is given, what is asked and what is assumed.
3. Choose a method and say why.
4. Calculate with the accuracy the context needs.
5. Check the answer against the context: size, units, sense.
6. Say what the model leaves out and whether that matters.

## Why Do Strong Students Still Plateau at a 5?

Strong students plateau at a 5 because the grade rewards consistent performance across criteria and task types, and a student with one weak criterion or one weak type of task keeps pulling the total down. The student can be excellent on tests and still lose levels on investigations and applied tasks.

Three causes recur:

**One criterion is carrying the grade.** A student with A at 7 and C at 4 is often described by parents as "good at maths who loses marks for no reason". The reason is that C is a skill, and a skill needs deliberate practice.

**The task types are unbalanced.** Many students prepare for tests and treat investigations and real-life tasks as one-off pieces. But B and D are assessed mostly on those tasks, and they need their own routines.

**Levels slip under pressure.** A student who writes complete solutions in practice but skips steps in a timed assessment loses C marks in exactly the setting where it counts.

If two or three of these apply, "more practice" is unlikely to help. Targeted changes will.

## How to Audit Your Own Gap in Five Steps

This process can be done in an evening with the last few assessment reports.

1. **Collect the criterion levels** for the last four or five assessed tasks, not just the final grade. Ask the teacher for them if the report shows only totals.
2. **Write them in a grid:** tasks down the side, criteria across the top.
3. **Find the pattern.** Is one criterion consistently lowest? Is one task type consistently weaker? Is a criterion high on tests and low on investigations?
4. **Compute the gap.** Add the most recent consistent levels and subtract from 24 (for a 6) and 28 (for a 7).
5. **Choose the cheapest route.** List the criteria where one level costs the least effort and aim for those first.

Whatever the school, the aim to move from a 5 to a 7 in MYP maths starts with this grid. It often reveals that the "5" is really one persistent weakness. A student who sees "C: 4, 4, 5, 4" across four tasks has a clear target and a measure of success, which is more motivating than "do better at maths".

| Task | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit test 1 | 6 | – | 4 | – |
| Investigation | – | 5 | 4 | – |
| Real-life task | 6 | – | 5 | 5 |
| Unit test 2 | 7 | – | 4 | – |
| Pattern | Secure | Slow | Persistently low | Single data point |

The dashes show where a task does not assess that criterion. The pattern at the bottom says what the family should talk to the teacher about.

## What Does a Realistic Timeline Look Like?

A realistic timeline for moving up one grade is one to two terms, driven by how often the student is assessed on each criterion. Criterion levels are updated through the year, so improvements show up only when the student does a task that assesses the criterion.

:::timeline A realistic upgrade across terms
Weeks 1-2 | Audit the grid, pick two target criteria
Weeks 3-6 | Habit work on C, accuracy work on A
Weeks 7-10 | Practise one B and one D task end to end
Weeks 11-12 | Check against new assessed work with the teacher
Illustrative pacing; it depends on when your school assesses each criterion.
:::

A parent should expect a lag. A student can change habits in a month and see the effect only when the next assessed task is marked. If the school has few assessed tasks per term, the timing may feel slow, and it helps to ask the teacher for feedback on practice tasks marked with the same descriptors. ## Where Does a Tutor Help, and Where Not?

A tutor helps most where the student cannot see their own pattern, where habits need strict and repeated feedback, and where open-ended tasks need guided practice. A tutor adds little if the student already knows exactly which criterion to fix, and the only missing ingredient is time.

The most useful things a tutor does for a student at a 5:

- **Reads the criterion grid** and identifies the cheapest upgrades.
- **Marks work against the descriptors,** not just for correctness, so the student sees where levels are lost.
- **Runs routines** for C, B and D that the student can follow alone.
- **Sets small tasks** and reviews the corrected versions.
- **Reduces prompting** over time, so that the student can produce a level-7 response without help.

What a tutor must not do: write or complete assessed tasks, which would breach school rules and harm the student, or promise a grade.

## Common Mistakes When Trying to Raise an MYP Maths Grade

**Chasing the grade and not the criteria.** A grade is the output. The criteria are what can be changed.

**Doing more of what already works.** A student who is strong in A does not benefit from another hundred procedural questions. The marks are elsewhere.

**Ignoring communication because "the answer was right".** In an assessment framework that rewards reasoning, a right answer with thin working loses levels.

**Treating investigations as one-off tasks.** B and D need routines practised across several tasks.

**Expecting a jump within a fortnight.** Criterion levels move when assessed tasks come back. Plan for a term.

**Not asking the teacher.** The teacher can show which descriptors were missed on a given task. That conversation is free and specific.

**Switching tutors too fast.** A month is a fair test, with evidence reviewed.

**Neglecting the MYP 4 to 5 step.** The decisions about standard or extended mathematics, and the later choice between Analysis and Approaches and Applications and Interpretation in the Diploma Programme, are shaped by these years. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What total do you need for a 7 in MYP maths?

A total of 28 out of 32 across the four criteria, according to the published general boundaries, which translates to no more than four points lost in all. In practice, that means at least three criteria at level 7 or 8 and the fourth no lower than 5 or 6. Confirm the boundaries with your school, since reporting formats and boundaries can change.

### What is the difference between a 5 and a 6 in MYP maths?

A grade 5 covers totals of 19 to 23, and a grade 6 covers 24 to 27, so the difference can be as small as one point. For a student at 21, three extra points across the four criteria are enough, which can come from a one-level upgrade in three of them. The practical difference is usually consistency and the quality of reasoning.

### Which MYP maths criterion is easiest to improve?

Usually Criterion C, Communicating, because the missing marks are often habits: undefined variables, skipped steps, loose notation, no conclusion. These can be changed within weeks with strict feedback. Criterion A follows. Criteria B and D take longer because they depend on ways of thinking, such as generalising and modelling, that need repeated practice on open tasks.

### Does a student need top marks in every criterion to get a 7?

No. A 7 needs a total of 28, so a student can have one weaker criterion if the other three are at 7 or 8. For example, levels of 8, 8, 8 and 4 give 28. The margin is slim, though, so a weak criterion leaves little room for slips elsewhere. Aim for a balanced profile where possible.

### Why is my child's MYP maths grade not improving despite more practice?

Because practice aimed at the wrong criterion does not move the total. A student strong in A who practises more procedures stays at the same A and the same C, B and D. Look at the criterion levels behind the grade, identify the lowest or the most persistent weakness, and practise that. If the pattern is unclear, ask the teacher for the descriptors missed.

### How long does it take to move from a 5 to a 6?

Typically one to two terms, depending on how often the school assesses each criterion. Habits can change in a month, but levels are recorded only when assessed tasks come back. Ask the teacher when the next assessed tasks are, and plan the work so that the improved habits are in place before then, rather than expecting a quick change.

### Can a tutor guarantee a 6 or a 7?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and the final judgement belongs to the school's teachers under IB moderation. A tutor can diagnose the criterion pattern, train the habits that raise levels and show evidence of progress. A promise of a particular grade is a warning sign. IB Gram can arrange a demo session before you commit, subject to tutor availability.

### Is the MYP maths grade an average of test scores?

No. It comes from criterion levels decided by the teacher against the IB's descriptors, based on the body of work, with weight on consistent and recent performance. A percentage on a test is not a criterion level. Ask the school how it records levels and how it arrives at the final criterion level in each strand.

### What if my child is strong in tests but weak in investigations?

That is a common pattern and points to Criterion B and sometimes D. Tests reward recall and procedure, while investigations reward noticing, generalising and justifying. Practise open tasks with the six-step routine for B: notice, tabulate, find the relationship, state a rule, test it on a new case and justify why it holds. A few tasks done end to end usually reveal the gap.

### Do the MYP 5 eAssessment results change the picture?

Where a school offers the optional eAssessment, on-screen examinations and ePortfolios contribute to a separate result, and the school explains how it combines with the criterion-based grade. The criterion habits are the same: clear communication, accurate methods and justified reasoning.
