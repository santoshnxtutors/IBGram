Why students struggle in IB Chemistry is almost never "the subject is too hard". It is one of five repeating faults: a shaky mole concept that corrupts every calculation after it, energetics sign and unit errors, mechanisms learned as pictures instead of electron movement, equilibrium reasoning done by formula instead of by logic, and answers that do not match the command term in the question. Find which of the five applies to your child and the fix is specific, short and teachable.

This guide is for International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) Chemistry students at Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL), and for parents who see a decent effort turning into a disappointing mark. It explains each cause with a worked example, shows what the repair looks like, and ends with a weekly routine you can run without a tutor. It is not about one city or one school, and it does not repeat topic-by-topic revision plans; for the HL-versus-SL choice see [IB Chemistry HL vs SL](/blog/ib-chemistry-tutor-in-gurgaon-hl-vs-sl-support-guide/), and for what tutoring covers topic by topic, see [IB Chemistry HL tutor guidance](/blog/ib-chemistry-hl-tutor-gurgaon/).

## What Makes IB Chemistry Different From School Chemistry?

IB Chemistry asks students to do four things at once that school chemistry often treats separately: recall facts, calculate, explain in sentences, and interpret data they have not seen before. A student who was good at one of these in Class 10 can be strong in a unit test and still lose heavily on a DP paper.

The current DP sciences (first assessed 2025) have a Paper 1 in two parts, 1A multiple choice and 1B data-based questions, and a Paper 2 with short-answer and extended-response questions. The internal assessment (IA) is a scientific investigation worth 20%, marked on four criteria of 6 marks each: Research design, Data analysis, Conclusion and Evaluation, with a maximum of 3,000 words. HL and SL share the structure; HL adds Additional Higher Level (AHL) content. Check the current subject guide for weightings and timings, because the guide is the authority.

:::stats DP Chemistry assessment at a glance
2 | Written papers, each with distinct question types
20% | Internal assessment, a scientific investigation
4 | IA criteria, 6 marks each
3,000 | Maximum words in the IA report
:::

Part of why students struggle in IB Chemistry is the way the content is arranged. It sits under Structure (how matter is built and bonded) and Reactivity (what drives reactions, how much, how fast, how far, and by what mechanism). Struggles tend to be at the joins: a calculation that needs stoichiometry plus energetics, or an explanation that needs bonding plus equilibrium. That is why isolated topic revision often feels fine and mixed papers feel brutal.

## The Five Root Causes, and How to Tell Which One Is Yours

Before the detail, here is a quick map of why students struggle in IB Chemistry. Most students have one dominant cause and one secondary one.

| Cause | What it looks like on a script | Quick self-test |
|---|---|---|
| Weak mole concept | Right idea, wrong number; marks lost in "easy" calculations | Can you convert grams to moles to particles without a formula sheet? |
| Energetics sign and unit slips | Positive where negative expected, kJ and J mixed | Do you decide the sign before you calculate? |
| Mechanisms as pictures | Arrows drawn from the wrong place, product right but arrows wrong | Do your curly arrows start at a lone pair or a bond? |
| Equilibrium by formula | Approximations unjustified, direction of shift guessed | Can you explain a shift without quoting a rule? |
| Command-term mismatch | Long, correct, but off-target answers | Does your answer length match the marks? |

If you cannot pick, collect your last three marked papers and tally where marks were lost. Ten minutes with a highlighter beats a month of general revision.

## Cause 1: The Mole Concept Is Not Secure

The mole concept sits at the base of almost every quantitative question in the course, so a leak there contaminates stoichiometry, gas volumes, solutions, energetics per mole, rates and equilibrium constants. Students often feel they "know" it because they can do textbook examples, then lose marks when the question wraps the mole step inside a longer problem.

### What the failure looks like

A student gets a mass, converts it to moles with the right molar mass, then forgets to use the mole ratio from the balanced equation, or applies it upside down. Another student correctly finds the limiting reagent but then calculates the product from the wrong reactant. Another divides when they should multiply, because they memorised "mass over molar mass" without picturing what moles mean.

### Worked example: a limiting-reagent problem done properly

Magnesium (2.40 g) reacts with 100 cm³ of hydrochloric acid, concentration 0.500 mol dm⁻³. Calculate the volume of hydrogen at standard temperature and pressure (using 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹, as given in the data booklet).

1. Write the balanced equation: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂.
2. Moles of Mg: 2.40 ÷ 24.31 = 0.0987 mol.
3. Moles of HCl: 0.500 × 0.100 dm³ = 0.0500 mol.
4. Compare with the ratio: 0.0987 mol of Mg would need 0.197 mol of HCl, but only 0.0500 mol is present, so HCl is limiting.
5. Moles of H₂ = 0.0500 ÷ 2 = 0.0250 mol.
6. Volume = 0.0250 × 22.7 = 0.568 dm³.

Look at what the structure rewards: a balanced equation first, moles for every species, an explicit comparison, and only then the product. The mistake on this problem is nearly always step 4 omitted: students use the Mg figure because it was given first.

### The fix

Rebuild the idea in three layers, in this order, and do not move on until the earlier layer is automatic.

1. **Picture it.** One mole is a counting unit, like a dozen. Say aloud what a mole of a specific substance weighs and how many particles it holds.
2. **Rehearse the conversion chain** (mass, moles, concentration, volume, particles) with ten quick conversions a day for a week. Speed matters; slow conversions leave no attention for the rest of the problem.
3. **Make the balanced equation a habit.** For every quantitative question, write the equation, then write "moles" under each species. Students who do this stop making ratio errors almost immediately.

Add a check at the end: is the answer plausible? A 0.568 dm³ gas volume from 2.4 g of metal is plausible. 56.8 dm³ should make you stop.

## Cause 2: Energetics Sign, Unit and Interpretation Slips

Energetics is where careful students lose marks through habit, not understanding. Typical losses: giving a positive enthalpy change for a reaction that heated the water, mixing joules and kilojoules, forgetting that bond breaking is endothermic, and using the wrong mass in q = mcΔT.

### Worked example: a calorimetry sign

In an experiment, 50.0 g of water rises in temperature by 6.5 K when 0.0250 mol of a solid dissolves. Use c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

- Heat gained by the water: q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 6.5 = 1358.5 J, about 1.36 kJ.
- The water got warmer, so the reaction released heat: it is exothermic and ΔH is negative.
- ΔH per mole = −1.36 ÷ 0.0250 = −54.3 kJ mol⁻¹.

Three separate marks can disappear here: forgetting to convert to kJ, forgetting to divide by moles, and getting the sign wrong. The sign is the cheapest to protect. Decide it in words before touching the calculator: "temperature up, so exothermic, so negative".

### The fix

- Write the direction of heat flow as a sentence before the calculation.
- Keep a line at the top of every energetics page: "J or kJ? per mole or per gram? sign?"
- When the question links to energy profile diagrams, practise labelling the activation energy and ΔH on a quick sketch. A wrong sketch signals a wrong idea.
- Reread any answer that uses Hess's law or bond enthalpies and ask whether the total makes physical sense. A very exothermic combustion should not give a small positive number.

For students who hit this in the IA, the same habits matter in data processing, where units and propagation of uncertainty show up in the Data analysis criterion. A tutor who teaches the underlying skill can help, but the investigation and report remain the student's own work; the [IB Chemistry IA tutor page](/ib-chemistry-ia-tutor-in-gurgaon/) describes what that support looks like, and the rules for any IA are in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

## Cause 3: Organic Chemistry Learned as Pictures

Organic chemistry feels overwhelming because there seems to be a reaction for everything. The students who handle it well have stopped memorising reactions and begun tracking what electrons do.

### The curly-arrow error

A curly arrow shows the movement of an electron pair. It starts where the pair is, a lone pair or a bond, and ends where the pair goes, an atom or the middle of a new bond. The commonest errors are arrows that start at an atom with no lone pair, arrows that point from the nucleophile's charge sign instead of its lone pair, and arrows missing a half-head or full-head distinction when the question uses radicals.

Consider hydroxide attacking a primary halogenoalkane such as bromoethane. The arrow starts on a lone pair of the oxygen in OH⁻, points to the carbon bonded to bromine, and a second arrow shows the C–Br bond breaking and moving onto bromine as Br⁻. A student who draws the first arrow starting at the minus sign loses the mark even though the product is correct.

### Mechanism type comes from structure

A primary halogenoalkane tends to undergo substitution in one step (SN2); a tertiary one tends to ionise first and then be attacked (SN1). Students who learn which is which as a list forget it under pressure. Students who ask "how crowded is the carbon, and how stable would a carbocation be here?" can rebuild the rule on the spot.

### The fix: reaction maps, not reaction lists

Draw a single page that places functional groups as nodes and reagents plus conditions on the arrows between them: alkene, halogenoalkane, alcohol, aldehyde or ketone, carboxylic acid, ester. A student who can redraw that map from memory can do the multi-step synthesis questions. Then practise mechanisms with one rule: every arrow starts on a pair of electrons.

Keep naming and isomerism on a separate fortnightly drill. They are mechanical, quick to practise and easy marks.

## Cause 4: Equilibrium, Acids and Bases Done by Formula

Reactivity questions on equilibrium and acid-base chemistry combine conceptual reasoning with calculation, and students often have only the second. They can substitute into an expression for Kₐ but cannot say why adding a reactant shifts the system, or why a weak acid does not fully dissociate.

### Worked example: a weak-acid pH

A weak acid HA has concentration 0.100 mol dm⁻³ and Kₐ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. Estimate the pH.

- Kₐ = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA]. With [H⁺] = [A⁻] = x and [HA] ≈ 0.100:
- x² = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100 = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶, so x = 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³.
- pH = −log(1.3 × 10⁻³) ≈ 2.87.

The mark is not only the number. It is the explicit statement that the approximation [HA] ≈ 0.100 is valid because x is tiny compared with 0.100 (about 1.3%). A student who simply writes the answer loses the justification mark; a student who writes the approximation check keeps it.

### Reasoning about shifts

For direction-of-shift questions, resist the rule list. Instead, say what the change does to the rate of the forward and reverse reactions, and what the system does to restore the balance. "Increasing concentration of a reactant increases the rate of the forward reaction at first, so more product forms until the rates match again" is both correct and more robust than "the equilibrium shifts right".

### The fix

- Keep a two-column sheet: "what the quantity means" and "how it relates to the others" for pH, concentration, strength, Kₐ, Kb and Kw.
- Practise explaining every equilibrium change aloud in one sentence, before calculating.
- Always state the approximation and check it.
- Treat titration curves as a story: starting pH, buffer region, equivalence point, excess. Know which species dominates at each stage.

## Cause 5: Command Terms and the Wrong Kind of Answer

A large share of lost marks on DP Chemistry are not about chemistry at all. They come from answering a different question from the one set. IB papers use command terms such as state, outline, explain, suggest, discuss and evaluate, and each asks for a particular depth. The full catalogue with examples across subjects lives in [IB command terms explained with real examples](/blog/ib-command-terms-explained-with-real-examples/); here is how it plays out in Chemistry.

| Command term | What the marker expects | A typical Chemistry slip |
|---|---|---|
| State | A fact, no reason | Writing a paragraph, wasting time |
| Outline | A short account of the main points | Giving only a single word |
| Explain | A cause and effect, using chemistry | Describing the trend without the reason |
| Suggest | A plausible answer using given information | Saying "I don't know" for an unfamiliar context |
| Compare | Similarities and differences, both sides | Describing one substance only |

### The mark-count rule

Count the marks and match the number of distinct points. A two-mark "explain" needs two linked statements: usually an observation or fact, then the chemical reason. A one-mark "state" needs one. Students who write four sentences for one mark lose time they need for the calculation questions.

### A model explanation

"Explain why boiling point increases down group 17." A weak answer: "Because the molecules get bigger." A strong answer: "The halogens are simple molecular, so boiling requires overcoming intermolecular forces. Larger molecules have more electrons, so London dispersion forces are stronger. More energy is needed to separate them, so the boiling point increases." The strong answer names the particle type, the force, the cause and the consequence. That chain of four is what an "explain" question rewards.

## The Fix, Step by Step: A Repair Process for Any Weak Topic

Why students struggle in IB Chemistry matters less than what they do next. The routine below works whichever of the five causes applies. It is short enough to remember and strict enough to work.

1. **Name the weakness precisely.** Not "Chemistry" but "limiting-reagent problems under timed conditions".
2. **Rebuild the concept** using one reliable source (class notes, your textbook, your teacher), until you can explain it in your own words without notes.
3. **Do three easy questions** to confirm the foundation holds.
4. **Raise the difficulty** gradually towards DP exam style. If you stall, go back a step, not forward.
5. **Mark and diagnose every error** in a log: topic, what went wrong, why, and the correct approach.
6. **Retest after a few days** with similar questions and no notes. If you can do them unaided, the repair is genuine.

:::steps A six-step repair loop for one weak topic
Name | One specific weakness
Rebuild | Explain it in your own words
Practise | Easy, then exam-style
Diagnose | Log each error and its cause
Retest | Similar questions, days later
:::

### What a good error log looks like

An error log is only useful if it records causes, not corrections. Compare two entries for the same mistake.

- Weak: "Q14, got 0.28, correct answer 0.56. Redo."
- Useful: "Q14, stoichiometry. Used moles of the excess reagent. Cause: did not compare with the equation ratio. Next time: write the limiting-reagent comparison as an explicit step."

Review the log weekly. Patterns appear within a month: students often discover that half their lost marks come from two habits.

## Data Questions, Graphs and Practical Skills

Paper 1B and parts of Paper 2 present unfamiliar data, a graph, a table, a spectrum or a set of experimental results, and ask for interpretation. Struggling students describe what they see; strong students say what it means chemically and what limits its reliability.

Use a fixed sequence for every data question:

1. Identify the variables and units on the axes.
2. State the trend in one sentence, with numbers from the data.
3. Link the trend to a chemical idea, such as rate, equilibrium or bond strength.
4. Comment on reliability: anomalies, uncertainty, number of repeats, or a limitation of the method.

Practise it on graphs from a past paper, then on graphs from your own practicals. The same mental sequence appears in the IA's Data analysis and Evaluation criteria, so the habit pays twice. Remember that the investigation is individual work; a tutor may teach graphing and uncertainty skills, but no one may write or edit the report for the student.

## A Weekly Routine That Replaces Aimless Revision

Hours matter less than structure. A focused session with active problem-solving beats a long passive read. This weekly pattern fits a student with about five to six hours of independent Chemistry time and can be scaled.

| Day | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | One weak concept rebuilt | A half-page explanation in your own words |
| Tuesday | Calculations on that topic | Ten questions, setup shown |
| Wednesday | Data and application questions | Two data sets, four-step method |
| Thursday | Error log review | Updated log, three causes named |
| Friday | Mixed practice | One timed section of a past paper |
| Saturday | Past-paper questions with markscheme | Marked and diagnosed |
| Sunday | Short recall plus plan for next week | Flashcards, next week's weak topic |

A good 60-minute session might spend 15 minutes on the concept, 30 on problems, 10 on marking and 5 on the error log. As the May session approaches, increase timed work. For the final stretch, see [the last 30 days before IB exams](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/), and for the wider preparation structure, the [IB exam preparation guide](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/).

### Timing and pacing

Some students know the chemistry and still run out of time. Practise under timed conditions and track which questions overran, which were skipped unnecessarily, and where calculation errors appeared under pressure. The aim is a realistic pacing plan, such as moving on from a stuck question after a set time and returning, built well before the exam.

## Should I Choose HL or SL, and Does Struggling Mean I Chose Wrong?

Struggling in a topic does not mean the level was a mistake. HL covers the same structure as SL with additional AHL content and more demanding application, so some strain is built in. A student whose difficulties are the five causes above will struggle at either level, and a level change would not solve the underlying faults. A student whose mathematics is weak, who finds even SL calculations draining and who needs HL only for a university requirement, has a more serious conversation to have with the school's coordinator. ## When Should Someone Get Outside Help With IB Chemistry?

Outside help makes sense when a student has applied the repair loop for several weeks and the same errors persist, when the student cannot tell which cause applies, or when the school's support does not reach the specific problem. A specialist can diagnose quickly, because they have seen the same five faults across many scripts.

Be realistic about what a tutor does. A good one asks the student to solve a problem aloud, watches where it stalls, assigns three well-chosen questions, and checks the error log. They do not do the thinking, and they do not write or edit IA reports. If the student does most of the talking by the third session, the tutoring is working.

Not every student needs a tutor. If the problem is a single topic, a teacher's help session and a fortnight of the repair loop may be enough. If the problem is general weakness in the mathematics behind chemistry, a maths tutor may help more than a chemistry specialist. ## What Parents Can Do Without Teaching Chemistry

A parent does not need to know what a curly arrow is. Three habits help.

- Ask your child to teach you one idea for five minutes each week. If they cannot, the concept is not secure, which is useful information.
- Look at the error log together once a fortnight. You are checking that causes are being written down, not that chemistry is being understood.
- Protect sleep before a Chemistry paper. Calculation accuracy and the ability to read an unfamiliar data question both drop sharply when a student is tired.

## Common Misconceptions Worth Correcting Early

Beyond the five causes, a few specific beliefs cause recurring damage.

**"Chemistry is a memory subject."** Some recall is needed, but the high-mark questions test reasoning with given information. Students who memorise harder and reason less typically plateau.

**"If I get the final answer right I get the marks."** Method and justification marks exist in calculations. A correct number with no working is a risk, and a wrong number with clear working still earns marks.

**"Past papers alone will fix it."** A past paper reveals where marks are lost; it does not repair the cause. Without the error log and the repair loop, repeated past papers rehearse the same faults.

**"I understand it when I read it."** Recognition is easier than recall. The test is whether you can start a blank page and produce the explanation.

**"A tutor will fix it by explaining again."** Explanation helps only if the student then solves problems alone. Understanding that has never been tested is not yet understanding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do students struggle in IB Chemistry?

Most struggle because of a specific, fixable fault: a weak mole concept, energetics sign and unit errors, mechanisms learned as pictures, equilibrium done by formula, or answers that ignore the command term. The subject is demanding but not unreasonable. Identifying which fault applies, by tallying marks lost across recent papers, is the first step to repairing it.

### Is IB Chemistry harder at HL than SL?

HL is harder because it adds Additional Higher Level content and expects more demanding application, but the assessment structure is shared. The same faults appear at both levels. If you struggle at SL because of calculation habits or weak explanations, moving level will not solve it. Check the current subject guide and your coordinator's advice before changing.

### How can I improve my IB Chemistry grade quickly?

Start with an error log from your last three papers and find the two habits costing the most marks. Fix those first, usually the mole step and the command-term mismatch. Quick does not mean overnight: a few weeks of a focused repair loop usually changes the pattern, and no one can guarantee a particular grade.

### Why do I lose marks in calculations when I know the theory?

The usual causes are skipping the balanced equation, mixing units, using the wrong quantity in a formula, or forgetting the final step such as dividing by moles. Write the equation, list moles under each species, state the sign or unit before you calculate, and check the answer for plausibility. Speed with conversions frees attention for the rest.

### How should I revise organic chemistry for IB?

Use reaction maps and mechanisms, not reaction lists. Draw a page linking functional groups through reagents and conditions, and practise mechanisms with the rule that every curly arrow starts on an electron pair. Keep naming and isomerism as separate short drills. Rebuild the maps from memory weekly until you can do it without notes.

### Do past papers fix IB Chemistry problems?

They expose problems but do not repair them. Doing paper after paper without diagnosing why marks were lost rehearses the same faults. Use papers after you have rebuilt the weak concept, mark them against the markscheme, log every error with a cause, and retest the topic a few days later with similar questions.

### How many hours a week should I study Chemistry?

Quality matters more than hours. A focused 60-minute session with concept review, problem-solving, marking and an error-log update usually beats several hours of passive reading. Many DP students plan several such sessions a week and add timed paper practice before exams. Match the load to your other subjects and protect sleep.

### How do I stop running out of time in Chemistry papers?

Practise under timed conditions and track which questions overran. Decide in advance how long you will spend before moving on, and return to skipped questions at the end. Match answer length to the marks: a one-mark state question does not need a paragraph. Pacing is a skill that improves with deliberate practice.

### When is a Chemistry tutor worth it?

A tutor is worth it when regular independent practice has not changed the pattern of lost marks, or when you cannot tell which cause applies. A good tutor diagnoses quickly and teaches the skill, not the answers. If a school help session and the repair loop are working, you may not need one.

### Can a tutor help with my Chemistry IA?

A tutor can teach the underlying skills, such as graphing, uncertainty and structuring an evaluation, and can question your reasoning or point at a criterion. The investigation and the report must be your own work, and no tutor may write or edit your submission. Ask any tutor to confirm this before you begin.
