A good home tutor does not walk through the textbook chapter by chapter. They read the student's recent work, choose the single most valuable thing to fix this week, teach it, watch the student attempt a fresh question, and leave a small task for the days in between.

## What Does IGCSE Maths Home Tuition in Gurgaon Actually Involve?

IGCSE maths home tuition in Gurgaon involves a tutor visiting the student's home on a fixed weekly slot, usually for sixty to ninety minutes, to teach, test and correct the student's work against the 0580 or 4MA1 syllabus. Each lesson combines three things: explanation of one priority topic, supervised practice on fresh questions, and review of the student's marked work from the previous week.

What it is not: homework supervision, a chapter-by-chapter repeat of what the school teacher said, or a place where the tutor completes assessed work. The distinction matters, because families sometimes expect the first and tutors sometimes drift into the second. A lesson should leave the student able to do something they could not do an hour earlier, and should leave a record of what that was.

The value of the home setting is practical. The tutor sees the pencil, not just the screen: how the student sets out a line of algebra, where the pen hesitates, which step is skipped. In mathematics, the working is the evidence. A tutor sitting beside a student for sixty minutes sees more of it than a teacher marking a finished page can.

:::steps The shape of one home session
Review | Last week's task and error log
Teach | One priority topic with examples
Attempt | Fresh questions, student working alone
Correct | Mark together and name each slip
Set | One small task before the next visit
:::

## Who Benefits Most From Home Lessons, and Who Does Not Need Them?

Home lessons benefit most the student who understands the idea when shown but cannot start a fresh question alone, the student whose working is untidy or incomplete, and the student whose confidence has dropped after a weak test. For these students, a tutor at the next chair catches the slip as it happens, which is when correction sticks.

Home lessons add little for a student who is secure, keeping pace and already marking their own work against mark schemes. They are also a poor fit if no quiet table is available at a regular time, or if the student will not do any work between visits. A weekly hour cannot carry a course on its own.

Three student types show up repeatedly in Gurgaon:

- **The Extended candidate with a shaky Grade 8 base.** Capable, ambitious, losing marks across algebra because fractions, negatives and bracket expansion are not automatic. Needs foundations repaired before new content.
- **The Core candidate who wants security.** Content is manageable, but exam technique and accuracy are inconsistent. Needs structured practice and strict marking.
- **The Edexcel Higher student near a mock.** Needs a plan that fits the weeks left, and a tutor who knows the 4MA1 paper structure.

If you are not sure which describes your child, the first session is designed to find out.

## What Happens in the First Session?

The first session is a diagnostic, not a lesson. It should leave the tutor with a short, specific list of where marks are being lost, and leave the student having done real work, not having been told what is wrong.

A well-run first session has four parts:

1. **A conversation of about ten minutes** about the syllabus (0580 Core or Extended, 4MA1 Foundation or Higher), the school's tier decision, the next assessment date and what the student finds hardest.
2. **A short diagnostic task of about twenty-five minutes,** mixing topics from the previous two years: fractions, percentages, linear equations, brackets, simple geometry and one multi-step problem. The student works alone, writing everything.
3. **Marking together for about fifteen minutes.** The tutor asks the student to explain their own working before saying anything about it.
4. **A plan of about ten minutes:** two or three priorities, the frequency, the first task, and the date of a review.

Bring recent school tests, the marked ones, to this session, not just the textbook. Those papers show the tutor which marks went and why. A tutor who does not ask for them is working blind.

The output of the first session, within a day, should be a short written note: the priorities, the evidence for each and the plan. If the note says "revise all topics", the diagnostic was not specific enough.

## What Does a Typical Sixty-Minute Session Look Like?

A typical sixty-minute session spends about ten minutes on review, twenty on teaching one topic, twenty on independent attempt and correction, and ten on setting the next task. The ratio shifts with the student, but the proportions protect against the most common failure: a lesson in which the tutor talks for fifty minutes and the student writes for ten.

| Minutes | Activity | What the tutor is watching |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | Review last week's task and one error-log entry | Did the fix hold? |
| 10 to 30 | Teach the priority topic with two worked examples | Can the student predict the next line? |
| 30 to 50 | Student attempts fresh questions alone, then correction | Where does the pencil stop? |
| 50 to 60 | Set the next task; record what was covered | Is the task small enough to finish? |

A ninety-minute block, common before a mock, adds a timed section: ten to fifteen minutes under exam conditions, marked straight afterwards. A ninety-minute session with no independent attempt is a lecture, and the student will not retain it.

The rule inside the session is that the student writes more than the tutor. If the tutor's pen is moving most of the time, the student is watching mathematics, not doing it.

## A Worked Example: One Session on Factorising Quadratics

Abstract descriptions of a lesson are easy to nod at. Here is what one actually looks like, for an Extended student who can expand brackets but freezes when asked to factorise.

**Review (10 minutes).** The tutor asks the student to expand (x + 3)(x − 5) from memory. The student writes x² − 5x + 3x − 15 = x² − 2x − 15. Correct. This is the reverse of what comes next.

**Teach (20 minutes).** The tutor writes x² − 2x − 15 and asks, "What two numbers multiply to −15 and add to −2?" The student tries 3 and 5, then notices the signs: +3 and −5 give a product of −15 and a sum of −2. The factorised form is (x + 3)(x − 5). The tutor then gives a second example, x² + 7x + 12, and asks the student to say aloud the pairs of factors of 12 before choosing. A third example has a coefficient, 2x² + 7x + 3, which requires splitting the middle term; the tutor models it once and then removes the prompts.

**Attempt (20 minutes).** Fresh questions: factorise x² − 9x + 20; solve x² + x − 12 = 0; factorise 3x² − 10x + 8. The tutor watches without speaking. On the second, the student correctly factorises to (x + 4)(x − 3) but writes the solutions as x = 4 and x = 3, forgetting that the roots are the opposite signs. The tutor does not correct it yet.

**Correct (as part of the same block).** The student substitutes x = 4 back into the equation: 16 + 4 − 12 = 8, not 0. The error is found by the student, not the tutor. The fix is to write the factor, set it to zero and solve in a separate line: x + 4 = 0 gives x = −4.

**Set (10 minutes).** Six questions, mixed difficulty, including two that need rearranging before factorising. The student must check each root by substitution.

Notice what happened. The tutor taught one idea and had the student find their own error. The slip with the sign of the root is one of the ten that cost marks most often, and it was caught in the room, not on a mock paper. ## How Is the Week Designed Around One Visit?

A weekly visit works because of what happens between visits. The week is built around the session, not the other way around.

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| Visit day | Tutor session; student leaves with a task of 30 to 40 minutes |
| Next day | Student completes the task while the lesson is fresh |
| Midweek | A short retrieval session, closed book, 15 to 20 minutes |
| Later in week | School homework and corrections to the error log |
| Day before the visit | Student sends a photograph or brings the corrected work |

The between-visit task has one rule: it must be small enough to finish. A set of thirty questions that is half done teaches the student that tasks are optional. Six well-chosen questions, finished and checked, teach that they are not.

The retrieval session is the part families skip, and it is among the most effective things in the week. The student closes the book and writes down the method for last visit's topic, then checks. Gaps are cheaper to fix three days later than three weeks later.

Parents sometimes ask whether twice a week is better. For most students, no. Two visits without enough follow-up work double the hours and halve the value of each. The exception is a defined window, such as the fortnight before a mock, when a second weekly session can be justified for a limited time.

## In What Order Should Topics Be Taught?

Topic order at home should follow the student's gaps first and the syllabus second. A tutor who follows the textbook sequence regardless of the diagnostic is wasting early sessions. That said, a sensible default order exists, and it follows how mathematics builds.

The Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus is organised into number, algebra and graphs, coordinate geometry, geometry, mensuration, trigonometry, transformations and vectors, probability and statistics. Edexcel 4MA1 covers a similar territory under its own headings. A sensible ordering for home lessons runs like this.

| Block | Topics | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Number foundations | Fractions, decimals, percentages, ratio, indices, standard form | Everything else uses these |
| 2. Algebra | Expressions, equations, inequalities, factorising, simultaneous equations | Largest source of marks and of lost marks |
| 3. Graphs and coordinates | Straight lines, gradients, quadratics, curves | Needs algebra to be secure |
| 4. Geometry and mensuration | Angles, circle theorems, area, volume, similarity | Needs algebra for unknowns |
| 5. Trigonometry | Right-angled, sine and cosine rules, bearings | Needs geometry and algebra |
| 6. Transformations and vectors | Reflections, rotations, vectors | Often a quick win at Extended |
| 7. Probability and statistics | Probability, trees, averages, graphs | Mix with earlier blocks |

The order is a default, not a rule. A student who is secure in number but weak in trigonometry should get trigonometry first. A student who has just been taught circle theorems at school should have home sessions reinforce those while they are fresh.

Two features of the order are worth keeping. Algebra threads through every block, so any session on geometry or graphs should include a few algebra questions as retrieval. And probability and statistics, which many students find easier, should be mixed in early as relief and confidence, not saved for the end.

The twelve-week structure that surrounds this, including full-paper weeks and the final fortnight, is laid out in [the IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/). The home sessions below fit inside it.

## How Do Calculator and Non-Calculator Papers Change Home Lessons?

Home lessons should treat the non-calculator and calculator papers as two different skills, because the marks are lost in different places. In Cambridge 0580, Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator), and Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Edexcel 4MA1, for either tier, has two calculator papers.

On a non-calculator paper, the marks depend on hand fluency: fraction arithmetic, exact values, surds, algebraic manipulation, and setting out. A tutor at home can run drills with a pencil only, and see the exact moment the student reaches for a machine in their head.

On a calculator paper, the marks depend on efficient use of the calculator and on interpretation: entering with brackets, working in the right angle mode, and estimating first. A tutor can watch the student's keystrokes, which a teacher marking a page cannot.

A good rhythm is to alternate. One week's task is pencil-only, the next is calculator-based, and every timed section is labelled with which kind of paper it simulates. A student preparing for 4MA1 should practise both calculator papers, but still needs hand fluency for the working that earns method marks.

## What Does the Parent See After Each Session?

A parent should see evidence, not reassurance. After each session there should be something concrete: a short note, a marked sheet or a folder, so that progress is visible without the parent needing to understand the mathematics.

| Evidence | What it shows | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Session note (3 to 5 lines) | Topic covered, one thing done well, one thing to fix | Every session |
| Marked task sheet | The student's own working, with corrections | Every session |
| Error log | Recurring causes of lost marks | Reviewed weekly |
| Timed section result | Accuracy under time pressure | Fortnightly in later weeks |
| Review summary | Comparison with the first diagnostic | After 3 or 4 sessions |

The session note is the minimum. If a tutor says "we did algebra today" and nothing else, you have no way to know whether anything changed. A better note says: "Factorising quadratics with leading coefficient 1 is now secure. With a leading coefficient, still prompted. Root signs: slip corrected on the second attempt. Task: six mixed questions, check each by substitution."

A parent who does not know the maths can still ask three questions of the student: "What did you learn today?", "What did you have to work out yourself?" and "What are you meant to do before next time?" The answers reveal more than whether the tutor was pleasant.

## How Should the Home Be Set Up for a Maths Lesson?

The home set-up is simple, and getting it wrong undermines a good tutor. What the lesson needs is a flat table, good light, a quiet room and a few materials.

- **A table and two chairs,** not a sofa and a coffee table. Mathematics is written, and posture matters over an hour.
- **A quiet room at the same time each week,** with a door that can be closed, and a plan for visitors and phones. A television in the next room is enough to halve the value of the hour.
- **Plain paper and a pencil,** plus pens in two colours for corrections. Squared paper is helpful for graphs.
- **The correct calculator,** the model the student will use in the exam, in the right mode. Do not lend a different one for the lesson.
- **A geometry set** with a protractor, compasses and ruler, for construction and bearings.
- **The syllabus and mark schemes,** printed or open on a device, so the tutor can point to how marks are awarded.
- **A small whiteboard,** if available, for quick working that need not be kept.

Parents ask whether they should sit in. The usual answer is to be in the house, available, and not in the room. A parent in the room often changes the student's behaviour, and the student may be reluctant to make mistakes in front of them, which is exactly what a lesson needs. Check in at the end, for the last five minutes, when the tutor can summarise.

## How Do Core, Extended and Additional Mathematics Change the Sessions?

The tier changes the content and the pace, not the structure of the session. The same shape (review, teach, attempt, correct, set) works at every level.

**Core.** Content is narrower, and the ceiling is grade C. Sessions spend more time on accuracy, setting out and the non-calculator skills that carry the marks. A Core student who is strong can still be asked whether Extended is an option, but that is the school's decision.

**Extended.** Content includes more demanding algebra, functions, trigonometry and vectors. Sessions often go deeper into multi-step problems and spend time on questions that combine topics. A tutor should check early that the foundations from Grade 8 are secure, because gaps there compound.

**Edexcel Higher (4MA1).** The structure is two papers with calculators, so home sessions place extra weight on accurate calculator entry, setting out and the Higher tier's algebra. The detail of the tier is covered in [the 4MA1 Higher tier guide for Gurugram](/blog/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-a-4ma1-higher-tier-tutor-in-gurugram-2026-guide/).

**Additional Mathematics 0606.** A separate syllabus with no tiers and two calculator papers, usually taken alongside 0580 Extended by students heading for IB Mathematics AA HL or A Level Mathematics. It needs its own sessions, because the content (functions, logarithms and exponentials, differentiation, integration, series) is distinct. The decision whether to take it is explained in [the IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

## How Do You Know After a Month Whether Home Tuition Is Working?

After a month you should be able to see four things: a smaller set of repeated errors, work that the student begins without prompting, a trail of attempts and corrections, and a plan for the next four weeks. Marks may lag, particularly if the next test covers material the tutor has not reached.

A concrete way to check: repeat part of the first diagnostic in session four. If a question the student froze on in week one is now done correctly, with working, the lessons are doing something. If it is still frozen, the tutor should be able to say why and what will change.

Look also at the student. A student who says "I know what to check now" is describing a habit. A student who says "the tutor explained it well" is describing the tutor. Habits are what carry into the exam.

If after a month the student has not completed the tasks, the first conversation should be with the student and the family about time and motivation, not with the tutor about content. If tasks are done and nothing has changed, the plan or the fit is likely wrong. At that point it is reasonable to ask for a different approach or a different match.

## What a Tutor Should Not Do at Home

Some boundaries protect the student and the tutor.

- **No completing homework or assessed work.** The student does the work. The tutor teaches the method and reviews the result.
- **No promises of grades.** No tutor can guarantee a grade.
- **No lessons without a plan.** Showing up and asking "what do you have today?" every week is a sign that no diagnosis was done.
- **No long cancellations without notice,** and no habitual lateness.
- **No private communication that excludes the family** for a student who is a minor. A parent should know how to reach the tutor and what is said about progress.

The same applies to the family: respect the slot, keep the room quiet and ensure the student has done the task. A lesson is a two-way arrangement.

## Mistakes Families Make With Home Maths Tuition

**Starting without sharing the school's marked papers.** The tutor then has to guess at the problem.

**Treating the tutor as the whole plan.** One hour a week supports the student's independent work and cannot replace it.

**Booking too many sessions too soon.** More hours without follow-up lower the value of each.

**Skipping the review.** An arrangement without a review date continues by inertia.

**Judging by the student's mood after the lesson.** A student can enjoy a lesson and learn little, or find it hard and learn a lot.

**Changing tutors at the first difficulty.** A month is the minimum fair test, with evidence reviewed.

**Ignoring past papers until late.** Home sessions should include exam-style questions from the start of term, with timed sections later. The use of past papers is the subject of [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many sessions a week do I need for IGCSE maths at home?

One session a week, with independent follow-up, is enough for most students. Add a second weekly session for a defined window, such as the fortnight before a mock, then step back. Two sessions every week without matching practice usually halves the value of each hour. Review after three or four lessons and adjust on evidence, not habit.

### How long should each home maths session be?

Sixty minutes suits most students, and ninety minutes suits a student who needs a timed section or is close to an exam. Longer is not automatically better. Attention often drops in the second half of a long block, so a structured sixty minutes can beat an unstructured ninety. Ask the tutor how the time will be divided.

### Can a home tutor teach both Cambridge 0580 and Edexcel 4MA1?

Some can, but check. The two syllabuses overlap heavily in content, and differ in paper structure and tiers: 0580 has Core and Extended with calculator and non-calculator papers, while 4MA1 has Foundation and Higher with calculator papers. Ask the tutor which they have taught recently and how they would prepare your child for the specific papers.

### What should I prepare before the first session?

Gather the student's last few marked tests and school reports, the syllabus code and tier, the school's assessment calendar and any mock results. Choose a quiet room, a table and the student's own calculator. Tell the tutor about the gate or entry procedure. Ask the student to note which topics they find hardest, in their own words.

### Should I sit in on the lesson?

Usually be in the house and not in the room. A parent in the room can make a student reluctant to make and show mistakes, which a lesson needs. Check in during the last five minutes, when the tutor can summarise what was done and what the task is. If your child is young or anxious, agree an arrangement that suits them.

### Is home tuition better than online for IGCSE maths?

Not always. Home lessons let the tutor watch the pencil and the working at close range, which suits students whose setting out is a problem. Online lessons widen the choice of tutor and remove travel, and work well with a pen tablet and a saved whiteboard. Many families combine both. The choice depends on the student, the tutor and the schedule.

### How do I know the tutor is teaching the right topics?

Ask for the plan. It should follow from a diagnostic, and it should name the priorities and the order. Check that the topics match the syllabus code and the tier, and that the plan responds to the school's calendar. If the tutor teaches whatever the school is teaching that week with no diagnostic, the lessons may be homework help, not a plan.

### My child is in Core. Is home tuition still worth it?

It can be, if accuracy and exam technique are the issue. Core students often lose marks through setting out, calculator use and misreading rather than missing content, and a tutor can train those habits. If the student is secure and the main worry is the ceiling of grade C, raise tier with the school rather than with a tutor.

### What if my child does not do the follow-up tasks?

Address it directly, since a weekly hour cannot carry the course. Talk to your child about time and motivation, shrink the task until it is finishable, and fix a regular slot. Tell the tutor so the plan can adjust. If tasks stay undone after a month, pausing lessons to fix the routine may be wiser than continuing.

### Can a tutor guarantee a grade in IGCSE maths?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and any promise of one is a warning sign. Results depend on the starting point, attendance, independent practice and the school's assessment. A good tutor can define a process, show evidence of progress and adjust the plan. IB Gram can arrange a demo session before you commit, subject to tutor availability.
