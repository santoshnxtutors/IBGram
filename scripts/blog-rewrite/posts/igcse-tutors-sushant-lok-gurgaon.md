## What Should You Do in the First 48 Hours After a Weak IGCSE Mock?

In the first 48 hours after a weak mock, do three things and nothing else: get the marked paper back, read it without arguing, and write down the raw marks per question and per topic. Do not start a new revision timetable, buy new books or change tutors in the heat of the result. Emotion at this stage produces plans that are too big and abandoned within a week. The paper itself is the asset.

Ask the school for the marked scripts and, if possible, the mark scheme, not just the total. A grade or a percentage tells you almost nothing. A script tells you whether marks went on a handful of long questions, on a scatter of small slips or on a whole section that was left blank. Schools differ in how they release mock papers, so ask the subject teacher politely and specifically: "Could we see the marked script so that we can work out where the marks were lost?" Most teachers are glad to be asked.

Then sit with the student for 30 minutes, with no commentary, and list three things for each subject: the questions that lost the most marks, the questions that were blank and the questions where the student says "I knew this". The third list is often the longest and the most valuable, because those are the marks that will return fastest once the cause is found.

### What not to do on day one

Do not compare the result with a friend's. Do not cancel the weekend. Do not announce a punishment or a reward. Do not tell the student that the grade "does not matter" while also asking for a plan; mixed messages increase anxiety. A neutral tone, such as "this is information; let us use it", works better than disappointment or false cheer.

:::steps The first 48 hours after a mock
Collect | Marked scripts and the mark schemes
Read | Raw marks per question, no commentary
List | Biggest losses, blanks, and "I knew this"
Pause | No new timetable until the causes are sorted
:::

## How Do You Sort Lost Marks Into Causes?

You sort lost marks by asking, for each one, what kind of loss it was. Without this step, "revise more" becomes the only advice, and it fixes almost nothing. Six categories cover nearly every lost mark on an IGCSE script, and each has a different cure.

| Cause of loss | What it looks like | The cure |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge gap | Student could not start; blank or wrong from the first line | Relearn the topic, then retrieval practice |
| Method error | Right topic, wrong or incomplete procedure | Worked examples, then similar questions, marked |
| Misread question | Answered a different question from the one asked | Underline the command word and the data before writing |
| Command-word mismatch | Described when asked to explain; stated when asked to calculate | Learn what each command word demands in each subject |
| Careless slip | Sign, unit, rounding, copying error | A fixed checking routine in the last minutes |
| Time | Questions at the end blank or rushed | Timed sections; learn to leave and return |

A useful way to run this is a simple tally. For each subject, go through every lost mark on the script and write one of the six labels beside it, then count. Most students find that two causes account for more than half of their losses. A student who finds that 40% of lost marks in Mathematics Paper 4 are misreads and slips does not need three new chapters; they need a routine. A student whose losses in Chemistry are mostly knowledge gaps in stoichiometry needs a topic repair first and past papers second.

### A worked example of the tally

Consider a typical Grade 10 student in Extended Mathematics who sat a mock and received a result well below target. The loss tally might show: 9 marks lost to blank questions at the end of the calculator paper (time), 7 marks lost to correct methods with arithmetic or rounding slips (careless), 6 marks lost on trigonometry and vectors (knowledge), 5 marks lost to missing method lines on multi-step problems (method presentation) and 4 marks to misreading what was asked. The picture is not "weak at maths". It is a time problem, a slip problem and two topic gaps. Each has a different plan, and the whole plan is smaller than the student feared.

This is illustrative, not a real student's result, but the pattern is common: the headline mark is dominated by a few causes that can be named and trained.

## Which Papers Does a Mock Usually Test, and What Does That Mean for Drilling?

A mock usually mirrors the structure of the real papers, so the first step in drilling is to know the structure of your own syllabus. Check the paper codes against the current syllabus and your school's entry. A few examples that matter for drilling:

- **Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams):** Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher, with two calculator papers per tier.
- **Cambridge IGCSE sciences (examined from 2023):** multiple choice, theory and practical assessment, weighted 30%, 50% and 20%. Core candidates sit Paper 1 and Paper 3; Extended candidates sit Paper 2 and Paper 4; practical assessment is through Paper 5 (practical test) or Paper 6 (alternative to practical), chosen by the school.
- **Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455:** Paper 1 multiple choice (30%) and Paper 2 structured questions with data (70%).
- **Cambridge IGCSE Business Studies 0450:** Paper 1 short answer and data response, and Paper 2 case study, 50% each.
- **Cambridge IGCSE Computer Science 0478:** Paper 1 Computer systems and Paper 2 Algorithms, programming and logic.

What this means for drilling: a student should practise the papers they will actually sit, in the form they will sit them. A student who finds the non-calculator paper weakest should do not "more maths" but more non-calculator work, under timing. A science student who lost marks on multiple choice needs a different drill from one who lost marks on extended theory answers. The paper is the unit of practice.

Many Indian schools enter students for the Cambridge February/March series, which exists specifically for India and lets Grade 10 results arrive in time for Class 11 and IB DP or A Level admissions. Others enter for May/June or October/November, and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE series run in January and May/June. Schools decide which series they enter students for. Ask the school which series your child is entered for, then count the weeks remaining. A mock in November for a February/March series leaves a different amount of time from the same mock for a May/June series.

## How Should You Drill Past Papers After a Weak Mock?

You should drill past papers by targeting the losses, not by completing more papers. The common failure is to sit paper after paper, check the total and move on, which produces a series of scores and very little learning. Past papers are valuable because each question comes with a mark scheme that shows exactly what earns credit. The value is released only when the student studies the scheme.

The drilling cycle for each weak area has five steps.

1. **Pick the weak area from the tally.** For example, calculator paper time management, vectors or rate-of-reaction calculations.
2. **Select eight to twelve past-paper questions on it**, across several years, from the same paper type.
3. **Attempt them without notes**, at exam pace, writing full working.
4. **Mark with the official scheme**, awarding marks only where the scheme says a mark exists, and tag each loss with a cause from the table above.
5. **Redo any question that lost more than one mark**, two days later, from a blank page.

Step 5 is the one that is skipped, and it is the one that locks in the gain. A question corrected today and not retried is a question the student will probably lose again.

### How many past papers are enough?

There is no magic number. A reasonable pattern in the recovery window is a handful of full papers per major subject, timed and marked, plus many topic-grouped question sets. The point is to cover each paper type at least twice and to leave time to correct. If a student has sat 15 papers and corrected none, they are in a worse place than a student who has sat six and corrected each one thoroughly. The sibling guide on [IGCSE maths past papers](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/) covers the maths side in more depth, including when to start and how to use examiner reports.

### Which years should you use?

Use recent papers for your syllabus code first, because syllabuses change and older papers may include content that is no longer examined or omit content that now is. Check that the paper code matches. Keep a small number of the most recent papers for a final, fully timed rehearsal in the last fortnight, and use older ones for topic drilling.

:::bars Where a recovery hour should go (an illustrative balance)
Marking and correcting | 100 | most valuable
Targeted topic questions | 80 | the weak areas
Timed sections | 60 | build pace
Full papers | 40 | rehearsal, not routine
Rereading notes | 15 | least return
A judgement about balance, not a research finding: marking and redoing earn more than sitting more papers.
:::

## How Do You Read a Mark Scheme the Way an Examiner Does?

Reading a mark scheme properly is a skill, and most students have never been taught it. A scheme is not an answer key; it is a list of the points that earn marks, with notes about acceptable alternatives and common errors. Reading it well tells a student not just the right answer but what the examiner is looking for, and often how many separate points a question expects.

Three habits help. First, count the marks: a 3-mark "explain" question typically wants three distinct points, so a student who gives one point three times has earned one mark. Second, notice the wording of the scheme: in the sciences, specific terms are often required, and a paraphrase may not earn the mark. Third, compare your answer line by line with the scheme and highlight the first point where you diverged. That divergence is where to focus.

In mathematics, schemes typically show method marks and accuracy marks separately, which is why working matters; a correct answer with no visible method may earn fewer marks than a method with a slip. Teaching a student this changes how they write. In English and humanities subjects, the scheme describes levels of response. Reading a level descriptor against your own essay and asking "which level is this, and what is missing from the next one?" is more useful than rereading the essay for errors.

### Examiner reports

Cambridge publishes examiner reports for past series, and these are a quiet source of high-value information. A report says which questions candidates found hard, which mistakes were common and what better answers did. Reading the report for the paper you have just sat shows you whether your errors are typical. It also shows you the examiner's own words for what was missing, which is the language you want to use in answers. Ask your teacher or tutor to point you to the reports for your syllabus, or check the board's official channels.

## How Does Recovery Differ by Subject?

Recovery looks different in each subject because the losses are different. A short guide to the usual patterns follows. A good tutor adapts it to the student; use it as a map of what to ask for.

### Mathematics

Most mock losses in IGCSE Mathematics come from a handful of sources: algebra slips, missing working, calculator and non-calculator habits, and a few topic areas such as trigonometry, vectors, sequences and probability. Drill non-calculator fluency in short daily blocks, practise setting out multi-step problems fully, and keep a list of formula that are not given. If time is the problem, practise timed sections and learn to leave a question that is stalling. For the specific error patterns, see [the ten most common IGCSE maths mistakes](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/), and for the top grade, the guide to [A* in IGCSE Maths 0580](/blog/how-to-score-a-star-in-igcse-maths-0580/).

### Sciences

Science mocks usually lose marks in three places: multiple-choice questions that rely on precise knowledge, extended theory answers where the student writes plenty but misses the marking points, and practical questions where tables, graphs and evaluation are handled loosely. Recover by retrieving key terms and equations, writing answers to the number of marks, and rehearsing practical-style questions. Remember that students must recall and rearrange the equations in the syllabus. If you want the cause behind these patterns, [why students struggle in IGCSE Science](/blog/igcse-science-tutoring-guide/) explains them in detail.

### English

English mocks usually lose marks through reading questions that are answered too thinly, writing that ignores audience and purpose, and a composition that is not planned. Recovery is writing: a timed response each week, compared with the mark scheme's levels and rewritten. Which English syllabus is entered matters: Cambridge First Language English 0500, English as a Second Language 0510 and 0511, Literature in English 0475, or the Pearson Edexcel equivalents. The communication side is discussed in [IGCSE English communication skills](/blog/igcse-english-communication-skills/).

### Economics, Business and Computer Science

These subjects reward precise language and structure. In Economics and Business, a student typically needs to define, apply and evaluate within the context of a case, and calculations such as break-even, cash-flow forecasts and ratios have to be set out. In Computer Science, Paper 2 depends on practising algorithms and pseudocode, and Paper 1 on recall of systems and networks. Drilling by question type, with the scheme open, works best.

## When Should You Question the Tier After a Weak Mock?

A weak mock raises the tier question, and it should be asked calmly and with evidence. Many Cambridge subjects have Core and Extended tiers: Core tops out at grade C, Extended reaches A*. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A is tiered Foundation or Higher. A student who sits Extended and consistently scores in the range that would correspond to a Core outcome faces a decision about whether to stay or switch.

The tier is usually decided by the school, not by the family, and the decision depends on the entry deadline for the series. So the first step is to ask the subject teacher what the entry deadline is and whether a change is possible. The second is to look at the evidence: not one mock, but several recent papers, and the cause of the lost marks. If marks are lost to slips and time on Extended papers, the student may be capable of the higher tier with training. If marks are lost because whole topics in the Extended-only content are not understood, there is a real choice to make.

Weigh the consequences honestly. A move to the lower tier caps the possible grade and may affect what the student can do next, such as entry to IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches or a particular A Level course, so check requirements with the destination. Staying in the higher tier with a weak result risks a lower grade than the lower tier might give. There is no universal answer, and a tutor can contribute a view, but the decision belongs to the family and the school.

## What Does a Four-Week Recovery Plan Look Like?

A four-week recovery plan is a sensible default when the real exams are at least two months away. If less time remains, compress it and follow the same order. The plan below is for a student with three or four subjects that need work, alongside school.

| Week | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tally lost marks by cause; fix the worst routine problems (misreads, working) | A cause table per subject; one routine change in each |
| 2 | Repair knowledge gaps with retrieval; start targeted past-paper question sets | Reds turned amber; first marked sets |
| 3 | Timed sections of the weakest paper types; redo questions that lost marks | Marked sections; a second pass on corrections |
| 4 | One full timed paper per major subject; compare with the mock | A new tally; a decision on what to keep drilling |

At the end of week 4, repeat the tally on the new paper. The aim is not to see a perfect score but to see the causes shifting. If misreads fell and knowledge gaps remain, the plan continues with a knowledge focus. If nothing moved, the diagnosis was incomplete and needs a second look.

:::timeline Recovery after a weak mock
Days 1-2 | Collect scripts, read, list losses
Week 1 | Tally by cause; fix routines
Weeks 2-3 | Repair gaps; targeted past-paper drilling
Week 4 | Timed paper; new tally; decide next steps
:::

## What Should a Tutor Do in a Mock-Recovery Session?

A recovery session is a working session with a clear shape. It begins with the student's own paper or last week's corrections, not with a new topic. The tutor asks questions to check understanding, hands over a past-paper question and watches the student attempt it, then marks together with the scheme, naming the cause of each lost mark. The student redoes one or two questions and ends the session by saying what they will do differently.

What good IGCSE tutors in Sushant Lok should do in these sessions:

- Review the marked script and name the main causes before proposing a plan.
- Teach the topic or technique behind a repeated loss, briefly and directly.
- Use past-paper questions as the working material, with the scheme.
- Give a short, specific set of tasks between sessions and check them at the start of the next.
- Say clearly what they can and cannot do, and report progress with marked evidence.

What a tutor should not do: complete homework for the student, promise a grade, or fill the session with explanation while the student writes nothing. A student who leaves a session without having attempted anything has watched a lecture, and a recovery needs practice.

## What If the Mock Shows the Problem Is Bigger Than Marks?

Sometimes a weak mock is a symptom of something outside the subject. A student who left papers blank may be anxious, exhausted or disengaged. A student who has missed several weeks of school has a content gap that practice alone will not close. A student who spends evenings on a very heavy schedule of activities may simply have no time to revise.

Look for the signs: marks that fall across all subjects, not one; sleep that has gone badly wrong; a student who avoids discussing school; a sudden change in mood. In those cases the first conversation is with the student, then the school counsellor or class teacher, not a tutor. A tutor can help with a subject gap, but not with a workload that is too heavy or a worry that needs a different kind of support.

When the problem is mainly marks and method, the recovery plan works. When it is bigger, treat the plan as secondary and protect sleep and wellbeing first. A student who sleeps and eats properly and attempts every question will usually outperform one who studies longer but arrives exhausted.

## How Should Parents Handle the Weeks After a Weak Mock?

Parents can help most by keeping the home calm and the schedule predictable. Ask what the student plans to fix, not what they scored. Offer to quiz from a card deck or the mistake log for ten minutes, which is more useful than asking about hours. Make sure the evening has a start and an end, and that meals and sleep are protected.

Avoid comparing the child with siblings or classmates, avoid adding extra classes in the final fortnight and avoid repeating the mock mark as a threat. Do speak to the school: the subject teachers can usually say which topics matter most and may offer extra help. If the family does bring in a tutor, share the marked mock and the cause tally with them in the first call, because it saves a week of rediscovery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I do first after a weak IGCSE mock result?

Get the marked script and mark scheme, and list the raw marks per question before changing anything. A total mark hides the pattern. Ask the teacher politely for the marked paper, sit with your child for half an hour without commentary and note the biggest losses, the blank questions and the questions where they say they knew the answer.

### How many past papers should my child do before the real exam?

There is no fixed number. A sensible pattern is to cover each paper type at least twice, with timed full papers kept for the final weeks and topic-grouped questions for weak areas. Correcting matters more than counting: a student who sits six papers and redoes every lost question usually gains more than one who sits fifteen and moves on.

### Is it too late to improve after a bad mock?

Usually not, if at least several weeks remain. Mocks often show causes that can be fixed quickly, such as misreading, missing working and time management. Knowledge gaps need longer, so prioritise the highest-mark topics. A recovery plan will not guarantee a grade, but it turns hours into marks more efficiently than general revision.

### Should we change the tier after a weak mock?

Only after looking at the evidence and asking the school. Several recent papers, not one mock, show whether marks are lost to slips and time or to whole topics not understood. The school decides the entry and there is a deadline. A lower tier caps the possible grade and may affect later choices, so check the requirements of the next course.

### Do I need a tutor, or can we recover alone?

Many students can recover alone if they mark honestly, tag the causes and redo corrections. A tutor helps when the cause is hard to see, when a topic cannot be repaired without explanation or when the student needs accountability. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and the student still has to practise between sessions. A trial lesson shows whether the help is useful.

### Is home tuition or online better in Sushant Lok?

Neither is better in general. Home suits students who lose focus on a screen and subjects where watching handwriting matters, while online suits busy evenings and avoids traffic delays. Many families use one home session on the weekend and short online sessions on weekdays. Try a format for a few weeks and change it if it is not working.

### How do I know if a tutor is good for recovery?

They should review your child's marked mock before proposing a plan, name specific causes of lost marks and use past-paper questions with the mark scheme in sessions. They should set short tasks between sessions and show marked evidence after a few weeks. Treat any grade guarantee, or a plan with no diagnosis, as a warning.

### Cambridge or Edexcel: does it change how we drill?

Yes, because papers, tiers and grading differ. Cambridge IGCSE uses Core and Extended tiers in many subjects, with Paper numbers that differ for each, while Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A is tiered Foundation or Higher, with grades 9 to 1. Always drill the papers of your own board and syllabus code, using the matching mark schemes.

### My child freezes in exams. Will past-paper drilling help?

It can help, because familiarity reduces panic, but only if the drilling builds a routine: read the paper first, bank easy marks, leave a stuck question and return, and keep time for checking. If the freeze is severe or affects daily life, speak to the school counsellor as well. Anxiety needs support beyond practice.

### What if the mock was weak in every subject?

Look for the common cause before treating each subject separately: sleep, workload, health, motivation or a change at school. A student who is exhausted performs poorly across the board, and extra lessons will not fix that. Talk to the student, then the class teacher or counsellor. Once the base is stable, run the recovery plan on the subjects that matter most.
