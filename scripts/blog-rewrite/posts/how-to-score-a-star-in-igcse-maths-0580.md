An A* in IGCSE Maths 0580 is earned on the Extended tier by being consistently accurate across two papers, not by being brilliant at the hardest question. Students who reach the top grade usually have the same profile: secure algebra, no careless losses in the easy marks, a habit of showing method on multi-step questions, and enough practice on unfamiliar problems that the last few questions of each paper do not freeze them.

This guide is for Grade 9 and Grade 10 students taking Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 at Extended level, and for parents who want to know what an A* actually demands. It explains how the two papers differ, where marks are won and lost, which accuracy habits matter most, how to use past papers properly and what the final term should look like. It also says honestly who does not need extra help. For the pacing side, there is a separate [12-week IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/); this post is about strategy for the top grade.

A short note on facts. Syllabuses are revised, and papers, durations and mark totals change between versions. Always check the current Cambridge syllabus and your school's entry details; the structure below is what the syllabus says at the time of writing.

## What Does an A* in IGCSE Maths 0580 Actually Require?

An A* in IGCSE Maths 0580 requires Extended-tier entry, near-complete syllabus coverage and a very high proportion of marks across both papers, including correct answers to the harder multi-step questions that sit towards the end. Core candidates cannot reach it: Core tops out at grade C, while Extended reaches A*.

That makes the first decision a tier decision. Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams) has two tiers. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). A student aiming for an A* needs to be entered for Extended, and the school normally decides the entry. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher, with two calculator papers per tier, and a different route to the top grade; this guide concerns the Cambridge syllabus.

Grade boundaries are set after each series, so there is no fixed percentage to quote. What can be said is the shape: an A* needs few lost marks. A student who drops 10 to 15 marks to carelessness on a total of about 200 across both papers has already put the grade at risk before meeting a single hard question. That is why accuracy gets as much space in this guide as difficult content.

2 | Written papers: Paper 2 and Paper 4
A* | Top grade, available on Extended only
3 | Exam series a year, with the February/March series for India
1 | Non-calculator paper and one calculator paper

Cambridge exam series run in February/March (a series offered only in India), May/June and October/November. The February/March series exists specifically for India and lets Grade 10 results arrive in time for Indian school admissions to Class 11 and for IB Diploma Programme (DP) or A Level entry in the same academic year. Schools decide which series they enter students for; a student cannot choose a series independently of the school.

## How Do Paper 2 and Paper 4 Differ?

Paper 2 is the non-calculator Extended paper and Paper 4 is the calculator Extended paper, and they reward different habits. Paper 2 tests fluency: exact values, algebra, fractions, surds and method without technology. Paper 4 is the longer paper with the larger share of the marks, built around multi-step problems where calculator use is expected but reasoning still decides the marks.

| Feature | Paper 2 (Extended, no calculator) | Paper 4 (Extended, calculator) |
|---|---|---|
| What it tests | Algebraic fluency, exact working, number sense | Multi-step problem solving, modelling, data and graphs |
| Typical risk | Arithmetic slips with no safety net | Rounding too early, unread instructions, time |
| Where an A* is secured | Not losing marks in routine algebra | Converting longer problems into correct working |
| Calculator habit | None; check by estimating | Efficient, with every step written down |

Check the current syllabus for each paper's duration and weighting, because Paper 4 carries the larger share of the total and deserves proportionate preparation time. A student who spends equal hours on both papers will usually under-prepare for Paper 4, where long questions combine several topics.

### Why the non-calculator paper decides more A* outcomes than people expect

Students often assume that Paper 2 is the easier one, because the questions look short. In practice, a non-calculator paper at Extended level has little margin: an error in an early fraction or a sign propagates, and there is no calculator to catch it. A student scoring an A* on Paper 4 but dropping marks on Paper 2 is a common profile. The fix is mundane. Do routine arithmetic and algebra by hand weekly, estimate every answer, and check by substitution whenever the question allows it.

## Where Do the Marks Come From in Extended Maths?

The marks in Extended come from the whole syllabus, but some areas appear in more questions and in more connected ways than others. The syllabus is organised into number, algebra and graphs, coordinate geometry, geometry, mensuration, trigonometry, transformations and vectors, probability and statistics. Algebra and graphs links to almost every other area, which is why weak algebra damages scores everywhere.

The chart below is a judgement about where a typical Extended student's marks depend on strength, not an official weighting. It is meant to show why algebra deserves the most practice time.

:::bars How much each area typically leans on algebra skill (illustrative)
Algebra and graphs | 95 | Core of the paper
Trigonometry and Pythagoras | 80 | Equations, rearranging
Mensuration and geometry | 75 | Formulae, unknowns
Coordinate geometry and vectors | 75 | Equations of lines
Number and statistics | 50 | Mostly arithmetic
This is a teaching judgement, not an official Cambridge figure.
:::

For an A* candidate, three groups of topics deserve extra care because they produce the multi-step questions that separate A from A*.

- **Algebra and graphs:** quadratics, simultaneous equations (linear and non-linear), algebraic fractions, rearranging formulae, functions and inverse functions, transformations of graphs, and sketching.
- **Trigonometry and geometry:** sine and cosine rules, area of a triangle, three-dimensional problems, circle theorems and proofs, arc length and sector area, similar shapes with area and volume ratios.
- **Calculus and sequences, where they appear in your version:** differentiation for gradients and stationary points, and sequences with nth term. Check the syllabus for which of these are in your tier.

A student who is already strong in number and statistics gains little from extra practice there. The efficient plan allocates time by weakness, not by syllabus order.

## Which Habits Separate an A from an A*?

The habits that separate an A from an A* are mostly small and mostly about accuracy: writing method on every multi-mark question, keeping full calculator values until the final answer, reading the instruction on accuracy, and checking. They do not need extra intelligence; they need rehearsal, because nobody does them reliably under exam pressure unless they have done them hundreds of times.

Here is a short list that works as a standalone routine:

1. Read the whole question, including the last line, before writing anything.
2. Underline the accuracy instruction (for example, to a given number of significant figures) and the units required.
3. Write the method line before the number, even when you could do it mentally.
4. Keep unrounded values in the calculator and round only the final answer.
5. Check the answer for size: does a length of 340 metres for a garden fence make sense?
6. Substitute back into the original equation whenever the question allows it.
7. Leave the last five minutes for a scan of blank spaces and units.

A note on accuracy: unless a question says otherwise, Cambridge papers usually expect answers to three significant figures, with angles to one decimal place, and print the instruction on the front of the paper. Confirm the current instructions on a recent paper, because they are the exact rule for your session.

### The cost of premature rounding

Consider a typical multi-step question: find a length with the sine rule, then use it to find an area. A student who rounds the length to 3 significant figures between the steps, and then rounds the area again, can end up with a final answer a few units off the mark scheme's accurate value. Method marks may survive, but the accuracy mark is lost. Multiply that over six or seven questions in a paper and you have the difference between an A and an A*. The habit is trivial: store values in the calculator memory and write the rounded version only for display.

## How Should You Use Past Papers to Chase an A*?

You should use past papers in three stages: topic questions while learning, section practice while consolidating, and full timed papers once most content is covered, with every one marked strictly against the mark scheme. The mistake is treating a past paper as a test to pass instead of a source of information.

Past papers and mark schemes are available through your school and Cambridge's official channels. The mark scheme is as useful as the paper because it shows exactly what earns method (M), accuracy (A) and independent (B) marks. Students who study it learn why a correct answer without working can still lose marks, and how an incorrect final answer can still earn most of them. For the broader case for this method, see [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/).

| Stage | When | What you do | What you record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic questions | While each unit is taught | 10 to 15 past questions on the topic, untimed | Method marks you missed |
| Mixed sections | After about half the syllabus | 30 to 40 minute mixed sets, timed | Topics you could not identify |
| Full papers | Final two terms | Whole Paper 2 or Paper 4, timed, no breaks | Every lost mark with a cause |
| Targeted repeats | Last six weeks | Repeat questions from your log after a gap | Whether the fix held |

### How to mark a paper like an examiner

Mark with a different colour, award marks only where the scheme says a mark exists, and be cold about it. "I knew that" is not working on the page. After marking, sort each lost mark into one of four causes: a knowledge gap, a wrong method choice, an arithmetic or notation slip, or a timing problem. A student who finds that 60% of lost marks are arithmetic slips needs a different final term from one who finds that 60% are knowledge gaps. This is the same cause-tagging system described in [how to score a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/), and it works equally well one level down.

## What Are the Most Expensive Mistakes in Extended Maths?

The most expensive mistakes are small ones repeated: sign errors, mishandled brackets, premature rounding, missing units, misread questions and incomplete working. Each loses one or two marks, but a typical script contains six or eight of them. The detailed ten-mistake breakdown with worked examples lives in [the top 10 mistakes in IGCSE maths](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/); the version below is the A*-specific cut.

| Mistake | What it looks like | A* habit that prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| Sign slip in expansion | (x - 3)(x + 5) written as x² + 2x + 15 | Expand in two visible lines, check with x = 1 |
| Early rounding | Rounding a sine-rule length before the area step | Keep full calculator values, round last |
| Missing units | Correct number, no cm² or m/s | Write the unit before the number is final |
| Unread final line | Giving an answer in the wrong form | Underline the instruction first |
| Skipped method | Answer only on a 4-mark question | Method line on every multi-mark item |
| Giving up on late parts | Blank space on the final question | Attempt every part; partial marks exist |

An A* script is not error-free. It simply loses very few marks to errors that a checking routine would have caught.

## How Do You Approach the Hardest Questions on the Paper?

You approach the hardest questions by breaking them into stages, writing what you know, and collecting the method marks that exist at every stage, instead of waiting for a flash of insight. The last questions on an Extended paper are designed to connect two or three topics, which makes them look unfamiliar even when each step is standard.

Take a typical late question: a shape built from a sector and a triangle, with a perimeter given and an unknown angle to find. The student who freezes is looking for one trick. The student who scores marks does four ordinary things: sketches the shape, labels every known length, writes the sector and triangle formulae separately, and sets up an equation from the perimeter. By the time they have an equation, they are already past the part that stopped most of the class.

Here is a numbered process for unfamiliar problems that is worth drilling until it is automatic:

1. Draw or redraw the situation and label everything given.
2. Write down what the question asks for, with its units.
3. List the formulae or relationships connected to the objects in the diagram.
4. Look for a link: a shared side, an equal angle, a total that must match.
5. Write an equation or a first calculation, even if you are unsure it is the final route.
6. Check that the result is sensible, then answer in the form requested.

The sixth step takes ten seconds and catches a surprising number of slips. A radius of 0.02 cm or a probability of 1.4 announces its own error.

## How Do You Build Fluency in Algebra, the Engine of the A* Paper?

You build algebra fluency through short, frequent, mixed practice with strict checking, not through occasional long sessions. Algebra links to nearly every topic, so a student who can manipulate expressions without hesitation gains time and accuracy everywhere else.

A practical weekly pattern for an A* candidate is three 20-minute algebra sets: one on expansion and factorising, one on equations and rearranging, and one on algebraic fractions and functions. Each set should include at least one question the student cannot solve at first sight, worked on for a full 10 minutes before looking at the answer. That unfamiliar question is the training for the last page of Paper 4.

Watch for these specific weak points, which commonly hold back otherwise strong students:

- **Algebraic fractions:** common denominators with brackets, and cancelling only factors, not terms.
- **Simultaneous equations with a quadratic:** substituting correctly and then checking both solution pairs.
- **Rearranging formulae where the subject appears twice:** collecting terms and factorising.
- **Function notation:** composite and inverse functions, which students often treat as arithmetic instead of algebra.
- **Surds and exact values:** the non-calculator paper expects exact answers where a calculator-trained student gives decimals.

If algebra is the main weakness across the class, [why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics](/blog/igcse-mathematics-tutoring-guide/) is the diagnostic guide, with the root causes and fixes laid out one by one.

## What Should the Final Term Look Like?

The final term should be built around full timed papers, strict marking, an error log and a steady taper, with new content finished well before it begins. The aim is to convert knowledge into marks, and that is mostly a matter of rehearsal and checking.

:::timeline The last 12 weeks before the Extended papers
Weeks 12-9 | Close content gaps, weakest topics first
Weeks 8-6 | Mixed sections, timed, marked strictly
Weeks 5-3 | Full Paper 2 and Paper 4, one each per week
Weeks 2-1 | Error-log repeats, light review, early nights
:::

A few specifics make a difference:

- **Alternate papers.** One non-calculator and one calculator paper a week keeps both skills warm.
- **Practise under exam conditions.** Same time of day as the real paper, no phone, a clock on the desk and the permitted equipment only.
- **Review before more practice.** Spend more time on the marked paper than on writing it. A paper that is written and not reviewed teaches almost nothing.
- **Keep the final week light.** No new topics. Review the error log, formulae you must remember and your timing plan.

Students targeting the February/March series, which is common for Indian schools, have a compressed calendar, because the final term overlaps with school mocks. Ask your school for the entry series and mock dates early, then plan backwards. ## How Does the Calculator Paper Reward Method, Not Button Skills?

The calculator paper rewards method because the marks sit in the working: setting up the equation, choosing the correct rule and presenting the result, not in the button presses. A calculator makes arithmetic reliable, but it does not choose the method, and it will happily compute the wrong thing quickly.

Three calculator habits matter for an A*:

1. **Know the functions you will actually use.** Fractions, standard form, statistics mode, equation solving (if your calculator permits and the paper allows it) and storing values.
2. **Write the line of working before pressing keys.** If the key sequence is wrong, the examiner can still award method marks for the line you wrote.
3. **Check the mode.** Degrees versus radians is the classic silent killer on trigonometry questions. Check it at the start of every paper.

Keep the calculator rules of your exam centre in mind, and never assume a function is allowed because it exists. Ask your school about the permitted model well before the paper.

## When Does a Student Move From Core-Style Working to Extended Thinking?

A student moves from Core-style working to Extended thinking when they stop looking for a procedure and start looking for a structure: what links the pieces of this problem. Core questions are mostly one idea at a time. Extended questions combine ideas, and the last third of each paper is largely built that way.

Consider a student who has been scoring well in school tests, which are mostly topic-by-topic, and then finds Paper 4 harder than expected. The content is the same; the question shape is different. The remedy is early mixed practice: sets where the topic is not named. Start as soon as roughly half the syllabus is covered, and keep the ratio rising. By the final term, most of the practice should be mixed.

Students who have taken or plan to take Additional Mathematics 0606 alongside 0580 Extended often feel this shift more strongly. Additional Mathematics has no tiers, two written papers with a calculator allowed, and is graded A* to E; it is usually taken alongside 0580 Extended by students heading for IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA) HL or A Level Mathematics. ## What Does the Climb to an A* Look Like Phase by Phase?

The climb from a mid-grade to an A* usually moves through the same phases: repairing foundations, building accuracy, introducing mixed practice and finally rehearsing under exam conditions. The timeline varies, but the order rarely does.

Consider an illustrative student, not a real one, who enters Grade 10 working at a solid B in Extended. A diagnostic shows that most lost marks come from algebraic fractions, simultaneous equations and unread instructions. The first phase is repair: four to six weeks on algebra with short daily sets. The second is accuracy: every question gets a method line, a units check and a sense check. The third is mixed practice, with sections where topics are not named. The fourth is timed papers, marked strictly. Along the way, the student's lost marks shift from knowledge gaps to arithmetic slips and then to almost nothing but occasional timing problems. That is what a rising grade looks like on the error log, long before it shows on a mock.

The fuller phase-by-phase account is in [IGCSE maths from average to A*](/blog/igcse-maths-success-stories-from-average-to-a-star/), which uses clearly labelled illustrative profiles. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and the speed of the climb depends on the starting point, the time available and consistency.

## How Much Does a Tutor Help With an A* Target?

A tutor helps with an A* target when there is a specific, diagnosable gap or when feedback at school is not detailed enough to act on, and does not help much when the student already has strong habits and a clear error log. Many A* students never have a tutor. The honest question is what is missing.

A tutor tends to add most in three situations: a student with a persistent weakness that self-study has not shifted, a student who knows the content but loses marks to presentation and timing, and a student switching from another board who has not met Cambridge-style mark schemes. In each case the value is feedback specific enough to act on, such as "you lose method marks on the sine rule because you round the intermediate length", which is a fix, as opposed to "practise trigonometry", which is a comment.

### What a good session actually looks like

A good session is mostly the student working. The tutor sets a short timed section, watches the working, then marks it alongside the student, naming the cause of each lost mark. The last fifteen minutes go on one or two unfamiliar problems, with the tutor prompting only when the student is stuck for several minutes. After four to six weeks, there should be evidence: a shorter error log, a better timed-paper mark and a student who can explain a method that was a mystery earlier.

## How Should Parents Support an A* Target at Home?

Parents should support an A* target by protecting routine, asking process questions and staying out of the mathematics, rather than checking answers or adding pressure. The most useful things at home are practical: a quiet fixed slot, enough sleep and a calm response to a bad mock.

Useful questions to ask are "what did you learn from the last paper?" and "what is on your error log this week?" Unhelpful ones are "why did you lose those marks?" asked over dinner. The first set keeps the student in charge of the diagnosis. The second turns it into a verdict.

Also be wary of any service promising a result. A grade depends on the student's work, the school's teaching, the paper on the day and the boundary for that session. Evidence of progress is a better yardstick than promises: shorter error logs, better timed marks and a student who can explain methods.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you get an A* in IGCSE Maths 0580 on the Core tier?

No, because Core tops out at grade C and only Extended reaches A*. Core candidates sit Paper 1 and Paper 3, while Extended candidates sit Paper 2 and Paper 4. Tier entry is normally decided by the school, so a student aiming for an A* should raise the question with the maths teacher early in Grade 9, when a move to Extended is still practical.

### What percentage do you need for an A*?

There is no fixed percentage, because Cambridge sets grade boundaries after each series. As a rough guide, an A* needs a very high proportion of the total marks across both papers, which leaves little room for careless losses. Treat any single percentage quoted online with caution, and use recent examiner reports and your own mock results as the more reliable signals.

### How many hours a week should an A* candidate study?

Most A* candidates need about four to six hours a week of focused work outside lessons, rising in the final term, though the right number depends on the starting point and other subjects. Consistency beats volume: four sessions of ninety minutes with timed work and strict marking outperform a long, unfocused weekend. Rest matters as well, particularly in the last two weeks.

### Is Paper 2 or Paper 4 more important?

Both matter, but Paper 4 carries the larger share of the marks and deserves proportionately more preparation time. Paper 2 is the non-calculator paper, where arithmetic slips are costly because there is no safety net. Check the current syllabus for exact weightings and durations. A balanced plan alternates the two papers each week from the middle of Grade 10.

### Do I need to do Additional Mathematics 0606 for an A*?

No, an A* in 0580 does not require Additional Mathematics. Additional Mathematics 0606 is a separate syllabus with no tiers, usually taken alongside 0580 Extended by students heading towards IB Mathematics AA HL or A Level Mathematics. It strengthens algebra and calculus, so many such students find 0580 easier, but it adds workload. Ask your school whether it is offered and whether it suits your plans.

### How do I stop making careless mistakes?

Replace willpower with routine: write the method line, keep unrounded values, underline the instruction, check units and use a ten-second sense check on every answer. Careless mistakes are usually a pattern, not a character flaw, so tag each lost mark with a cause in an error log and review it weekly. The pattern normally narrows to two or three habits that can be trained.

### Should I memorise formulae or use the formula sheet?

Learn the formulae you will use, and check your current paper for what is provided. Cambridge papers have historically given some formulae on the paper and expected others from memory, but this can change with the syllabus version. Practise recall of the ones not given, using short daily retrieval, and understand what each formula means so that you can rebuild one under pressure.

### What if I am stuck on the last question of the paper?

Attempt every part, because the marks are awarded by part, not by question. Write down what you know, draw a diagram if one is missing, and set up an equation even if you are unsure it will finish the problem. Method marks exist at every stage. If you are stuck for more than a few minutes, move on and return, and use the final scan for blanks.

### How early should I start preparing for the A*?

Start in Grade 9 with solid foundations and weekly algebra practice, then add mixed practice once half the syllabus is covered. Starting in Grade 10 is still workable if time is planned carefully. The earlier phases, repairing algebra and building accuracy, take the longest and are the ones that leave the least room if postponed. The last term is for rehearsal, not repair.

### Is a tutor worth it for an A* in IGCSE Maths 0580?

Sometimes, if there is a clear gap or the school gives little specific feedback; often not, if the student already has strong habits. A tutor is worth considering when self-study has not shifted a weakness, when marks are lost to presentation and timing, or when the student is new to Cambridge mark schemes. No tutor can guarantee a grade, so judge the arrangement by evidence of progress after four to six weeks.

### Can a student recover from a poor mock in the final term?

Yes, if the mock is analysed honestly and the remaining weeks are used on the biggest causes of lost marks. A poor mock is information, not a verdict. Sort the lost marks into knowledge gaps, method choices, slips and timing, then rebuild the plan around the largest group. Improvement is possible in a few weeks, but it depends on consistent work and cannot be promised.
