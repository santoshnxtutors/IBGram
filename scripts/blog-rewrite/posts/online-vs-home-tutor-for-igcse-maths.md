For IGCSE Mathematics, the online vs home tutor for IGCSE maths question has a short answer: pick the format in which your child's *written working* is visible to the tutor in real time, and in which your child actually pays attention. For most students that is either a good home tutor at the table or an online tutor with a pen tablet and a shared whiteboard. A video call where the student types answers and the tutor talks is the weak option, and so is a home tutor who arrives late and explains from a chair across the room.

This guide is for parents of Grade 9 and 10 students on Cambridge IGCSE or Pearson Edexcel International GCSE who have to choose a format, and for students who feel their current arrangement is not helping. It compares the two formats on the things that matter in this subject: how maths is written, how the non-calculator paper is practised, how attention behaves, how logistics work in Gurgaon, and which kind of student suits which. It is written from the mentoring side of IB and IGCSE maths by Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, and it does not pretend one format wins for everyone.

## What Is Different About IGCSE Maths When Choosing a Tutor?

IGCSE Maths is a paper-and-pencil subject with a calculator paper and a non-calculator paper. That single fact shapes the choice more than any other.

Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams) has two routes. Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Core tops out at grade C and Extended reaches A*. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher, with two calculator papers per tier. Always confirm the exact papers and tier with the school, since the school decides the entry.

What this means for tutoring:

- **Working is assessed.** Method marks are given for the steps on the page. A tutor must see the steps, not only the final answer.
- **The non-calculator paper trains fluency.** Fractions, surds, algebra and number need to be fast and neat without a device. That is practised by hand.
- **The calculator paper trains efficiency.** Knowing which keystrokes to use, when to round and how to avoid premature rounding.
- **The paper is long and arithmetic-heavy.** Stamina and accuracy count as much as insight.

A tutoring format works if it supports these four things. Every comparison below comes back to them.

:::stats IGCSE Maths 0580 at a glance
2 | Routes: Core and Extended
2 | Papers per route, one non-calculator and one calculator
C | Highest grade available on Core
A* | Highest grade available on Extended
:::

## How Maths Is Actually Written in Each Format

This is the section most parents skip and the one that decides most outcomes.

### Writing maths at a home session

At home, the student writes on paper and the tutor watches. The tutor sees pen speed, the order in which steps are written, where the student hesitates, how fractions are laid out and whether the equals signs line up. A great deal of coaching happens in the first few seconds of a mistake: the tutor sees the wrong line appear and says "stop, look at that line again".

Take a student solving 3(x + 4) = 24 who writes 3x + 4 = 24. A tutor beside the student notices the missing multiplication the moment it is written, and the correction costs ten seconds. The student has not had time to build on the error.

### Writing maths online

Online, the quality of the visible working depends entirely on the setup. There are four common setups, in descending order of usefulness.

| Setup | What the tutor sees | Quality for IGCSE maths |
|---|---|---|
| Pen tablet and shared whiteboard, both sides writing | Live working, step by step | Strong, close to home |
| Student writes on paper, phone or document camera on the page | Live paper, a little delayed | Good, if the angle is steady |
| Student writes on paper, sends photos after each question | Finished working only | Moderate, errors found late |
| Student types answers in chat | Final answers only | Weak, hides method errors |

The first two setups are the ones to insist on. A mouse is a poor writing tool; a basic drawing tablet is enough and does not have to be expensive. The tutor should also write on the shared board, so that the student sees correct layout, not only hears it.

A short example of why layout matters. A student solving 2x + 7 = 19 who writes the steps one under another in a clear column can be corrected quickly. A student who writes a jumble of numbers across the page, which is common when typing, hides the very mistake the tutor needs to see. Online tutoring works for maths when the working is visible and structured.

### The non-calculator paper

Non-calculator practice has to be handwritten and timed. Both formats can do it. At home the tutor can sit in the room as the student works; online, the student can work on paper with the camera on and the tutor watching the clock. The weaker pattern is any session in which the student does the paper alone and the tutor reviews it a week later. Fluency is built by quick correction.

## Does Online or Home Tutoring Give Better Attention in a Maths Session?

Neither is better in general; they fail differently. Attention is a property of the student and the setup, not of the format.

**At home, the risks are interruptions and a passive student.** A sibling walks in, a parent offers tea, the student is half-listening because a tutor will "explain it anyway". The benefit is physical presence: a tutor can tell at once when attention has dropped and change the activity.

**Online, the risks are tabs, notifications and a second device.** The benefit is that the student is already at the desk and does not have to wait for a tutor to travel. A student who is comfortable at a screen can concentrate for a full session. A student who is easily pulled to games, messaging or video will drift, and a tutor on a call cannot see the second screen.

A practical rule: if your child's attention is the main problem, begin with home sessions or, online, insist on the camera showing the desk and the screen with notifications switched off. Review after two weeks. If attention improves, stay; if not, change the format, not just the tutor.

### Age and stage

Grade 9 students, who are new to the IGCSE style and often younger in habit, tend to need more supervision than Grade 10 students. A Grade 10 student with an exam series approaching, who has to fit tutoring between school, projects and revision, may value the online format for its time savings. The exam series matters here: Cambridge runs February/March (a series offered only in India), May/June and October/November, and Pearson Edexcel runs January and May/June. Schools decide which series they enter students for. Confirm yours, and plan the format around the weeks before it.

## Calculator Skills: Which Format Teaches Them Better?

A Cambridge or Edexcel calculator paper rewards efficiency. The student should know how to use the scientific calculator quickly: fractions, powers, roots, standard form, trigonometric functions and statistical mode where the syllabus uses it. Both formats can teach this if the tutor and the student hold the same model.

At home, tutor and student can hold the same calculator and compare keystrokes. Online, the student can hold up the calculator to the camera or screen-share an emulator for explanation, then practise on the physical device allowed in the exam. The golden rule is identical in both: **practise on the device you will use in the exam.** A tutor demonstrating on a different model can cause confusion with bracket handling and display formats.

A frequent error seen in scripts: premature rounding. A student rounds an intermediate answer to two decimal places and then uses that value in the next line, so the final answer is off in the third significant figure and the accuracy mark is lost. The fix is to store values in the calculator memory and round only at the end. That habit is taught in the same way in either format; what matters is whether the tutor sees the key presses or the working.

## Which Kind of Student Suits Online and Which Suits Home?

Student type is the best guide to the decision. These descriptions are patterns, not rules.

### A student who suits home tutoring

- Needs someone beside them to start work.
- Rushes, writes untidily or skips steps, and benefits from being corrected mid-line.
- Is younger in habit, newer to IGCSE, or anxious about maths.
- Has a quiet study space and a reliable evening slot.
- Gets distracted by screens.

### A student who suits online tutoring

- Is organised and starts work without prompting.
- Writes clearly and is comfortable with a pen tablet or camera.
- Has a busy week where travel time matters.
- Needs a specific specialist, such as a tutor strong in Extended content or Additional Mathematics 0606, who lives far away.
- Is abroad for part of the year, or travels, so that continuity matters more than location.

### A student who may need both

A Grade 10 student who has been weak since Grade 9 may need home sessions to repair habits through the autumn and online sessions to fit extra practice before the exam series. The mixture is explored in [home vs online IB tutors in Gurgaon](/blog/home-vs-online-ib-tutors-in-gurgaon-what-works-better/), written for IB students but with the same logic for IGCSE.

## A Decision Method You Can Use This Week

If you are stuck, use a short test rather than a debate.

1. **Watch your child do a past-paper question without help**, with a pen, and note where the working breaks down.
2. **Ask whether the problem is skill, habit or access.** Skill gaps need good teaching in either format. Habit gaps (rushing, not writing steps, drifting) favour home. Access problems (no suitable tutor nearby, no free evenings) favour online.
3. **Book a trial in your preferred format** and judge it by what the tutor sees of the student's writing, not by how pleasant the call was.
4. **Review after two weeks**, comparing the error log with the first week.
5. **Change one thing at a time**, either the format or the tutor, never both.

That list works as a standalone answer to "how do I choose between online and home tutoring for IGCSE maths?" because it moves the decision from preference to evidence.

:::steps Choosing a format for IGCSE maths
Observe | Watch a past-paper question done unaided
Diagnose | Is it skill, habit or access?
Trial | Try the format you lean towards
Compare | Review the error log after two weeks
Adjust | Change one variable only
:::

## What a Good IGCSE Maths Session Looks Like in Either Format

Format aside, the shape of a productive session is the same.

The shape below applies to the online vs home tutor for IGCSE maths decision in both directions: if a format cannot support it, it is the wrong format.

:::bars Typical split of a 70-minute IGCSE maths session (illustrative)
Review of last errors | 14 | 10 min
Teaching one topic | 36 | 25 min
Supervised attempt | 36 | 25 min
Set and record | 14 | 10 min
A sketch of a well-run session, not a fixed rule.
:::

**Review (10 minutes).** The tutor opens with the last session's errors, not with new content. The student attempts one of them again.

**Teach (20 to 25 minutes).** One priority topic, shown with a worked example. The tutor explains the method and the reason for each step. The student restates it in their own words.

**Attempt (20 to 25 minutes).** The student works a fresh question while the tutor watches the writing, whether on paper beside them or on a shared board. Corrections are immediate.

**Practice and set (10 minutes).** The tutor agrees a short independent task, and records it.

If either format loses this shape, the student pays for it. A session in which the tutor speaks for forty minutes while the student nods is poor value at home or online.

### An example: the 0580 algebra gap

Consider a Grade 10 student on the Extended route who is losing marks in algebra. The tutor sets a quadratic equation to solve by factorising and watches the first line. The student writes x² + 5x + 6 = 0 and then (x + 5)(x + 1). Immediately the tutor can ask: "What do the two numbers need to do?" The student realises the pair must multiply to 6 and add to 5, writes (x + 2)(x + 3), and solves. The conversation took thirty seconds because the tutor saw the line as it was written. That is the quality to demand from either format.

## Past Papers, Markschemes and the Error Log in Each Format

Past papers are the heart of IGCSE maths preparation, and the format affects how they are used.

- **At home**, a student can sit a printed paper at the desk with the tutor in the next room, timed, and the tutor can mark it in person. Debrief is a conversation.
- **Online**, the student sits the paper on paper, scans or photographs it, and the tutor marks it on screen using the markscheme. Debrief is a shared screen with annotation. This is often neater and faster.

The method guidance, including when to begin and how many to do, is in [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/). Common mistakes to log are collected in [top 10 mistakes in IGCSE maths](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/). A simple error log works in either format: date, question, mistake type (method, arithmetic, reading, notation, time), fix, and rule to remember.

| Error type | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Expands 3(x + 4) as 3x + 4 | Multiply every term in the bracket |
| Arithmetic | 7 × 8 written as 54 | Short daily non-calculator drill |
| Reading | Answers for the wrong angle | Underline what is asked |
| Notation | Missing units or "= 0" | Checklist before moving on |
| Time | Spends too long on one part | Mark and move; return later |

## Mistakes Families Make When Choosing a Format

Most errors in the online vs home tutor for IGCSE maths choice come from convenience beating evidence.

- **Choosing on convenience alone.** A format that saves ten minutes of travel but hides the student's working costs far more in lost marks.
- **Judging the tutor by friendliness.** A pleasant call can hide a session in which the student did none of the work.
- **Skipping the trial.** Both formats need a trial; the first fifteen minutes show whether the working is visible.
- **No camera on the page.** Online without a view of the paper is the weakest arrangement.
- **Too many hours.** Doubling sessions without independent practice rarely helps.
- **Changing everything at once.** If you change tutor, day and format together, you will not know what helped.
- **No record.** Without an error log, a session is a one-off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an online tutor as good as a home tutor for IGCSE maths?

Yes, if the tutor can see the student's handwritten working live and the student concentrates on a screen. The format matters less than visibility of working. A pen tablet or a camera on the paper makes online sessions close to a home session. Without that, an online tutor sees only answers and misses method errors, which is where IGCSE marks are lost.

### Which format is better for a weak student?

Often home, at least at first, because a tutor beside the student can correct errors as they appear and build habits. But a weak student with a strong, patient online tutor and a good pen tablet can progress just as well. Judge by what the tutor sees of the working in a trial. If the student does not start work without prompting, begin at home.

### Can the non-calculator paper be practised online?

Yes. The student works on paper with the camera on and a timer, and the tutor marks the paper afterwards on screen. What matters is that the practice is handwritten and timed. Fluency in fractions, algebra and number is built by quick correction, so schedule the debrief close to the attempt, not a week later.

### Does my child need a pen tablet for online IGCSE maths?

It is the best option, but a camera on the paper also works. A pen tablet lets the student and tutor write on a shared board, which keeps the working visible and tidy. A basic drawing tablet is enough. If you cannot get one, a phone on a stand pointing at the page is a reasonable second choice, provided the angle is steady.

### Is hybrid tutoring a good idea for IGCSE maths?

It can be, when the student's needs change over the year. A common pattern is home sessions to repair habits in Grade 9 or the first term of Grade 10, and online sessions for extra practice before the exam series. The risk is two tutors giving different methods, so keep one shared plan and one error log.

### How do I know if the format is working?

Look for fewer repeated errors, tidier working and better timing in papers over two to four weeks. Compare the error log with the first week. If the same mistakes keep appearing, change one variable, either the tutor or the format. No tutor can guarantee a grade, but a good one can show a process that is improving.

### What if my child gets distracted online?

Set rules and test them: camera on, notifications off, one device, phone out of reach, and the desk visible if possible. Keep sessions structured, with a clear task every ten to fifteen minutes. If distraction continues after two weeks, move to home sessions or a hybrid. Persistent distraction is a signal about the setup, not a failure of the child.

### Should a Grade 9 student start with online or home?

Home is a sensible default for Grade 9, because habits are still forming and supervision helps, but it depends on the student. An organised Grade 9 student with a strong online tutor can do very well. Starting early with a light weekly session is more useful than waiting for Grade 10, when time is tighter.

### What if the tutor is good but the format is wrong?

Change the format with the same tutor if possible. Many tutors teach both ways, and continuity is valuable. Tell the tutor what is not working, for example, that the student is drifting online, and agree a trial period in the other format. Keep the shared notes so that nothing is lost in the switch.

### Do Edexcel and Cambridge students need different formats?

No. The choice of format depends on the student and the setup, not the board. The papers differ (Cambridge 0580 has Core and Extended with non-calculator and calculator papers; Edexcel 4MA1 has Foundation and Higher), and the tutor should know the board's papers and markschemes. The writing, attention and logistics questions above apply to both.
