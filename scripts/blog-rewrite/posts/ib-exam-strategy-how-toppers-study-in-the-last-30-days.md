The last 30 days before IB exams are not the time to learn the course again. They are the time to turn what you half-know into marks you can reliably collect, and to protect your sleep, your timetable and your nerves while you do it. A student who spends this month on triage, timed papers and error repair almost always finishes better than one who spends it rereading notes for twelve hours a day.

This guide is for International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) students in DP2 who sit the May session, and for the parents who live in the house during that month. It sets out a day-by-day structure, a way to decide what to drop, a rotation for past papers across six subjects, a sleep and logistics plan, and the mistakes that cost the most. It assumes you already have the course covered once, even if badly.

One caution before the plan. Exam dates differ by subject and by candidate, so every day number below counts back from *your* first paper, not from a calendar date. Take the personal timetable your school's DP coordinator issues, mark Day 0 on it, and count backwards. If you are further out than a month, the wider calendar is in the [IB exam preparation guide](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/); this page owns the final stretch only.

## Why the last 30 days before IB exams are different from every other month

Up to the final month, revision serves two purposes at once: learning new material and consolidating old material. In the final month, the first purpose mostly ends. The syllabus is taught, the internal assessments are submitted, and the marks left on the table are of three kinds: knowledge you have forgotten, knowledge you hold but cannot apply under time pressure, and marks lost to habits such as skipped working or ignoring the command term.

Each of those has a different cure. Forgotten content needs retrieval practice. Poor application needs timed questions with honest marking. Bad habits need an error log that is read, not just written. Rereading a textbook cures none of them, which is why it feels productive and changes so little.

A second difference is that the month is finite and you cannot buy more of it. Between school study leave, mock-exam fallout, family events and the exams themselves, a month that looks like 30 study days usually contains 22 to 25 usable ones. Plan for that number, not the optimistic one.

### What a 30-day plan can and cannot do

It can lift a subject by a grade if the gap is in execution. A student on a steady 5 whose losses are skipped working, unit slips and abandoned long questions can move in a month, because those habits are fixable at speed.

It cannot rebuild a subject you never understood. A student who missed most of Paper 2 content in Biology or has never completed a calculus unit will not fix that in four weeks while also covering five other subjects. For that student the honest strategy is triage: secure the 4 you can reach, and decide deliberately where the one big gamble goes. Triage gets its own section below.

## The 30-day shape at a glance

The structure that holds up across subjects has four phases. The boundaries move by a day or two depending on your timetable, but the order does not.

:::timeline The last 30 days, counted back from your first paper
Days 30 to 22 | Diagnose, triage, repair the weakest content
Days 21 to 12 | Mixed question practice, then sectional timing
Days 11 to 4 | Full timed papers in exam conditions, one per day
Days 3 to 1 | Light recall, logistics, sleep, no new material
:::

Notice what is absent: a separate week for "learning everything again". The first phase repairs gaps; it does not restart the course.

### Phase 1: Days 30 to 22, diagnose and repair

The goal is a ranked list of what is costing you marks, and a first pass at fixing the top items. By Day 22 you should know, per subject, which five or six topics carry your losses.

### Phase 2: Days 21 to 12, practise in pieces

Questions replace notes. You work in sections: one topic's worth of exam questions, then mixed sets, then a half paper against the clock. This is where retrieval practice does most of its work.

### Phase 3: Days 11 to 4, rehearse the real thing

Full papers, in the paper's real duration, at the time of day your exam will actually run. Marked the same day, with the error log updated the same evening.

### Phase 4: Days 3 to 1, protect what you have

No heroics. A short recall session each day, logistics settled, sleep restored. The marks you will earn are mostly already in your head; the aim is to let them come out.

## How do you triage subjects when you cannot revise everything equally?

Triage means ranking your topics by how many marks you can still gain per hour spent, and spending your time in that order. It is not the same as revising what you find hardest. A topic that is both weak and heavily examined is a high priority; a topic that is weak but appears rarely, or is so far from your current level that an hour gains nothing, is a candidate for dropping.

Start with a table per subject. Rate each topic on two axes: how well you can currently score on it (red, amber or green) and how many marks it typically carries in the papers. Then apply the rules in the table below.

| Topic status | Weight in the papers | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Red, high weight | Large | Repair first, in Phase 1 |
| Amber, high weight | Large | Question practice in Phases 1 and 2 |
| Red, low weight | Small | Learn the easy marks only, then leave it |
| Amber, low weight | Small | One short session, then retrieval only |
| Green, any weight | Any | Keep alive with one question a week |

The rows that matter most are the first and the fourth. Students under stress either avoid the red high-weight topic (because it hurts) or over-invest in the amber low-weight one (because it is winnable and feels good). The table forces the right order.

### The 20-minute rule for deciding to drop a topic

Give any red topic one honest 20-minute attempt: read the explanation, do two questions. If you can do neither with the explanation open, the topic needs teaching, not revision. Either book a tutor session on it in Phase 1 or drop it. Do not drift into a third hour of confused rereading; that hour is the single most common way a final month is wasted.

### Triage across six subjects

Six subjects means competing claims. A workable split in Phase 1 is by gap, not by love: the subject with the largest distance between your current grade and your target gets the most hours, but never all of them. Keep every subject touched at least every second day, because a subject left alone for a week loses more than the hours suggest.

A DP student with HL Mathematics, HL Physics and HL Economics, for example, will usually find the two HL sciences and mathematics fight for evenings. Putting the language and humanities subjects in the morning, when memory and reading run best, and the quantitative subjects in the late afternoon, when problem-solving holds up for most students, reduces the fight. Adjust to your own energy; the principle is to assign subjects to the time they suit, not to the time they are left over.

## Days 30 to 22: a diagnostic week that ends in a written plan

The first nine days are the only ones in which you can still change course cheaply. Use them to collect evidence.

:::steps A diagnostic pass for each subject
Gather | Mocks, class tests, past papers, teacher comments
Tag | Mark each lost mark by cause, not topic alone
Rank | Count marks lost per cause and per topic
Plan | Write the topic list for Phase 2
:::

### Tag losses by cause

For every mark lost in your latest mock and in two or three recent past papers, write one word beside it: **content** (did not know it), **method** (knew it, chose wrong route), **execution** (slip, units, arithmetic), **reading** (misread the question or command term), or **time** (ran out). Add up the tags. The result usually surprises students. In many scripts the content column is the smallest and the execution and reading columns are the largest, which means the cure is practice habits rather than more notes.

### The two numbers that matter by Day 22

Per subject, you want two numbers: the marks you lose to content gaps, and the marks you lose to everything else. If the second is bigger, your Phase 2 is mostly timed questions with strict marking. If the first is bigger, Phase 2 still starts with repair. Write the plan on one page per subject. A plan that cannot fit on one page is not a plan.

### Use your mock honestly

Your DP2 mock examinations, whose dates your school sets, are the most realistic evidence you have. Do not just look at the grade. Re-sit the questions you got wrong, cold, a week later. A question you can now do is a content problem solved; a question you still cannot do is a Phase 1 task.

## Days 21 to 12: question practice in pieces

By Day 21, the rule changes. For each session, the default is questions, not notes. Notes are the thing you consult when a question exposes a gap.

### The two-pass method for any practice question

Attempt the question closed-book. Then mark it against the markscheme line by line, not just the final answer. Then redo any lost part from scratch, without looking, the next day. The redo is where learning happens. Most students skip it and then wonder why the same marks vanish in the next paper.

### Sectional practice before full papers

Do not jump straight to full papers if you are not yet fluent. Work in this order across the phase:

1. Topic sets: 6 to 10 exam questions on one topic, untimed, marked strictly.
2. Mixed sets: questions from three or four topics shuffled, so you must recognise what each one is asking.
3. Half papers against the clock: half of a real paper in half its time.
4. One full paper, as a pressure test at the end of the phase.

The shuffling step matters more than students expect. A topic set tells you the question is about quadratics before you start; the real paper does not. Mixed sets train the recognition skill that decides whether you pick the right method in the first two minutes.

### What retrieval practice looks like in a real subject

For subjects with heavy recall (Biology, Economics, History, Business Management), replace rereading with blank-page recall. Close the notes, write everything you know about a topic on a blank sheet for five minutes, then check against the notes and fill gaps in a different colour. The coloured gaps are your revision list. For calculation-heavy subjects, the equivalent is closing the formula booklet and writing the key relationships from memory before solving.

### Where command terms fit in

Roughly a third of lost marks in humanities and sciences come from answering a different question to the one asked: describing when the term was *evaluate*, listing when it was *explain*. In Phase 2, underline the command term on every question you attempt and write one line on what depth it demands. The full catalogue with worked examples is in the [guide to IB command terms](/blog/ib-command-terms-explained-with-real-examples/); you only need the ones your own subjects use most.

## How should you rotate past papers across six subjects?

Rotate by paper type, not by subject alone, and by weakness, not equally. In the final month, a sensible target is one timed paper or half paper per day in Phase 3 and a mix of sectional work and half papers in Phase 2, with the subject chosen by a simple rule: the subject you have practised least recently, unless a subject has a paper in the next three days.

Here is a rotation that works for a six-subject student with two HL sciences and one HL humanities, over a 12-day stretch.

| Day | Morning paper (or half) | Afternoon repair block |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Maths Paper 1 (no calculator) | Maths error log, redo lost marks |
| 2 | Physics Paper 2 | Physics Paper 1 multiple choice |
| 3 | Economics Paper 1 essays (timed) | Economics data diagrams |
| 4 | Chemistry Paper 2 | Chemistry calculations drill |
| 5 | Maths Paper 2 (calculator) | Languages: timed writing |
| 6 | Rest half-day, light recall only | Sleep catch-up |
| 7 | Biology Paper 2 | Biology data-based questions |
| 8 | Physics Paper 2 again, different year | Physics error log |
| 9 | Economics Paper 2 data response | Economics evaluation paragraphs |
| 10 | Maths Paper 1 again | Redo, then Paper 3 style questions if HL |
| 11 | Language paper, timed | TOK and loose ends |
| 12 | Chemistry Paper 1 and Paper 2 sections | Mixed recall |

Adapt the subjects to yours. The structure is what matters: every paper type appears at least twice in 12 days, a half-day of rest sits in the middle, and the afternoon block is repair, never new papers.

### Do not run out of past papers

A common worry in the last 30 days before IB exams is that the stock of official papers is small. Past papers and markschemes come through your school or the IB's official channels, and many subjects have only a handful of recent years in the current syllabus format. Use them carefully: do the most recent first, save the single best-matched paper for Days 5 to 4 as your final rehearsal, and use question banks and older papers for sectional practice. Check the current subject guide before leaning on older papers; some syllabuses were revised and older questions can cover content that is no longer examined.

### Do a paper as a document, not a score

After each paper, produce one page: score by section, three marks lost to execution, three lost to content, one habit to change tomorrow. A score tells you where you stand; the page tells you what to do. A student who completes nine papers and writes nine pages is far better placed than one who completes fifteen and writes nothing.

:::bars Illustrative split of a Phase 3 study day
Timed paper or half paper | 40 | 40%
Marking against the markscheme | 25 | 25%
Error log and redo | 20 | 20%
Recall of formulae or definitions | 15 | 15%
This is a typical split, not an official figure. Marking and repair together take more time than the paper itself.
:::

### The paper you should not be doing

If you are still doing untimed practice papers with open notes on Day 8, you are practising a different exam to the one you will sit. Timing is a skill: pace per mark, a rule for moving on, a minute at the end for checking units and signs. It decays if you do not use it, and it takes several full papers to develop. Start timed work no later than Day 14 for your highest-priority subjects.

## Building the daily structure: hours, blocks and breaks

Most students overestimate how many productive hours a day contains. Four blocks of 75 to 90 minutes with real breaks between them give a quality day of five to six working hours; going beyond that mostly adds hours that look good in a diary and teach nothing.

A day that follows this rhythm looks like this:

- **Block 1, early morning:** the subject with the heaviest recall load, or the paper you are doing that day, while you are fresh.
- **Block 2, late morning:** repair of the lost marks from yesterday's paper.
- **Block 3, afternoon:** mixed question practice in a second subject.
- **Block 4, evening:** a timed half paper or recall session, ending at least two hours before sleep.

Place movement and food honestly. A 20-minute walk and a proper lunch are not wasted time; students who skip them report a mid-afternoon slump that costs more than the walk did.

### The one-line daily review

At the end of each day, write one line: "Today I fixed ___ and still lose marks on ___." Over thirty days that is thirty lines, and the repeated phrases in the second half of each line tell you exactly what Phase 3 must cover. It also replaces the question "how many hours did I study?" with "what changed?", which is the question that predicts results.

### A realistic day for a six-subject student

Consider a DP2 student on Day 18 with two HL sciences. Block 1: Chemistry mixed questions on organic reactions, 80 minutes. Block 2: redo the lost marks from Monday's Physics paper, 60 minutes. Lunch and a walk. Block 3: Economics essay plan, then one timed essay, 75 minutes. Block 4: Maths Paper 1 section B, timed, 60 minutes, then 20 minutes of formulae recall. In bed by 11. Nothing in that day is dramatic; the effect comes from repeating it with small adjustments for three weeks.

## Subject-by-subject priorities for the final month

The structure above is the same for every subject. The content of each block changes. These are starting points; the subject guide and your teacher's feedback set the real priorities.

### Mathematics (Analysis and Approaches or Applications and Interpretation)

Focus on the marks that slip: working shown, notation, units and accuracy, and the last parts of long questions that students abandon. Practise the non-calculator paper separately from the calculator one, since they train different habits. HL students should rehearse the extended Paper 3 style of question: break a long investigation into parts and bank the early parts instead of freezing at the hard one. For the full subject detail on AA HL, the flagship guide on [how to score a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/) covers the paper structure and the mistake patterns; for a topic-by-topic approach see the [step-by-step AA HL strategy](/blog/how-to-score-a-7-in-ib-maths-aa-hl-step-by-step-strategy/).

### Sciences (Physics, Chemistry, Biology)

From the first assessment in 2025, the sciences have Paper 1 in two parts (1A multiple choice and 1B data-based questions) and Paper 2 with short-answer and extended-response questions. Phase 2 for sciences is data practice: unfamiliar graphs, tables and experimental set-ups, because the papers reward application to a new context more than recall of a known one. Practise units, significant figures and the habit of identifying the principle before reaching for an equation. If a topic keeps defeating you in chemistry, the [why students struggle in IB Chemistry](/blog/ib-chemistry-why-students-struggle-how-to-fix-it/) article explains the common sticking points.

### Economics and other humanities

These subjects are lost on structure and evaluation, not on knowing the theory. In Economics, Paper 1 is extended response and Paper 2 is data response, with Paper 3 (a policy paper) for HL only. Practise diagrams drawn fast and labelled correctly, a real-world example for each major concept, and an evaluation paragraph that actually weighs something. A timed essay plan, five minutes, is a better use of a Phase 2 afternoon than a polished essay written over an hour.

### Languages

English A students rehearse unseen passage analysis for Paper 1 and comparative essay structure for Paper 2. English B and other Language Acquisition students need timed writing and listening or reading practice; keep one short writing task a day alive from Phase 2 onwards. Language skills decay quickly without use and rebuild quickly with it, so small daily doses beat one long weekend session.

## What should you do in the last three days before the first exam?

In the last three days, stop learning new material and do three things: short recall of formulae and definitions, one light timed set per paper type, and a full logistics check. Study blocks shrink to two or three hours a day, finish by early evening, and leave long gaps for walking, food and sleep. The goal is to arrive at the first paper rested, organised and calm.

### Day 3, Day 2, Day 1

- **Day 3:** last mixed set in your first-paper subject; review your error log; clear the desk of finished piles.
- **Day 2:** recall sheets only (one page per subject); pack the bag; confirm the venue route and timing.
- **Day 1:** one hour of recall, then stop. A walk, an early dinner, a fixed bedtime.

Students who study until midnight the night before a first paper trade a few remembered facts for a worse performance on the day. The facts you gain at midnight are the ones you were least likely to retain anyway.

### Between exams, not just before the first

The month does not end on Day 0. Exams run across about two weeks, and the plan for the gaps matters. On each evening between papers, spend 45 to 60 minutes on the *next* paper and nothing on the one you just sat. Do not hold post-mortems with friends: someone will have written a different answer, and you cannot change yours.

## Sleep, food and the body in the last 30 days before IB exams

Sleep is the cheapest revision tool most students sacrifice first. Memory consolidation, attention and error-checking all depend on it. Aim for a consistent bedtime and wake time from about Day 14, so that your body clock is stable by the time exams start, and treat a night of five hours as a loss to repair the next day, not a badge of effort.

### A sleep plan that survives study leave

Study leave tempts students to drift later every night. Counter it by fixing the *wake* time first, because you can control when you get out of bed more easily than when you fall asleep. Keep screens off for the last hour. Keep caffeine to the morning. If you are naturally a late sleeper, shift in 30-minute steps across a week instead of a sudden jump.

### Food, water, movement

Eat three real meals. A student surviving on snacks and energy drinks reports a crash around mid-afternoon and poor focus, which looks like "lack of motivation" but is mostly blood sugar. A 20 to 30 minute walk or any light exercise most days improves sleep and mood more than the time it takes. None of this is glamorous; it is what top performers actually do.

### Stress that does not go away

If your anxiety about exams is affecting your sleep for more than a week, or you are having panic symptoms, speak to your school counsellor or your family doctor. Exams matter, but not at any price, and extra support exists for a reason. A parent who notices a child who has stopped eating or sleeping should raise it calmly and early, and a tutor can help by bringing the work back to a size that feels manageable.

## The error log in the final month: how to make it work

An error log is a list of the marks you lost and why. Done well, it is the best revision document you own; done badly, it is a diary of guilt that nobody reads. The difference is in the format and in the habit of reading it.

| Date | Subject and question | Marks lost | Cause tag | Fix | Retested on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14 Apr | Physics P2, Q4 (c) | 2 | Method | Use energy conservation first | 18 Apr |
| 15 Apr | Maths P1, Q9 | 3 | Execution | Check signs on the second line | 19 Apr |
| 16 Apr | Econ P1, essay 2 | 4 | Reading | Question said evaluate, I described | 20 Apr |

The last column is the one students leave blank. A fix you have not retested is a hope, not a fix. Four days later, attempt a fresh question of the same type; if you can do it, tick it. If you cannot, the fix goes back on the list.

### Read the log, not just write it

Every third day, read the whole log for one subject and look for repeated cause tags. Three execution slips in a row on signs means a physical habit (write the sign on a separate line) rather than three separate errors. Once you see a pattern, a single rule fixes five marks. The [step-by-step guide for AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-a-7-in-ib-maths-aa-hl-step-by-step-strategy/) describes how this works for one subject in more detail.

## What do students lose marks to in the final month?

Students lose marks in the last month to four things: passive revision that feels useful, unbalanced subject time, practice without marking, and exhaustion. All four are habits, which means all four can be changed within a week, and none of them requires extra intelligence or extra hours.

### Passive revision

Rereading, highlighting and rewriting notes feel productive because they are comfortable. They test nothing. If you finish a session and cannot name something you can now do that you could not do before, the session was passive.

### Balanced neglect

The subject you are good at gets constant attention because it feels rewarding; the one you dread gets none. Look at the hours per subject once a week. The distribution should look like your gap table, not your preferences.

### Practice without marking

A past paper that is not marked line by line is a wasted three hours. Mark with the markscheme beside you, circling the exact mark you missed and the phrase that earned it. Notice the vocabulary of the markscheme; it shows what examiners are listening for.

### Over-polishing

Beautiful notes, colour-coded schedules, new apps. A modest, finished plan beats an elegant unfinished one. If you spend Day 29 building a spreadsheet, you have spent the best day of Phase 1 on stationery.

## Logistics: sort these before Day 7

A surprising number of lost marks have nothing to do with revision. Close the list below before the exams begin so that nothing competes for your attention in the exam fortnight.

| Item | Check | When |
|---|---|---|
| Personal timetable | Dates, start times, paper numbers | Day 30 |
| Candidate number and ID | Where it is kept | Day 14 |
| Permitted equipment | Calculator model and mode, batteries, pens, ruler | Day 10 |
| Calculator exam mode | Memory cleared if required by your school | Day 7 |
| Venue and route | Time to reach it, a backup plan | Day 5 |
| Clothing and water | Layers for a cold hall, a clear bottle if allowed | Day 2 |
| School rules on phones | Where devices are left | Day 2 |

For calculator papers in Maths and the sciences, practise on the exact calculator you will use, in the mode you will use. Students who borrow a new model the week before often lose minutes to unfamiliar menus. Your coordinator confirms which models and conditions are allowed in your centre; do not rely on a friend's recollection.

## Parents: what helps and what does not

The most useful thing a parent can do in this month is keep the house boring. Regular meals, a quiet study space, a stable bedtime, no new arguments, and no daily questions about marks. Asking "how many hours did you do?" invites a defensive number. Asking "what are you working on tomorrow?" invites a plan.

A parent can also check the logistics list above without turning it into a lecture, and can notice when a student has stopped sleeping or eating. What does not help: comparing with cousins, adding a new tutor for every subject in Week 3, or booking an overseas visit during study leave. If a tutor is helping, the useful role is a few targeted sessions on a specific, named weakness, not a daily timetable of three-hour lessons on top of school and self-study.

### Study space in an Indian home

Many Gurgaon apartments are lively places: visiting relatives, building maintenance, construction in the next tower, a pressure cooker in the evening. If the student's room is not quiet, agree on two fixed silent blocks a day and make it a household rule, rather than asking the student to tolerate noise. A shared study room in a library, or a quiet spot at a neighbour's, is a fair alternative in the last fortnight.

## When does a tutor help in the last 30 days, and when does one not?

A tutor helps when there is a specific, named gap that self-study has not closed: a topic you have attempted three times and still cannot do, a paper type where you consistently run out of time, or a subject where you cannot mark your own work honestly. A tutor does not help if the problem is volume (too many subjects, too little time) or avoidance, because a session on a topic you did not prepare for becomes a lecture, not a repair.

Used well, a tutor in this month does three jobs. They diagnose the three or four recurring error patterns in a subject from your latest papers. They run one or two focused sessions on each, ideally with you attempting questions while they watch your method, because the mistake is in the working, not the answer. And they mark a timed paper with you, explaining where the marks were lost against the scheme.

Used badly, a tutor becomes a comfort: a few extra hours that fill the diary without changing the error log. A good test after two sessions is simple: has the error log for this subject shortened? If not, change the plan.

### What a tutor may and may not do

For exams, a tutor can teach, question, mark and coach. For internal assessments, the rules are stricter: submitted work must be the student's own, and the IB does not allow anyone to write or rewrite it. By the last 30 days IAs are almost always submitted, so this is mostly settled, but the principle is covered in the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/) for any family still finalising anything.

### Finding help in Gurgaon

IB Gram is an independent platform that matches families with verified tutors. A family sends a query or books a free consultation, IB Gram asks about the programme, subject, level, school calendar, location and schedule, and a shortlist of fitting tutors follows, subject to tutor availability. A demo session can be arranged before committing, lessons run at home, online or hybrid, and the family can ask for a different match. For exam-focused work see the [IB exam preparation tutors in Gurgaon](/ib-exam-preparation-tutor-in-gurgaon/) page and the [IB DP tutors in Gurugram](/ib-tutors/gurugram/dp/) listing.

## What does a calm exam fortnight look like?

A calm exam fortnight is one where each day has a single main job: sit today's paper, recover, prepare the next one. The student wakes at a fixed time, eats breakfast, arrives early, sits the paper, leaves without a post-mortem, and spends the evening on 45 minutes of preparation for the next paper and then stops.

### The morning of a paper

Wake at your fixed time. Eat something you know sits well. Read your one-page recall sheet once, not five times. Leave early enough that traffic is a nuisance, not an emergency; in Gurgaon, a morning crunch on a main road can double a journey. Arrive, breathe, and go in.

### In the first five minutes of a paper

Use reading time to scan the whole paper, mark the questions you can bank quickly, and decide your pace per mark. Start with a question you are confident about, because early marks calm the nervous system. If you freeze, write down what you know about the question; method marks are available for correct setup even when the answer fails.

### After the paper

Do not discuss answers. Do not check the textbook. Eat, rest, and move to the next paper. If a paper goes badly, you cannot change it, and your only job is to protect the next one. Students who spiral after a hard Paper 1 and lose Paper 2 as a result are the clearest example of a preventable loss.

## Common scenarios and what to do

Real students rarely fit the generic plan. A few common situations, and the adjustment each one needs.

### "I have a month and one subject is at a 3"

Do not abandon the subject, and do not give it all your hours. Choose the two or three topics that carry the most marks and that you can reach, and drill them to a 4. A move from 3 to 4 in one subject can matter for the diploma total more than a move from 6 to 7 in another, particularly if you are near the points threshold. Speak to your coordinator about how the failing conditions apply to your situation; they differ by candidate.

### "My mock was a disaster and I have lost confidence"

A mock is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Spend a day on the error tagging described earlier; the first thing students notice is how many marks came from fixable habits. Confidence follows evidence. Replace the vague belief "I am bad at this" with the specific "I lose 12 marks to units and reading; I can fix those".

### "I am a strong student and I am bored"

Strong students sometimes coast in the final month because everything feels familiar. The cure is harder questions, not more of the same: unseen problems, higher-mark questions, and timed work at 90 per cent of the real time limit. If the plan is easy, raise the standard.

### "My school finishes teaching late"

Some schools complete content close to the exams, which compresses revision. In that case, treat the content catch-up as Phase 1 and shorten Phase 2; start timed practice earlier on completed topics so the skill is built in parallel. Be honest with your teacher about what you have not yet seen.

### "I am retaking one subject"

A retaking candidate often has good recall but old habits. Their month is weighted heavily toward Phase 3 with the error log from the previous attempt open. Check with your coordinator about what is carried forward, since internal assessment marks are usually kept, and plan only for the externally assessed papers.

## The final 24 hours: a checklist you can quote

1. Pack your bag: ID, candidate number, permitted equipment, calculator checked, spare batteries and pens.
2. Confirm your start time, the venue and your route, with a margin for traffic.
3. Do one hour of light recall from your one-page sheet, then stop.
4. Eat a normal dinner and drink water through the evening.
5. Switch off screens an hour before bed and set two alarms.
6. Sleep at your fixed bedtime; do not read the textbook "just once more".

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many hours a day should I study in the last 30 days before IB exams?

Aim for five to six focused hours, not ten. Four blocks of 75 to 90 minutes with proper breaks, adjusted for your school's study leave, is enough for most students. Beyond six hours the quality of work drops and sleep suffers. If you feel you need more, check whether your hours are active (questions, marking, recall) or passive (rereading), and fix that first.

### Should I learn new topics in the last month?

Only if a topic is heavily examined and you can reach it quickly. If a topic carries many marks and you have not seen it, learn the core of it in Phase 1. If it is a small topic far from your current level, skip it and protect the time for subjects where an hour gains more. Never start a new topic in the last three days.

### How many past papers should I do?

Fewer, marked properly, beat more, marked badly. A reasonable target is one to two full timed papers per major subject in Phase 3, plus half papers and sectional work in Phase 2. The number matters less than the line-by-line marking and the redo of lost marks. Save your best-matched paper for Days 5 to 4.

### What if I fall behind my plan in the first week?

Cut, do not stretch. Remove the lowest-priority topics from the plan instead of adding hours, because a plan that depends on extra sleep loss is already broken. Check the plan against your gap table: if something is low weight and low status, it goes. Reset on the next morning without drama.

### Is it too late to hire a tutor with a month to go?

Not if the need is specific. A tutor for a named gap, such as a stubborn topic or paper-timing, can help within two or three sessions. A tutor to "cover everything" in a month will mostly add hours. IB Gram can send a query to shortlist verified tutors for a demo session, subject to tutor availability. Decide after the demo whether the fit is right.

### Should I stop revising the day before the first exam?

Do light recall for an hour or so, then stop. The day before is for logistics, food and sleep. A calm student with 90 per cent of the knowledge usually beats a tired one with 95 per cent. If you feel the urge to cram, write it into a one-page sheet and read it once in the evening.

### How do I stop panicking when I look at the timetable?

Break it into days, then into blocks. A month is overwhelming; today's four blocks are not. Write down the plan, do the first block, and let the feeling follow the evidence. If the panic stays high and interferes with sleep or eating for more than a week, speak to your school counsellor or doctor.

### Can I use the same plan for IGCSE exams?

The logic carries over, since triage, timed papers and error logs work anywhere, but the details differ. IGCSE has different papers, tiers and series months. For that, see the [IGCSE six-week revision plan](/blog/igcse-revision-six-week-plan/), which is written for the Cambridge and Pearson Edexcel structure.

### What if my tutor and my teacher disagree on how to approach a topic?

Follow the markscheme and your teacher's feedback on the school's own papers first, then ask the tutor to show where the markscheme supports their approach. Examiners mark against the scheme, not against any individual's preference. Where two methods are both valid, pick one and use it consistently; switching between them in the last month costs more than either method gains.

### How do I know the plan is working?

Check the error log, not the hours. After two weeks the number of marks lost to repeated causes in each subject should be falling, your timed papers should finish with a minute to spare, and your recall sheets should shorten. If none of those has moved, change one thing, usually the quality of marking, and review again in a week.
