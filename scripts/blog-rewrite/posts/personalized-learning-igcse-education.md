Personalised learning in IGCSE means that a student's revision and practice are decided by evidence about that student, not by the order of the textbook or the habits of the class. It is a method, not a purchase. A motivated Grade 9 or 10 student with a parent who checks in once a week can run most of it at a kitchen table, and the families who do usually learn far more about what actually moves marks than the families who outsource the whole thing from the first day.

This guide is for IGCSE students, Cambridge or Pearson Edexcel, and their parents. It lays out a system with five parts: a diagnostic you can do in a weekend, a revision schedule built on spaced retrieval, an error log that tells you what to fix next, a tier and subject decision framework, and a weekly review that keeps the plan honest. It then explains, without selling anything, the situations where outside help adds something a family cannot supply alone.

## What Is Personalised Learning in IGCSE?

Personalised learning in IGCSE is a way of studying in which the student's own results, errors and calendar decide what to practise next. It starts by finding out what the student can and cannot do in each subject, turns that into a short priority list, schedules practice so that topics are revisited at increasing intervals, records every lost mark with its cause, and reviews all of this weekly. The student's plan changes as the evidence changes.

This differs from the usual alternatives in three ways. Classroom teaching must move at the pace of the whole group. Textbook revision goes chapter by chapter whether or not a chapter needs it. Crash revision before the exam series treats every topic as equally urgent. Personalised learning ranks topics by the marks you are likely to recover per hour spent, which is almost never the order the book is written in.

It is also not the same as learning styles. The idea that a student is a "visual learner" or an "auditory learner" and should be taught only that way has little support in the research on how people learn. What does differ between students is prior knowledge, error patterns, reading speed, confidence and the time available. Those are the things to personalise around.

## Why IGCSE Rewards This Approach

IGCSE in India means Cambridge IGCSE (Cambridge International Education) or Pearson Edexcel International General Certificate of Secondary Education (International GCSE), usually a two-year course across Grades 9 and 10. Several features of the qualification make a personal plan worth building.

**Many subjects at once.** A typical student sits six to nine subjects. Each has its own paper types, command words and practical component. A single "study more" instruction cannot cover that.

**Tiers.** Many Cambridge subjects have Core and Extended tiers. Core tops out at grade C and Extended reaches A*. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher. The tier choice shapes the ceiling and the content, and it should be made with evidence, not hope.

**Exam series.** Cambridge exam series run in February/March (a series offered only in India), May/June and October/November. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE series run in January and May/June. Schools decide which series they enter students for, and a student cannot choose a series independently of the school. The family's plan has to work backwards from the actual date.

**Syllabus codes and paper structures.** Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (from the 2025 exams) has Core candidates sitting Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator), and Extended candidates sitting Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Cambridge IGCSE sciences (syllabuses examined from 2023) have multiple choice at 30%, theory at 50% and practical assessment at 20%, with the practical through Paper 5 or Paper 6, chosen by the school. Syllabuses change, so always check the current document from the board.

For a wider tour of the exam side, see [IGCSE exam preparation](/blog/igcse-exam-preparation-guide/), which covers entries, series and paper types.

## Step One: The Weekend Diagnostic

A diagnostic is a structured look at where you are now. It should take one weekend, not a month, and it should produce a short document you can act on. Do it at the start of Grade 9, at the start of Grade 10 and after every set of school mocks.

### Gather what already exists

Collect the last two or three returned tests or mocks in each subject. Do not rely on memory. For each, write down the paper, the mark, the topics tested and the teacher's comments. If there is a mark scheme or examiner report, keep it with the paper.

### Redo one paper under exam conditions

For one or two subjects where there is doubt, sit a past paper with the time limit and no notes. Mark it with the mark scheme, line by line, in a different colour. The aim is not the total. The aim is the pattern of lost marks.

### Sort every lost mark into a cause

For each mark lost, choose one cause from a short list. A workable list is: did not know it, knew it but could not recall it, chose the wrong method, made a slip, misread the question, ran out of time, or answered at the wrong depth for the command word. Count the causes. The biggest count is your first priority, and it is often not the one you expected.

| Cause counted | Looks like | First response |
|---|---|---|
| Did not know it | Blank answers, guesses | Learn the topic, then test it a week later |
| Could not recall it | Felt familiar, nothing came | Retrieval practice, not rereading |
| Wrong method | Started well, hit a wall | Mixed practice that forces a choice |
| Slip | Sign, unit, rounding | A built-in check step at the end |
| Misread | Answered a nearby question | Underline command word and data first |
| Out of time | Last questions blank | Timed sections, mark-per-minute rule |
| Wrong depth | "Describe" written as "explain" | Practise command word ladders |

Doing this once for each of your main subjects gives you a map no textbook can give. It is also the part of personalisation most commonly skipped, because it is uncomfortable to see.

## Step Two: Turn the Diagnostic Into a Priority List

A priority list has at most five items per subject and ranks them by expected return. To rank, ask two questions of each weakness. How many marks is it costing across a typical paper? How quickly could it be fixed?

A weakness that costs six marks and can be fixed in two weeks (for example, forgetting the units and significant-figure rules in Physics calculations) goes above a weakness that costs eight marks and needs three months (for example, a large conceptual gap in organic chemistry). Both need attention. The first should start today.

Consider a typical Grade 10 student working through an Extended Mathematics mock. The diagnostic might show that most lost marks come from three places: handling algebraic fractions, reading the calculator papers too fast and losing the "give your answer to 3 significant figures" instruction, and running out of time on Paper 4's final two questions. The priority list that follows is not "revise algebra". It is a fraction drill, an underlining habit and a timing rule. That is personalisation.

## Step Three: A Revision Schedule Built on Spaced Retrieval

Retrieval practice means testing yourself on something from memory, rather than rereading it. Spacing means revisiting it at increasing gaps. Research on learning has consistently found that retrieval and spacing both beat rereading and massed cramming for long-term retention, and they fit IGCSE well because the exams reward recall and application together. The exact intervals are a rule of thumb, not a law.

A workable scheme for a student is as follows. When a topic is first learned in class, do a short self-test the same day. Test it again after about a week. Test it again after about two weeks, then about a month, then about two months before the exam series. Each test is short and specific, with a handful of questions from memory, and any failure goes in the error log and returns to the schedule sooner.

:::timeline Illustrative spacing for one topic
Day 0 | Learn it, then test yourself the same evening
Day 7 | Five questions from memory, no notes
Day 14 | Mixed with two other topics
Day 30 | Past-paper question on it, timed
Week of exam | One last check on anything failed twice
:::

### A weekly shape that works

Rather than planning each day separately, plan a weekly shape and let the contents change. A sample for a student with school until mid-afternoon:

1. **Daily, 30 minutes.** One subject, retrieval from the schedule: questions, flashcards, or a blank-page recall of a topic.
2. **Two evenings, 45 minutes.** A timed section of a past paper in the subject you most want to move.
3. **One weekend block, 90 minutes.** A full or near-full paper in one subject, marked the same day with the error log.
4. **Sunday, 20 minutes.** The weekly review, covered below.

That is about six hours a week of deliberate work on top of school and homework. For most students that is more than enough. Doubling it rarely doubles the result, and it often ends in burnout.

### What retrieval looks like in each subject

- **Mathematics.** Do not reread solutions. Close the book, take a question type, and solve a fresh example. Once a week, mix topics so the method has to be chosen, not assumed. The method for using past papers in particular is covered in [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/).
- **Sciences.** Write out a process or definition from memory (for example, the stages of the carbon cycle or the steps in a titration), then compare it with the syllabus. Rework calculations from the start, with units. Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610, Physics 0625 and Chemistry 0620 each cover topic lists that double as retrieval checklists, and the Pearson Edexcel equivalents are 4BI1, 4PH1 and 4CH1.
- **English.** Retrieval looks different: write a paragraph from memory using a model structure, re-read an old answer and rewrite it at a higher level, or summarise a text aloud in 90 seconds. The communication side is covered in [IGCSE English communication skills](/blog/igcse-english-communication-skills/).
- **Economics, Business Studies, Computer Science.** Practise definitions and the structure of an analytical answer (point, evidence, explanation, consequence), and for Computer Science write short pseudocode from memory and trace it.

### What to do when the calendar is crowded

A schedule that depends on perfect weeks will fail in the first mock fortnight. Build in a rule: when a week is lost, do not try to catch up on everything. Move the tests due that week forward by one week, drop the least valuable item from the priority list, and keep the daily 30 minutes alive even in a bad week. Streaks of small blocks are more reliable than heroic weekends.

## Step Four: The Error Log That Drives the Plan

The error log is a notebook or document with one line for every mark lost in practice. It is the single most useful personalisation tool, because it is the only thing that records what this particular student does wrong.

Each entry has four fields: the date and paper, what the question asked, what the student did, and what to do next time. The last field is the one that matters. It must be a behaviour, not a feeling. "Be more careful" is not a behaviour. "Underline the command word and circle the units before starting" is.

A short sample, written as illustrative entries rather than any real student's:

- *Paper 4, Q7(b):* asked for the area of a sector. Used degrees in the formula for arc length in radians. Next time: write the formula first, tick the units.
- *Chemistry Paper 4, Q5:* "explain why" answered as "describe". Next time: add the reason after every observation.
- *English reading, Q3:* quoted a whole sentence when one word was needed. Next time: choose the shortest quotation that proves the point.

### How to use the log weekly

On Sunday, read the week's entries and look for repeats. If the same behaviour appears three times, it becomes a priority item for the next week. If an item has not appeared for a month, retire it. In the last six weeks before an exam series, the log is also the best revision material you have, because it is a list of the exact things this student gets wrong.

A log kept honestly for two months usually shows that two or three habits explain most of the lost marks. That is encouraging, because habits can be changed.

## Step Five: Choosing the Tier With Evidence

For subjects with tiers, the decision between Core and Extended (Cambridge) or Foundation and Higher (Pearson Edexcel mathematics) is among the most consequential choices in the course. A student placed too low is capped. A student placed too high is exposed to content they are not ready for, and may gain less than in the lower tier.

Evidence for the decision can be collected without waiting for the school:

1. **Take a past paper from the Extended tier** in the subject, under timed conditions, in the second half of Grade 9.
2. **Mark it with the mark scheme** and record the percentage and the causes of lost marks.
3. **Check whether the losses are fixable.** If most are slips and recall gaps, the student is probably an Extended candidate in training. If most are "did not know it", with whole topics blank, the gap is larger.
4. **Talk to the subject teacher with the paper in hand.** The school makes entry decisions, and a specific paper helps the conversation.
5. **Revisit in Grade 10 term one** with another paper.

The cost of the decision is also asymmetric. Moving up a tier early can be managed with a stretch plan. Moving up late may not be possible. The special case of Mathematics, including the Additional Mathematics decision, is in [the IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

A note on grading: Cambridge grades most syllabuses A* to G, and some syllabuses have 9 to 1 versions under different codes. Pearson Edexcel International GCSE grades 9 to 1. Always check which scale applies to the specific syllabus code the school has entered the student for.

## Step Six: The Weekly Review

The weekly review is 20 minutes, usually on Sunday, and it is where personalisation stays alive. Without it, a plan drifts into a routine. With it, the plan adapts.

1. **Count what happened.** How many of the planned blocks were done? Be honest and do not judge.
2. **Read the error log.** Which behaviours repeated?
3. **Check the next two weeks of school.** Tests, deadlines, practical sessions, trips.
4. **Choose three priorities for the week.** No more than three.
5. **Decide one thing to drop.** A plan with no deletions grows until it fails.

A parent can join the review as a listener, and the best question a parent can ask is "what is the one thing you are going to change this week?". The answer shows whether the student is learning from the log.

## How Should Personalisation Differ Between Subjects?

Personalisation differs between subjects because each assesses a different skill, and the cause of lost marks usually differs with it. The sensible adjustment is to weight the plan by what each paper rewards. Maths rewards method selection and accuracy. Sciences reward recall, calculation and precise wording. English rewards control of purpose and audience. Economics and Business Studies reward structured analysis. A student who studies all of them the same way is not personalising.

:::bars Illustrative: how the daily 30 minutes might be split by subject type
Maths: mixed problems, no notes | 40 | 40%
Sciences: recall and calculations | 30 | 30%
English and humanities: writing | 20 | 20%
Reading around and review | 10 | 10%
A typical starting split, not an official figure. Move it towards whichever subject the diagnostic shows is weakest.
:::

### Mathematics

Maths is where personalisation most visibly pays, because the errors are so countable. Common patterns include algebra gaps carried forward from earlier years, the non-calculator paper's arithmetic fluency, problem-reading under pressure, and misuse of the calculator. For top-grade strategy in Extended, the dedicated resource is [A* in IGCSE Maths 0580](/blog/how-to-score-a-star-in-igcse-maths-0580/). For the most common individual errors with worked examples, see [top 10 mistakes in IGCSE maths](/blog/top-10-mistakes-in-igcse-maths/).

### Sciences

Three issues recur: command words (a "state" answer where "explain" was asked), calculations with units, and the practical paper. The practical paper (Paper 5 or 6 in Cambridge) is often neglected because it feels like a school-lab matter, but it carries a fifth of the weighting. Practise it by reading old practical papers and tabulating results from the data given, not only by doing experiments.

### English

IGCSE English is several different syllabuses: Cambridge IGCSE First Language English 0500, English as a Second Language 0510 and 0511, and Literature in English 0475, with Pearson Edexcel offering International GCSE English Language A (4EA1), English Language B (4EB1) and English as a Second Language (4ES1). Confirm which one the student is entered for before planning anything. Personalisation here means identifying whether the student's weakness is reading inference, writing control or accuracy, and practising that precisely.

### Economics, Business Studies and Computer Science

For Economics 0455 and Business Studies 0450, the plan should include timed analytical answers and calculations (break-even, cash flow, ratios in Business). For Computer Science 0478, the plan should include tracing code and writing short algorithms in pseudocode, since Paper 2 is built on them.

## What a Parent's Role Looks Like

The parent's role in personalised learning is more modest, and more useful, than the role of teacher. Four jobs matter.

- **Keep the calendar.** Know the exam series, the mock dates and the school's deadlines, and put them where the family can see them.
- **Protect the blocks.** Defend the daily 30 minutes and the weekly paper from being absorbed by other commitments, without nagging during them.
- **Ask process questions.** "What is in your error log this week?" is better than "what did you get?".
- **Watch for overload.** Sleep, appetite, mood and the number of subjects falling behind at once are early signs. A plan that costs sleep is a bad plan.

A parent does not need to understand the content. A parent who can ask "how do you know that is right?" without being able to check the answer is doing a real service, because the student has to explain.

## Where Does the Method Break Down?

Honesty requires saying where self-run personalisation stops working. Four situations are common.

**The student cannot find the cause of an error.** Sometimes the log says "wrong method" and the student cannot say what the right method would be. The student needs explanation, from a teacher, a tutor or a good source. More practice will not help.

**The same topic keeps failing despite repeated retrieval.** After two or three honest attempts across a month, a topic that will not stick usually has a deeper gap beneath it, such as fractions underneath algebra or the mole concept underneath stoichiometry. Finding it needs someone who can question the student and see where reasoning breaks.

**Motivation has gone.** A student who has lost belief in a subject may not run any plan, however good. This is a conversation first, and a methods question second.

**Time has run out.** Within a few weeks of an exam series, a diagnostic and a plan are still useful, but the choices are about triage. Some gaps will be accepted, and a second pair of eyes helps decide which.

## When Does Outside Help Add Real Value?

Outside help is worth adding when it supplies something the family system cannot: expert diagnosis, explanation, accountability or time. It is not worth adding as a default. The honest list of cases:

| Situation | Does outside help add value? | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Student runs the system and marks are rising | Rarely | Perhaps one review before mocks |
| One topic repeatedly fails despite retrieval | Often | A short block aimed at that topic |
| Tier decision is unclear | Sometimes | A diagnostic paper and an honest opinion |
| Student cannot explain errors | Yes | A tutor who questions, not lectures |
| Weeks before the exam series, several gaps | Often | Triage and a timed-paper schedule |
| Student wants accountability | Sometimes | A weekly check-in, short and specific |
| Motivation is the problem | Not first | Conversation, school support |

If you do decide to add a tutor, treat the tutor as part of the system, not a replacement for it. Hand over the diagnostic, the priority list and the error log in the first session. A tutor who ignores them and begins at the next chapter has not personalised anything. ## A Worked Example: Eight Weeks Before the May/June Series

Consider a typical Grade 10 student with eight weeks before the May/June series and six subjects. The mock results show Mathematics in the middle of the Extended range, Physics uneven, Chemistry strong, Biology adequate, English steady and Economics weak on analytical writing.

The weekend diagnostic sorts lost marks: in Maths, mostly slips and unfinished last questions; in Physics, calculations with units and one topic (electricity) that is a real gap; in Economics, answers that define and stop. The priority list follows. Maths gets a check-step habit and a timing rule. Physics gets a two-week repair on electricity and a daily unit check. Economics gets weekly timed analysis paragraphs with a model structure.

The schedule that results has a weekly shape: five daily 30-minute blocks rotating through the subjects, two evening timed sections (Maths and Physics in the first four weeks, shifting to Economics and Biology in the second four), and one weekend paper with marking and logging. Sunday reviews retire items that stop appearing, and by week four the Physics electricity gap has moved from the priority list to a once-a-fortnight retrieval slot.

No tutor is involved. If at week three the student still cannot explain series and parallel circuits after repair attempts, that is the moment to bring in a few sessions with someone who can ask questions and watch the reasoning break. That is outside help doing what the family cannot.

## Common Mistakes in Self-Run Personalisation

- **Planning too much.** A plan with eleven priorities has none. Cap it at three a week.
- **Rereading as revision.** It feels productive and is among the weakest methods for retention.
- **Marking leniently.** Giving a mark for "nearly" removes the information the log needs.
- **Skipping the weak subject.** Everyone drifts to what they like. The plan must schedule the rest.
- **Never retiring items.** A priority list that only grows becomes a to-do list.
- **Treating the log as punishment.** It is a tool. Anyone who feels bad reading it will stop writing it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is personalised learning in IGCSE?

It is a study approach in which a student's own results, errors and calendar decide what to practise and when. It starts with a diagnostic, ranks priorities by marks recoverable per hour, schedules spaced retrieval, records every lost mark with its cause in an error log, and reviews weekly. It can be run by a student and parent at home, with or without a tutor.

### Can IGCSE students personalise their learning without a tutor?

Yes, and many do. A diagnostic weekend, a short priority list, daily retrieval blocks, a weekly past-paper section and an error log cover most of the method. A tutor becomes useful when the student cannot explain why errors happen, when a topic keeps failing despite practice, or when time before the exams is short and triage is needed.

### How much time should an IGCSE student spend each week?

Around six hours of deliberate revision a week, beyond school and homework, is a sensible baseline for most students: five daily blocks of about 30 minutes, two evening timed sections and one weekend paper. Increase gently in the last six weeks before an exam series. If sleep suffers, the plan is too heavy and should be cut, not extended.

### What is spaced retrieval and does it really work?

Spaced retrieval means testing yourself from memory on a topic at widening intervals, such as the same day, after a week, then two weeks and a month. Research on learning has consistently found that retrieval and spacing beat rereading and cramming for long-term retention. The exact gaps are a rule of thumb, so adjust them to the exam date.

### How do I choose between Core and Extended?

Use evidence, not hope. Sit an Extended past paper under timed conditions, mark it with the mark scheme, and look at whether the lost marks are slips and recall gaps or whole topics missing. Then discuss the paper with the subject teacher, since the school makes entry decisions. Core tops out at grade C, so a capable student should not stay there by default.

### My child studies a lot but marks do not improve. What is going wrong?

The usual cause is that the study method is rereading or copying, not retrieval and mixed practice, or that effort goes to comfortable topics. Run the diagnostic: sort lost marks by cause and compare that with how hours are spent. If effort and errors do not line up, change the plan before adding hours. If errors are unexplained, a short tutor block can help.

### Is an error log really necessary?

It is the most useful single tool, because it is the only record of what this student does wrong. Without it, revision defaults to topics, and the real problem is often a habit such as ignoring units or misreading command words. Keep it simple: one line per lost mark, with a behaviour for next time, reviewed weekly.

### How is personalised learning different from personalised home tuition?

Personalised learning is the method, which a family can run alone. Personalised home tuition is a service in which a tutor delivers the method one-to-one. A good tutor uses the same ingredients, a diagnostic, a plan, an error log and reviews, and brings explanation and accountability. The tutoring version is described in [personalised home tuition for IGCSE students](/blog/personalized-home-tuition-igcse-students/).

### When is the right time to bring in a tutor?

Bring one in when the method has been run for four to six weeks and a specific problem persists: unexplained errors, a topic that will not stick, an unclear tier decision, or too little time before the exams. Do not bring one in only because marks dipped once. Give the tutor the diagnostic and error log so they start from evidence.

### What if my child resists the whole system?

Start smaller. Ask for one 30-minute retrieval block and a five-line error log for two weeks, and let the student pick the subject. Resistance often means the plan feels like surveillance. Make the student the owner and the parent a listener. If resistance persists across several weeks, the issue is probably motivation or workload, and a conversation with the school may help more than a better schedule.

### Does personalised learning work for students from CBSE or ICSE moving to IGCSE?

Yes, and it matters more in that case. Students switching boards meet different command words, more explanation and different paper structures. A diagnostic on a real IGCSE paper shows which of these costs the most marks, and the plan can target them. Equivalence and entry rules are a separate matter for the school and receiving institutions.
