The MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D are the four lenses through which every Middle Years Programme (MYP) maths task is marked: Knowing and understanding (A), Investigating patterns (B), Communicating (C) and Applying mathematics in real-life contexts (D). Each is scored 0 to 8, the four add up to a total out of 32, and that total converts to a final grade from 1 to 7. A student can be excellent at one lens and weak at another, which is why a "good at maths" child sometimes comes home with a puzzling report.

This guide is for parents and students in MYP 3, 4 and 5 who want to understand what each criterion actually rewards, what top-band work looks like, which kinds of task test it, and what to practise. It is the hub for IB Gram's MYP mathematics guides, so every other guide is linked at the end with a line on what it covers. It is written with the Gurgaon family in mind, but the criteria are the same wherever the school is. For how the criterion levels turn into a report grade, and what to ask the teacher, the companion piece is [MYP maths criteria explained for parents](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/); this page goes deeper on the skills behind each letter.

One caution. Schools write their own task-specific clarifications, and the MYP mathematics guide is the authority on wording, so treat the descriptions of the strands below as a plain-language orientation, and check the exact descriptors your school hands out.

## What are the MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D?

The MYP mathematics criteria are four assessment criteria: A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating, and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. Each is scored from 0 to 8 across four bands of descriptors. The criterion total out of 32 converts to a final 1 to 7 grade through IB grade boundaries. Schools often split MYP 4 to 5 mathematics into standard and extended.

The Middle Years Programme runs for five years (MYP 1 to 5, roughly ages 11 to 16) across eight subject groups, and every subject is assessed against four criteria. There is no single national MYP examination in India. In MYP 5, on-screen eAssessment is optional and each school decides whether to offer it; the format is covered in the [MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment guide](/blog/ib-myp-5-mathematics-eassessment-preparation-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/).

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score range per criterion
32 | Maximum criterion total
1-7 | Final MYP grade scale
:::

| Criterion | Name | In one line | A typical task |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Knowing and understanding | Select and apply the right mathematics correctly | Unit test with routine and non-routine questions |
| B | Investigating patterns | Find, state and justify a general rule | Open investigation with a number or shape pattern |
| C | Communicating | Express reasoning clearly, with correct language and forms | Any written task; marked on the working |
| D | Applying mathematics in real-life contexts | Model a real situation, solve it and judge the answer | A project or problem set in an authentic context |

Because each criterion is marked separately, a child may score 7 in A and 3 in B on the same unit. That is information, not failure: it tells you which skill to train. The rest of this guide takes each in turn.

## Why do the four criteria matter more than the final grade?

The criteria matter because the final 1 to 7 grade hides which skill is weak, while the four criterion scores show it. A student who understands the mathematics but writes little loses marks in C. One who calculates well but cannot start an open problem loses marks in B. The criterion profile tells a family what to practise.

Consider two students who both end with a final grade of 5. The first scores high in A and D and low in B and C: strong calculation and good context sense, but thin investigation and sparse written reasoning. The second scores the opposite pattern. The same grade, the same hours of study, completely different training plans. A tutor who responds to the grade alone ("needs to work harder") gives both the same advice; a tutor who reads the profile gives them different homework.

That is also why report-reading is a skill. The next steps, such as which criterion moves fastest and what each upgrade requires, are explained in [how a MYP maths student moves from a 5 to a 6 or 7](/blog/how-can-an-ib-myp-maths-student-move-from-5-to-6-or-7/). Here we focus on what each criterion is made of.

## What does Criterion A (Knowing and understanding) reward?

Criterion A rewards selecting appropriate mathematics and applying it correctly to solve problems, in both familiar and unfamiliar situations. It is the closest of the four to a traditional test: correct methods, correct results, and the ability to adapt when the question looks different from the textbook example. The top band requires accuracy plus flexibility, not memorised procedures alone.

In broad terms, Criterion A has three strands: selecting appropriate mathematics, applying it, and solving problems correctly in a variety of contexts. Lower bands reward routine problems solved with few errors; higher bands expect correct solutions to more complex or unfamiliar problems.

### What top-band Criterion A work looks like

- Correct method chosen without being told which method to use.
- Few or no errors in calculation and algebra, including multi-step problems.
- Problems in unfamiliar settings solved by recognising the underlying structure.
- Answers that are the right kind (a length, a probability, an expression) as well as numerically right.

### Typical Criterion A tasks

Unit tests, topic quizzes, end-of-term examinations and short problem sets. In MYP 5 with eAssessment, the on-screen examination leans on this skill too.

### Common Criterion A mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Memorised procedure, no meaning | Works the textbook example, freezes on a variation | Ask "what is this method doing?" before drilling it |
| Sign and fraction slips | Right method, wrong answer | A check line: substitute back or estimate |
| Wrong tool chosen | Uses a formula for a different shape or situation | Write what is known and what is wanted before choosing |
| Skipping to the answer | Correct number, no route | Write at least one line per step |
| Misreading the question | Answers a similar question | Underline the command and the quantity asked for |

### How to practise Criterion A

Mix topics, not only blocks. A student who has just done ten quadratics questions knows which method to use because the worksheet says so. A mixed set of ten questions on five topics forces selection, which is what the criterion rewards. Add one "explain why" prompt per set, such as "why does this factorisation work?" The depth of understanding behind procedures is the subject of the guide to [concept-first MYP mathematics tutoring](/blog/concept-first-ib-myp-mathematics-tutoring-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/), with worked contrasts between procedure and concept.

## What does Criterion B (Investigating patterns) reward?

Criterion B rewards discovering a pattern in an open task, describing it as a general rule, and then justifying or proving that the rule holds. The marks are for the process of exploring, conjecturing, testing and generalising, not only for the rule itself. A student who writes the correct rule with no evidence or justification earns a middle band at best.

In broad terms, the strands cover selecting and applying problem-solving techniques to discover patterns, describing the pattern as a general rule consistent with the findings, and verifying or proving that rule. Higher bands expect complex patterns, a general rule stated correctly in algebra, and a justification that goes beyond testing a few cases.

### What top-band Criterion B work looks like

- A systematic table or diagram of cases, not a few random ones.
- The pattern described in words first, then as an algebraic rule.
- The rule tested on a case not used to find it.
- A justification: why must it work, not only that it worked three times. At the top band this may be a proof or a structural argument.

### A worked illustration

Suppose a task asks students to investigate how many handshakes occur when n people each shake hands once with every other person. A student who records n = 2, 3, 4, 5 gives 1, 3, 6, 10 and states "the rule is add one more each time" has found a recursive pattern but not a general rule. A stronger student sees that each of the n people shakes hands with n minus 1 others, which counts every handshake twice, so the rule is n(n − 1)/2. They then test n = 6 (15 handshakes) and explain the double count. The mathematics is within MYP reach; the difference is the habit of asking *why*. That is B.

### Common Criterion B mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too few cases | Pattern claimed from two or three examples | Record at least five to six, in order |
| No structure | Cases chosen at random | Build a table, change one thing at a time |
| Rule without a statement of scope | "It works" with no description | State what the variables mean and when the rule applies |
| Testing, not justifying | "I checked and it works" | Explain why the structure forces the rule |
| Stopping at the first rule | Recursive rule only | Convert to a closed form if the task allows |

### How to practise Criterion B

Do short investigations regularly, not only before an assessment. Ten minutes on a sequence of squares, a number puzzle or a growing diagram, with a rule to state and a reason to give, is enough. Reasoning skills by MYP year, including generalising, justifying and proving, are developed in the guide to [problem-solving and reasoning skills in IB MYP Mathematics](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/).

:::steps An investigation routine for Criterion B
Explore | Try small cases and record them in a table
Notice | Describe the pattern in words
Generalise | State the rule using variables
Justify | Explain why it works, then test a new case
:::

## What does Criterion C (Communicating) reward?

Criterion C rewards correct mathematical language, appropriate forms of representation, moving between forms, and a clear, complete and logically organised line of reasoning. It is the criterion that rewards showing the route. A right answer with no working earns little here, and a clear explanation with a minor slip can earn a lot.

In broad terms, Criterion C covers using mathematical language (terms and notation), using appropriate forms of representation such as tables, graphs, diagrams and equations, moving between those forms, communicating complete, coherent and concise reasoning, and organising information logically. Higher bands expect all of these to work together without the reader having to guess.

### What top-band Criterion C work looks like

- Variables defined before use; units and labels present.
- Correct notation: equals signs used only when two quantities are equal, brackets where needed.
- A graph, table or diagram where it helps, and a sentence connecting it to the argument.
- Working in logical order, each line following from the one before, ending with a statement that answers the question.

### Why Criterion C is often the cheapest to improve

Many students lose marks in C not because they cannot communicate but because they were rewarded for answers in earlier years. A student who writes "x = 12" for a task that asks for reasoning may know the mathematics fully. The fix is habit, and habit responds quickly to targeted practice.

### A worked contrast

Task: a rectangle has perimeter 36 cm and its length is twice its width. Find the area.

A thin answer: "area = 128".

A communicating answer: "Let the width be w cm. Then the length is 2w cm. The perimeter is 2(w + 2w) = 6w = 36, so w = 6. The length is 12 cm. Area = 6 × 12 = 72 cm². The area is 72 cm²."

The thin answer is also wrong, and a reader cannot tell why. The second shows a variable defined, an equation set up, a solution and a labelled answer. Even with an arithmetic slip, the second earns most of the available marks.

### Common Criterion C mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Answer only | A number with no route | Write the set-up and one line per step |
| Undefined symbols | Letters appear without meaning | Begin with "Let x be..." |
| Chain equals signs | 2 + 3 = 5 × 4 = 20 | Write each step on its own line |
| Missing units or labels | Graph without axis labels | A checklist for every diagram |
| Conclusion missing | Working ends without an answer | End with a sentence in the question's words |

### How to practise Criterion C

Take a solved problem and rewrite it as if for a reader who has never seen it. Swap with a friend or parent and ask them to find the first line they could not follow. Practise moving between forms: given a table, draw the graph and write the equation. The confidence and routine-building side of this, including error logs and timed practice, is in [MYP maths exam confidence](/blog/ajay-vatsyayan-sir-ib-myp-mathematics-exam-confidence-gurgaon/).

## What does Criterion D (Applying mathematics in real-life contexts) reward?

Criterion D rewards identifying the relevant mathematics in an authentic situation, choosing and applying a strategy to reach a solution, then judging how accurate the solution is and whether it makes sense in context. The final two steps are the ones students forget: stating the degree of accuracy and checking the answer against reality.

In broad terms, the strands cover identifying relevant elements of an authentic real-life situation, selecting appropriate mathematical strategies, applying them to reach a solution, explaining the degree of accuracy, and explaining whether the solution makes sense in the context. Higher bands expect all five, not just the calculation.

### What top-band Criterion D work looks like

- The relevant information is picked out, and assumptions are stated openly.
- A model or method is chosen with a reason, and the working is correct.
- The answer is given with an appropriate degree of accuracy, and the reason for that accuracy is explained.
- A sentence judging whether the result is sensible: does a negative length, a 140% discount or a speed beyond a car's limit make sense?

### A worked illustration

Consider a task about comparing two mobile plans with different fixed and per-minute charges. A student sets up a linear model for each plan, finds the number of minutes where they cost the same, and states "the plans are equal at 250 minutes". A top-band answer goes further: it notes that the model assumes usage is billed per exact minute, that a real plan rounds up, that the break-even is therefore approximate, and that for a user who talks about 100 minutes a month the cheaper plan is the one with the lower fixed fee. The mathematics is the same; the judgement is what is rewarded.

### Common Criterion D mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Treating context as decoration | Pulls numbers and ignores the situation | Write what each quantity means before calculating |
| No assumptions stated | Model appears from nowhere | List two or three assumptions in a sentence each |
| Accuracy ignored | Eight decimal places for a cost | Match accuracy to the context and say why |
| No reasonableness check | Answer left unquestioned | Ask "could this happen in real life?" |
| Overlong set-up, thin solution | Pages of description, little mathematics | Set a balance: brief context, strong model |

### How to practise Criterion D

Use authentic data: a school canteen menu, a train timetable, a phone plan, a sports table. Each time, finish with two sentences: how accurate is this answer, and does it make sense? The routines for modelling and for gradually removing the tutor's scaffolding are covered in [MYP maths problem solving and Criterion D tasks](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/).

## How do MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D work together in one task?

In one extended task, MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D show up in sequence: understand the mathematics (A), investigate a pattern or relationship (B), explain the reasoning clearly (C), and apply the result to a real situation (D). Most strong assessments touch more than one, and a weakness in one can pull down the others.

Think of a unit on linear growth. A test checks the method (A). An investigation asks students to explore how different starting values and rates of change affect a graph, state a general rule and justify it (B). Every written part is also read for language, notation and the logic of the working (C). A final project asks students to model a real subscription, savings or travel situation and judge the result (D). A student who understands the idea but rushes the writing loses on C even in a task labelled "investigation".

:::timeline How one MYP maths unit often runs
Weeks 1-3 | Concepts and procedures, with a short quiz (A)
Weeks 4-5 | Open investigation of a pattern (B), written up clearly (C)
Week 6 | Real-life task and reflection (D)
Week 7 | End-of-unit test mixing all criteria
Schools set their own sequence; this is an illustrative pattern.
:::

Because tasks are often marked by criterion, the same piece of work can reveal different strengths. Ask your child's teacher which criteria each task assesses, and read feedback by letter, not only by total.

## How are the criteria different in MYP 3, MYP 4 and MYP 5?

The four criteria stay the same across the years; what changes is the complexity of the mathematics and the independence expected. In MYP 3 tasks are more scaffolded; in MYP 4 and 5 the investigations are more open, the real-life contexts more complex, and communication is expected to be tighter. Schools often split MYP 4 and 5 into standard and extended mathematics, with extended covering additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches.

| Year | What typically changes |
|---|---|
| MYP 3 | More guided investigations; routine contexts; building the habit of showing working |
| MYP 4 | Less scaffolding; algebraic generalisation expected; early standard versus extended choice |
| MYP 5 | Complex contexts; justification and proof; possible on-screen eAssessment if the school offers it |

The topic map and the way MYP 4 and 5 feed into the choice between DP Analysis and Approaches (AA) and Applications and Interpretation (AI) are in [MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics tutoring](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/). The later DP decision is explained in the [AA versus AI guide](/blog/ib-maths-aa-vs-ai-which-one-should-you-choose/), and the school's DP coordinator makes the final placement.

## How do you diagnose which criterion is weakest?

To work out which of the MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D is weakest, read the criterion scores across several tasks, comparing the pattern, and then checking the scripts for the reason. One low score is a data point; the same letter low across three tasks is a pattern. The reason usually sits in one of a few places.

1. **Collect the last three or four marked tasks** with criterion scores and comments.
2. **Tabulate the scores by letter.** Which letter is lowest on average? Which varies most?
3. **Read the teacher's comments** for the lowest letter and underline the specific words: "no justification", "notation", "context ignored".
4. **Match the comment to a mistake** in the tables above.
5. **Choose one habit to change** for the next task and write it at the top of the page.
6. **Re-check** after the next task to see whether the letter moved.

Criterion C | 50 | 4 of 8
Criterion D | 62 | 5 of 8
An example profile, not a record of a real student. The totals depend on the school's marking.
:::

In that example the total is 19 out of 32 and the lowest letters are B and C. That points the practice towards short investigations and a rewrite habit, not towards more A-type worksheets. A tutor, if one is used, should read the profile in the same way. ## What does a tutoring session look like when it targets one criterion?

A criterion-targeted session starts from the student's own marked task, names one letter and one habit, practises that habit on a fresh problem, and ends with a short independent task. The tutor asks more than tells, and the student does most of the writing. The aim is for the student to need the tutor less each week.

Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, frames MYP maths as a progression: Concept, Pattern, Application, Expression. The student first builds a firm understanding of the idea (A), learns to recognise relationships (B), applies the idea in unfamiliar and real-life contexts (D), and finally learns to communicate the reasoning clearly (C). The order is not a rule of the criteria, which are separate, but a way of making sure each skill has something to stand on. He also emphasises "why before how": understanding why a method works makes the procedure adaptable when a question changes.

:::figure signature-ajay:::

How a session runs in practice, for a student in MYP 4:

1. **Opening (10 minutes).** The student explains, in their own words, what the last task asked and where marks were lost.
2. **Targeted work (30 minutes).** For a weak B, a fresh investigation: the student explores, the tutor asks "what do you notice?" and "how could you be sure?", and the student writes the rule and the justification.
3. **Rewrite (10 minutes).** The student rewrites a piece of old working so a stranger could follow it, addressing C.
4. **Task for the week (5 minutes).** One short investigation or context problem to complete alone, with a note on what to look for.

A parent should see the original attempt, the feedback, the corrected attempt and a later transfer task. ## When does a child not need a tutor for MYP maths?

A child does not need a tutor when the criterion profile is balanced, the teacher's feedback is specific and the student acts on it. Outside help makes sense when one letter is persistently low, when feedback is too general to act on, when a school change has opened gaps, or when confidence is the issue. Many students do well with school teaching and steady habits at home.

Before paying for help, try three things: ask the teacher what the lowest criterion needs in one sentence, run the six-step diagnosis above, and set a four-week experiment on one habit. If the letter moves, the family has its answer. If not, the experiment has produced evidence for a better conversation with a teacher or a tutor. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the four MYP mathematics criteria?

They are A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. Each is scored 0 to 8, the total out of 32 converts to a final 1 to 7 grade, and each describes a different skill: correct use of mathematics, discovering and justifying rules, clear reasoning, and modelling real situations. Your school's descriptors give the exact wording.

### How is the final MYP maths grade calculated?

The four criterion scores are added to give a total out of 32, and the total converts to a 1 to 7 grade through IB grade boundaries. The boundaries are set by the IB, so the same total gives the same grade. The mechanics, including which criterion moves a grade fastest, are covered in the guide on moving from a 5 to a 6 or 7.

### Which criterion is hardest for most students?

It varies, but Criterion B and Criterion D are often the least familiar because they ask for open exploration and real-life judgement, which traditional maths teaching rarely rewards. Criterion C is often the cheapest to improve because the loss comes from habit, not ability. The only reliable answer for your child is the pattern across their last three or four tasks.

### My child gets correct answers but low marks. Why?

Usually because the marks are for the route and the reasoning, not only the answer. A correct number with no working earns little in Criterion C, and an investigation with a rule but no justification earns a middle band in B. Ask the teacher which criterion the marks were lost in, and practise showing a complete line of reasoning for every task.

### How can I help at home without doing the maths?

Ask your child to explain a problem aloud, as if teaching you, and ask "how do you know?" after each answer. Read their working and mark the first line you cannot follow. Encourage a short weekly investigation or real-life question. You do not need to know the mathematics to ask for clarity, reasons and a sensible-answer check.

### What is the difference between standard and extended MYP maths?

Schools often split MYP 4 and 5 into standard and extended mathematics. Extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches. The decision belongs to the school, based on the student's readiness and plans, and families should ask what evidence supports the placement and when it can be revisited.

### Is MYP maths very different from CBSE maths?

Yes, in approach. MYP rewards investigation, communication and application as well as correct answers, and tasks are marked against criteria, while many CBSE assessments emphasise fixed solutions and recall. Students switching in either direction need time to adapt. The comparison and the switching issues are in the guide to MYP maths versus CBSE maths.

### Can a tutor help with MYP investigations and projects?

A tutor can teach the skills, such as exploring systematically, justifying a rule and writing clearly, and can question the student's reasoning. They should not write or complete the assessed work. The work must be the student's own, and the school's policies on academic honesty apply. If you are unsure, ask the teacher where the line sits.

### Does the eAssessment change the criteria?

The criteria remain the same, but the on-screen format changes how some tasks are presented and how working is entered. eAssessment in MYP 5 is optional and each school decides whether to offer it. Ask your school whether your child will sit it, and see the dedicated eAssessment guide for the format and a preparation plan.

### How long does it take to improve one criterion?

Often several weeks of focused practice shows movement in a habit-based criterion such as C, while B and D can take longer because they rely on exploration and judgement. A four-week experiment on one habit, followed by a look at the next task's score for that letter, gives evidence. No one can promise a particular improvement.

## Next steps

1. Gather your child's last three or four marked maths tasks, write the four criterion scores for each in a small table, and circle the letter that is lowest most often.
2. Choose one habit for that letter (for example, define variables and write a conclusion for C, or explain why a rule works for B) and run a four-week experiment.
3. After the next task, check whether that letter moved, and decide with the teacher, or with a tutor if you use one, what to change.
