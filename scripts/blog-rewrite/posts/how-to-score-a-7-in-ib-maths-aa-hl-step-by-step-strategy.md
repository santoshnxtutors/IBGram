If you want a 7 in Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches (AA) Higher Level (HL), you cannot revise "maths". You have to revise five different subjects that happen to share a syllabus: Number and algebra, Functions, Geometry and trigonometry, Statistics and probability, and Calculus. Each one loses marks in its own way, each one is rescued by a different kind of drill, and a student who gives them equal time usually ends up with the wrong shape of revision. This IB Maths AA HL topic-by-topic strategy goes through all five, names the errors that actually cost marks on scripts, lists the question types worth drilling and ends every section with a short self-test you can run tonight.

It is written for International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) students in DP1 and DP2, and for parents who want to see what real preparation looks like beyond "do more past papers". It is the topic-level companion to the overall system in [how to score a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/), which covers grade boundaries, method marks, the study cycle and the error log. Read that for the machinery. Read this for what to do inside each topic. Where another guide owns a subject, such as paper timing or the graphic display calculator (GDC), this post gives a sentence and a link instead of repeating it.

## How This Topic-by-Topic Playbook Works

The syllabus has five topics, and the examination papers draw on all of them. Paper 1 (no calculator) and Paper 2 (GDC allowed) each run 120 minutes for 110 marks and sample the whole course; Paper 3 gives two long, scaffolded problems that tend to join several topics together; the Internal Assessment (IA) is a mathematical exploration worth 20% of the grade. Check the current subject guide for exact details, because the IB revises documents.

:::stats AA HL at a glance
5 | Syllabus topics examined
3 | Written papers
20% | Internal Assessment weight
7 | Top grade on the 1-7 scale
:::

For each topic, the playbook uses the same five parts, so you can compare them and skip to the one you need.

1. **What examiners tend to test.** The shape of questions, not a list of chapter titles.
2. **Where marks leak.** Errors seen repeatedly on scripts at the 5-6 boundary.
3. **Must-drill questions.** The question types that repay repetition.
4. **The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** What changes when the calculator is, or is not, allowed.
5. **A self-test.** Four or five questions you should be able to do cold before calling the topic done.

The self-tests are deliberately short. They are not a replacement for past papers; they are a smoke alarm. If you fail one, you know where the fire is.

## Which AA HL Topic Should You Master First?

Start with Number and algebra, then Functions, because everything else leans on them. A student with shaky algebra loses marks in calculus, vectors and statistics without ever realising the cause. Functions comes second because transformations, composites, inverses and asymptotes appear inside nearly every other topic. Calculus then gets the largest block of time, with Geometry and trigonometry and Statistics and probability interleaved around it.

That is the order for teaching and for rebuilding. For revision in DP2, the order changes: sort by what your own error log says. A student whose last three mocks show most lost marks in trigonometric equations and vectors should not spend a month on perfect algebra drills because algebra "comes first".

:::bars Where revision time should go, for a typical DP2 student (illustrative)
Calculus | 30 | Largest block
Number and algebra | 22 | Foundation
Functions | 18 | Connective
Geometry and trigonometry | 18 | Vectors and trig
Statistics and probability | 12 | Self-contained
This is a suggested starting split, not an IB figure. Re-weight it from your own error log.
:::

## Number and Algebra: Where Paper 1 Is Won or Lost

**What examiners tend to test.** Fluency without a calculator. Sequences and series (arithmetic, geometric, sums to infinity), exponents and logarithms, the binomial theorem, proof (including proof by induction at HL), complex numbers in Cartesian, modulus-argument and exponential forms, de Moivre's theorem, roots of complex numbers, systems of equations, and partial fractions. The questions are rarely hard in concept. They are hard because every line must be correct and nobody lets you check on a screen.

**Where marks leak.**

- Sign slips when expanding brackets, especially a minus in front of a bracket.
- Using the sum-to-infinity formula without checking that the common ratio has modulus less than one.
- Treating log(a + b) as log a + log b.
- Writing the argument of a complex number in the wrong quadrant because the student used a calculator-style arctan on a negative ratio.
- In induction, jumping from "assume true for n = k" to "therefore true for n = k + 1" without showing the algebra that links them, and forgetting the closing statement.
- In partial fractions, setting up the wrong form when a repeated or irreducible factor is present.

**Must-drill question types.**

- Prove by induction that a series formula or a divisibility statement holds for all positive integers. Do five of each. The structure is mechanical once you drill it: base case, assumption, step, conclusion.
- Convert between Cartesian and polar forms, then use de Moivre to compute a power. For example, (1 + i)^8: write 1 + i as √2 times cis(π/4), raise to the 8th power to get 16 times cis(2π), which is 16.
- Find all n-th roots of a complex number and sketch them on an Argand diagram.
- Solve simultaneous equations with a parameter and discuss the number of solutions.
- Expand (a + bx)^n for a requested coefficient, with a negative or fractional-looking term that tests sign care.
- Decompose a rational function into partial fractions as the first step of an integration.

**The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** Paper 1 is where this topic shows. On Paper 2, a GDC polynomial solver can find complex roots, but the student still has to interpret them. Do not let the calculator become the reason you never learn the algebra, because Paper 1 will remove it.

**Self-test (twenty minutes, no calculator).**

1. Prove by induction that the sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared.
2. Write -1 + i√3 in the form r cis θ and find its cube.
3. Find the coefficient of x^3 in the expansion of (2x - 1/x)^7. Before calculating, say which powers of x can appear at all, using the general term.
4. Decompose (3x + 5) / ((x + 1)(x + 2)) into partial fractions.
5. A geometric series has first term 12 and sum to infinity 16. Find the common ratio.

## Functions: The Topic That Hides Inside Every Other One

**What examiners tend to test.** Domain and range, composite and inverse functions, transformations, rational functions with asymptotes, the modulus function, odd and even functions, polynomial theory (factor and remainder theorems, sum and product of roots), and solving equations and inequalities graphically and algebraically. Functions is also the language of modelling in Paper 2.

**Where marks leak.**

- Describing a sequence of transformations in the wrong order. For y = 2f(x - 3) + 1, the horizontal shift and the vertical stretch and shift each have to be stated, and order matters when a stretch and a translation act on the same axis.
- Finding an inverse and forgetting to state its domain, which equals the range of the original function.
- Composite functions: computing f(g(x)) when the question asked for g(f(x)).
- Stating a domain restriction without a reason ("because the function is not one-to-one" earns the mark; a bare interval often does not).
- Solving modulus equations and keeping extraneous roots without checking.
- Reading asymptotes from the graph on a GDC but not stating them as equations, with the correct "x =" or "y =" form.

**Must-drill question types.**

- Given f and g, find f of g, g of f, and the domain of each composite.
- Find an inverse and sketch f and its inverse on the same axes, marking the reflection in y = x.
- Describe a sequence of transformations from a given pair of graphs, and write the new equation.
- Rational functions: vertical, horizontal and oblique asymptotes, intercepts, and a sketch with all of them labelled.
- Solve |2x - 1| < x + 3 both algebraically and by sketching.
- Use the sum and product of roots of a polynomial to find unknown coefficients without finding the roots.

**The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** Paper 1 wants exact algebra and labelled sketches. Paper 2 wants the GDC to find intersections, turning points and asymptote behaviour, followed by a clear statement in context. Fast graph reading is the Paper 2 skill; the technique is covered in [the GDC advantage in AA HL](/blog/the-gdc-advantage-in-ib-dp-mathematics-aa-hl/).

**Self-test.**

1. f(x) = (2x + 1)/(x - 3). State the domain, find the inverse and state its domain.
2. Describe the transformations taking y = x squared to y = 3(x + 2) squared - 5.
3. Solve |x - 2| = 3x + 1 and verify each root.
4. The polynomial x^3 + px^2 + qx - 6 has roots a, b and c with a + b + c = 4. Find p, confirm abc = 6 from the constant term, and state what extra fact you would need to find q.
5. Sketch y = (x - 1)/(x + 2) with every intercept and asymptote labelled.

## Geometry and Trigonometry: Vectors, Identities and the Unit Circle

**What examiners tend to test.** Three-dimensional geometry, the sine and cosine rules, radian measure with arcs and sectors, the unit circle, trigonometric identities including compound angle and double angle formulae, the inverse trigonometric functions, solving trigonometric equations over an interval, and vectors at HL: lines in two and three dimensions, scalar and vector (cross) products, planes, and intersections of lines and planes. The syllabus groups vectors with geometry, which is why a student can spend weeks on "trigonometry" and still be unprepared for the third dimension.

**Where marks leak.**

- Leaving the GDC in degrees when the question uses radians, or the reverse. It is the cheapest mark in the paper to lose.
- Dividing both sides of a trigonometric equation by sin x and silently deleting solutions.
- Giving only the principal solution when the interval asks for all solutions.
- Using the position vector where the direction vector is needed, and vice versa, when writing the equation of a line.
- Concluding that two lines intersect after finding values of the parameters that satisfy two of three component equations, without checking the third.
- Computing the angle between two lines from the angle between a direction and a position vector.
- In sector and arc problems, mixing degrees and radians in the same formula.

**Must-drill question types.**

- Solve a quadratic in sine or cosine over a stated interval. Example: 2 sin squared x - sin x - 1 = 0 for 0 to 2π factorises as (2 sin x + 1)(sin x - 1) = 0, giving x = π/2, 7π/6 and 11π/6. Notice that the equation yields three solutions, not two; students who forget the quadrants lose the third.
- Prove an identity using double angle formulae, writing each line, never "obviously".
- Find the intersection of two lines in three dimensions, or show that they are skew.
- Find the angle between a line and a plane, or between two planes, through the correct vectors.
- Find the Cartesian equation of a plane from three points or from a point and a normal obtained by a cross product.
- Compute the shortest distance from a point to a line.
- Arc length and sector area in context, with an optimisation twist that links back to calculus.

**The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** Paper 1 trigonometry needs exact values from the unit circle (π/6, π/4, π/3 and their multiples) without a calculator. Paper 2 vector questions often allow the GDC for the arithmetic but still need the vector setup written out. A line of notation such as "r = a + λb" with a and b labelled earns marks before any number appears.

**Self-test.**

1. Solve 2 cos squared x + 3 sin x - 3 = 0 for x between 0 and 2π, exact answers.
2. Prove that (1 - cos 2θ)/(sin 2θ) = tan θ.
3. Lines L1: r = (1, 2, 3) + λ(2, -1, 1) and L2: r = (4, 0, 2) + μ(1, 1, -1). Decide whether they intersect, and show every step.
4. Find the Cartesian equation of the plane through (1, 0, 0), (0, 2, 0) and (0, 0, 3).
5. A sector has radius 8 cm and angle 1.2 radians. Find the arc length and the area, then say which GDC mode you needed.

## Statistics and Probability: Short Syllabus, Frequent Setups

**What examiners tend to test.** Descriptive statistics and correlation, probability rules, conditional probability and independence, Bayes' theorem at HL, discrete random variables with expectation and variance, the binomial and normal distributions, continuous random variables defined by a probability density function, and hypothesis testing and related inference. Check the current subject guide for the exact list of distributions and tests examined, because it is the kind of content that is easy to over- or under-prepare.

This is the most self-contained topic. A student who commits two focused weeks can turn it into the most reliable block of marks on Paper 2, and it often forms the backbone of a Paper 3 scenario, as the flagship post notes.

**Where marks leak.**

- Not reading whether the question wants P(X ≤ k) or P(X < k) for a discrete variable.
- Using a normal distribution with the standard deviation squared, or the variance given as the spread.
- Using inverse normal incorrectly: giving the z-score where the real value was needed.
- Forgetting that a probability density function must integrate to 1, and failing to use that fact to find an unknown constant.
- Writing "the test is significant" without a conclusion in the context of the original claim.
- Conditional probability set the wrong way round: P(A | B) taken as P(B | A). A tree diagram or a table prevents this.
- Calculator syntax errors, such as the wrong bounds in a normal CDF.

**Must-drill question types.**

- A two-stage context with a tree diagram, ending in a Bayes-style reversal ("given the test was positive, find the probability the person has the condition").
- Find an unknown parameter from a stated normal probability or from a percentile, using the inverse normal function and the standardised form.
- A continuous random variable with an unknown constant: find k, find the mean and variance by integration, find the median.
- Binomial with "at least" and "at most" statements, plus a follow-up on how many trials are needed for a probability to exceed a threshold.
- A hypothesis-test scenario written out in full: hypotheses, test statistic, p-value or critical region, conclusion in context.

**The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** On Paper 2 the GDC does the heavy arithmetic, so the marks are in the setup and the interpretation. On Paper 1 expect probability reasoning with exact fractions and integration of a pdf by hand.

**Self-test.**

1. A test for a condition is correct 95% of the time for people who have it and 90% of the time for people who do not. If 2% of a population have it, find the probability that a person who tests positive has the condition. Set it up as a tree first.
2. X is normal with mean 50 and standard deviation σ. P(X < 44) = 0.2. Find σ.
3. A pdf is f(x) = kx(4 - x) for 0 to 4, zero elsewhere. Find k, then the mean.
4. A coin is claimed to be fair. In 40 tosses it lands heads 28 times. State hypotheses, find the relevant probability, and write the conclusion in a sentence.
5. How many independent trials with success probability 0.1 are needed before the probability of at least one success exceeds 0.9?

## Calculus: The Largest Topic and the Biggest Mark Reservoir

**What examiners tend to test.** Limits and continuity, differentiation by the product, quotient and chain rules, implicit differentiation, related rates, optimisation, curve sketching with first and second derivatives, integration by substitution and by parts, definite integrals and areas, volumes of revolution, kinematics, differential equations (separable and by integrating factor or other prescribed methods), and Maclaurin series. This is the heaviest topic by volume, and it feeds Paper 3 more than any other.

**Where marks leak.**

- Forgetting the constant of integration in an indefinite integral, and then using the incomplete function in the next part.
- Dropping or inventing a factor when applying the chain rule to a composite such as e to the power of x squared.
- In substitution, changing the variable but not the limits (or the reverse), or leaving dx undealt with.
- In integration by parts, choosing u and dv the wrong way round and generating a harder integral.
- Implicit differentiation: differentiating y terms without the dy/dx factor.
- Optimisation questions where the student finds a stationary point but never justifies that it is a maximum or minimum, and never checks the endpoints or the domain.
- Related rates: writing rates without a clear link equation or with the wrong sign for a decreasing quantity.
- Volumes of revolution: using the wrong formula for rotation about the y-axis, or forgetting π.

**Must-drill question types.**

- Integration by substitution, including a definite integral with changed limits.
- Integration by parts, including one requiring two applications (an integral of x squared times e to the x) and one using the "go round in a circle" trick.
- Implicit differentiation to find a tangent or normal to a curve like x squared + xy + y squared = 7.
- Related rates with a geometric setup, such as a draining cone, a ladder or an expanding circle.
- A separable differential equation with an initial condition, solved for y explicitly.
- A Maclaurin series built from derivatives, then used to approximate a limit or an integral.
- Optimisation with a constraint that must be turned into one variable first.
- Kinematics: from acceleration to velocity to displacement, with the constants fixed by conditions.

**The Paper 1 vs Paper 2 angle.** Paper 1 tests technique: exact antiderivatives, clean substitution, correct notation. Paper 2 uses the GDC for numerical integration and for finding maxima, but still expects the integral to be written down and the answer to be in context ("the area under the curve is the total distance in metres"). Students who work only on Paper 2 style calculus often discover in DP2 that they cannot integrate by hand.

**Self-test.**

1. Find the integral of x e^x dx, then evaluate it from 0 to 1.
2. Use the substitution u = x squared + 1 to find the integral of 2x / (x squared + 1) dx, and state the definite integral from 0 to 2.
3. For x squared + y squared = 25, find dy/dx and the equation of the tangent at (3, 4).
4. A spherical balloon is inflated at 100 cm cubed per second. Find the rate at which the radius is increasing when the radius is 5 cm.
5. Solve dy/dx = 2xy with y = 1 when x = 0.

## How Do You Know a Topic Is Really Done?

A good IB Maths AA HL topic-by-topic strategy needs a definition of finished.

A topic is done when you can pass its self-test cold, explain each method in a sentence, and attempt a fresh question from a past paper without hesitation about the first line. "I have read the chapter" and "I did the exercise" are not evidence. The standard is performance on an unseen question, a week after you last looked at the topic.

Use this simple tracker, which is stricter than the green-yellow-red scheme because it asks for evidence.

| Status | Evidence required |
|---|---|
| Not started | No questions attempted |
| Learning | Can follow worked examples but fail unseen questions |
| Working | Passes the self-test with notes or hints |
| Exam-ready | Passes the self-test cold, then passes it again a week later |
| Maintained | Revisited in a mixed set every two weeks |

Notice the last row. Topics decay. A student who finishes vectors in November and returns in March finds it half-gone. A short mixed set each fortnight, which includes at least one question from every "Exam-ready" topic, is what maintains it.

## Where Do the Marks Actually Go? Common Errors Across Topics

The same families of error repeat across topics. An error log that tags each lost mark by one of these causes will show you what to fix, in a way that "I need to study calculus more" never will.

| Error family | Typical examples | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Algebraic slip | Sign errors, dropped brackets, wrong fraction arithmetic | Write one transformation per line; check by substituting a value |
| Notation and communication | Missing "=" signs, no vector notation, unlabelled sketch | Label every object the first time it appears |
| Method not shown | Mental substitution, GDC answer without a setup line | Write the setup, then use the GDC |
| Wrong mode or setting | Degrees vs radians, wrong distribution parameters | Run a one-line mode check at the start of every paper |
| Misread question | P(X < k) vs P(X ≤ k), "exact" ignored, wrong interval | Underline the command and the constraint before starting |
| Incomplete answer | Only the principal trig solution, no interpretation | Reread the question's last line; answer in context |
| Premature rounding | Intermediate values rounded to three significant figures | Carry full values on the GDC; round only at the end |

If you build only one habit this term, make it this table. Tag the cause for every mark you lose, in every set, for two months. The pattern will tell you where to spend the next fortnight.

## The Question Types That Cross Topics and Decide Paper 3

Paper 3 is hard because it joins topics. The usual pattern is a scaffolded investigation: you prove a small result, extend it, then use it to answer something unfamiliar. Calculus plus complex numbers, vectors plus trigonometry, and probability plus integration all appear in this style.

You cannot predict the pairing, but you can practise the habit. After each topic's self-test, add a "cross-over" question that forces two topics together.

- **Complex numbers and trigonometry.** Use de Moivre's theorem to derive an identity for cos 3θ in terms of cos θ.
- **Calculus and functions.** Show that a function is invertible using its derivative, then differentiate the inverse.
- **Vectors and calculus.** Find the minimum distance between a moving point on a line and a fixed point by differentiating the squared distance.
- **Probability and integration.** Find the mean and variance of a continuous random variable defined by a piecewise pdf.
- **Series and calculus.** Recognise a Maclaurin series as a geometric series and sum it.

The two cross-over questions a week, attempted with no hint about which topics are joined, are the best Paper 3 preparation that does not require a Paper 3 past paper. Paper 3 itself, including timing and how to chase partial marks, is covered in [the paper-by-paper preparation guide](/blog/ib-math-aa-hl-paper-preparation-gurgaon/).

## A Sixteen-Week Plan for Running the Topics

Here is one way to sequence topic work across a term of revision. Adjust the weeks to your school's calendar, because mocks fall at different times in different schools.

1. **Weeks 1-2: diagnose.** Take one mixed paper per paper type under timed conditions, then tag every lost mark by error family. Choose your two weakest topics.
2. **Weeks 3-6: repair the weakest two topics.** Use the topic cycle (learn, mix, self-test, log, re-test) twice per topic. Keep one mixed set per week from the other topics.
3. **Weeks 7-10: calculus block.** Four weeks, because it feeds every paper. Integration technique one week, differential equations and series one week, applications one week, and a cross-over week.
4. **Weeks 11-12: geometry and trigonometry.** Vectors and trig identities; add a plane and line intersection set every second day.
5. **Weeks 13-14: statistics and probability.** Setups and interpretations, plus a hypothesis-test set.
6. **Weeks 15-16: integration of everything.** Full timed papers, cross-over questions and the error log. Re-test every topic you marked "Exam-ready".

:::steps The weekly rhythm inside the plan
Mon | Topic work, new method or repair
Wed | Mixed set across completed topics
Fri | Self-test, no notes, timed
Sat | Full or half past paper, marked strictly
Sun | Error log and re-test list
:::

## Common Questions Students Ask Before They Start

Any IB Maths AA HL topic-by-topic strategy raises the same practical questions in the first session.

The FAQ below covers the questions asked most often. A few extra points belong here first, because they come up in almost every first session.

**Should I do every topic before starting past papers?** No. Start past-paper questions by topic as soon as a topic reaches "Working". Full papers can wait until you have covered most of the syllabus, but isolated questions from the first month tell you what the examiner's version of a topic looks like.

**Should I memorise the formula booklet?** You should know what is in it and where, so you do not waste time hunting, but memorise the derivations you will be asked to produce. Knowing that a formula exists is different from knowing when it applies.

**What if I am strong in one topic and weak in four?** Treat it as an opportunity. A strong topic gives you a model of what mastery feels like. Use the same cycle on the weak ones, and use your strong topic as a source of cross-over questions.

**What if I started late?** Be honest about the calendar, then triage using the error table. A DP2 student with ten weeks has to choose: repair the two most expensive error families, run the cross-over questions and let one low-yield area stay imperfect. A 7 does not need perfection, as the flagship guide explains.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which topic in IB Maths AA HL is the hardest?

There is no single hardest topic; it depends on the student. Most find Calculus the largest and Complex numbers or Vectors the least familiar. The honest way to find your hardest topic is to run each self-test cold and see which one you fail. In an IB Maths AA HL topic-by-topic strategy, the topic with the lowest pass rate in your own attempts, not the one that sounds hardest, should get the first repair block.

### How many hours per topic do I need for a 7?

There is no fixed number, because the starting point differs so much between students. A better measure is the evidence in the tracker: have you passed the self-test cold, and again a week later? A topic you already know may need two hours of maintenance. A weak one may need ten or more across several weeks. Spend hours until the evidence changes, not until a number is reached.

### Should I revise by topic or by past paper?

Do both, in sequence. Revise by topic while you repair, then switch to mixed sets and full papers as topics reach "Exam-ready". Topic work builds the skill; papers test whether you can choose the right topic under pressure. Students who only do full papers keep meeting the same weak spots without fixing them, and students who only do topic work are surprised by the mixture.

### Is Paper 3 really connected to all the topics?

Usually, yes. Paper 3 tends to scaffold an unfamiliar investigation that draws on more than one area, often calculus with another topic. You cannot predict the exact pairing, so practise cross-over questions weekly. The goal is not to guess the topic but to be comfortable reading a long problem, attempting every part and using follow-through marks when a middle part fails.

### How do I stop losing marks to silly mistakes?

Treat them as a pattern, not as bad luck. Tag each one by cause in an error log: sign slip, missing setup line, wrong mode, misread constraint. After two weeks the same two or three causes will dominate, and each has a specific fix, such as writing one transformation per line or running a mode check before each paper. Carelessness that repeats is a habit, and habits can be changed.

### Can I get a 7 if I am weak at algebra?

Yes, but you must repair the algebra first, because it affects every other topic. Spend two or three weeks on a daily fluency set: expanding, factorising, fractions, logs and exponentials, done by hand. Algebra is a trainable skill, and the improvement is usually visible within a month. Weak algebra left unfixed will cost marks in calculus, vectors and Paper 1 for the rest of the course.

### How often should I revisit topics I have already finished?

About every two weeks, in a mixed set. Topics decay if left alone, and the decay is faster for the ones with many steps, such as integration techniques or plane and line intersections. A mixed set with one or two questions from each completed topic costs less than an hour and keeps your "Exam-ready" list honest. Re-test anything you miss within a week.

### Do I need a tutor for this?

Not always. A self-disciplined student with past papers, markschemes and an error log can do all of it alone. A tutor helps when the diagnosis is the bottleneck, when you cannot tell why you lose marks, or when accountability is the issue. The decision guide in [how to choose the right IB maths tutor in Gurgaon](/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-ib-maths-tutor-in-gurgaon/) can help you judge whether to hire one and how to evaluate a trial.

### What should I do if I fail a self-test twice?

Go back one step in the cycle. Re-learn the method with five basic questions, do ten mixed ones, and re-take the self-test only after that. If you still fail, the gap is probably in a prerequisite from an earlier topic, so check algebra and functions. Persistent failure after a proper repair attempt is a good moment to bring the scripts to a teacher or tutor for a second pair of eyes.

### Is this strategy enough on its own?

It is a map of the topics, not the whole system, which is why an IB Maths AA HL topic-by-topic strategy works best beside a general one. Pair it with the study cycle, error log and paper tactics in [the main 7 guide](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/), with timing and paper strategies in the paper-preparation guide, and with calculator technique from the GDC guide.
