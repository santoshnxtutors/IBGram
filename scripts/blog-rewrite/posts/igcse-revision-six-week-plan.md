A six-week IGCSE revision plan works when the six weeks are spent in a fixed order: find what is weak, rebuild it by retrieval instead of rereading, then move to timed papers, and keep the last seven days almost entirely for rehearsal and rest. Six weeks is long enough to change a grade in most subjects and short enough that every hour has to be chosen. Students who spend those weeks rereading notes and highlighting textbooks usually finish feeling busy and are surprised by the paper.

This guide is for IGCSE students in Grade 10 or the equivalent year, and for parents who want to know what a sensible plan looks like without nagging. It covers Cambridge IGCSE and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE, and it is written for several subjects at once, because that is how real students revise: maths, two or three sciences, English and an option subject, all competing for the same evenings. If maths is your main worry, the sibling guides on [IGCSE maths past papers](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/) and the [3-month study plan for IGCSE Maths 0580](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/) go deeper on that one subject; this page is the multi-subject schedule.

## Is Six Weeks Enough to Revise for IGCSE?

Six weeks is enough to improve results meaningfully in most subjects, provided the student has covered the syllabus and is not starting from nothing. It is not enough to learn a whole course from scratch in every subject at once, and a plan that pretends otherwise sets the student up to fail in week four. The honest question is how much of the syllabus is genuinely unfamiliar. If most topics have been taught and are merely rusty, six weeks is comfortable. If an entire unit was missed, the plan needs a separate repair block in the first week.

Think of the six weeks as a funnel. Week 1 is wide: an audit of everything. Weeks 2 and 3 narrow to the topics that need rebuilding. Weeks 4 and 5 move from knowing to performing: timed papers and corrections. Week 6 is narrow again: a few last weaknesses, formulae, command words and sleep. Each stage has a different job, and mixing the jobs is the commonest planning mistake.

:::timeline The six-week funnel
Week 1 | Audit every subject, set priorities
Weeks 2-3 | Rebuild weak topics through retrieval
Week 4 | Mixed practice and first timed sections
Week 5 | Full timed papers and corrections
Week 6 | Final gaps, rehearsal, rest
:::

A word on timing. Cambridge exam series run in February/March (a series offered only in India), May/June and October/November, and Pearson Edexcel International GCSE series run in January and May/June. Schools decide which series they enter students for; a student cannot choose a series independently of the school. Count back six weeks from the first paper in your own timetable, not from the month name, and treat the date as fixed.

## What Does the Six-Week IGCSE Revision Plan Look Like at a Glance?

The six-week IGCSE revision plan gives each week a single purpose, a weekly hour budget and a visible output. The hours below suit a student carrying five or six subjects alongside school, with a heavier load in the final fortnight. They are a guide, not a target; a student who is sleeping badly should lower them before cutting sleep.

| Week | Purpose | Weekly hours (all subjects) | Output at the end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audit and triage | 8 to 10 | Colour-coded topic lists, mistake log started |
| 2 | Rebuild the reds | 10 to 12 | Reds turned amber, first retrieval notes |
| 3 | Rebuild the ambers, start spacing | 10 to 12 | A one-page summary per subject |
| 4 | Mixed practice, timed sections | 12 to 14 | First marked sections, updated log |
| 5 | Full timed papers | 12 to 14 | One paper per major subject, marked |
| 6 | Rehearse, repair, rest | 8 to 10 | Packed bag, formula and definition cards |

The table hides a rule that matters. Total hours should rise gradually and then fall. A student who works at maximum for six weeks is usually a student who is exhausted in week six, which is exactly when the exams arrive.

## Week 1: How Do You Triage Several Subjects at Once?

Triage is the act of finding out, honestly and quickly, which topics in each subject are secure, shaky or missing. Use three colours. Green means you can explain it and answer a past-paper question without notes. Amber means you remember some of it. Red means you cannot start. Do the audit from the syllabus topic list, not from your memory of what felt hard, because memory flatters the topics you enjoy.

Spend the first two evenings on the audit, one hour per subject, and use a quick test for each topic instead of a feeling. Close the notes, write the key ideas for a topic from memory, attempt two questions and mark them. The result is a list of reds and ambers for each subject. This list is the most valuable page in your folder, so keep it somewhere you will see it daily.

### What to audit in each subject

- **Mathematics:** algebra, graphs, geometry, trigonometry, probability, statistics, vectors, mensuration and transformations. For Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580, Core candidates sit Paper 1 (non-calculator) and Paper 3 (calculator); Extended candidates sit Paper 2 (non-calculator) and Paper 4 (calculator). Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) is tiered Foundation or Higher.
- **Sciences:** each separate science has its own topic list. Cambridge IGCSE sciences (syllabuses examined from 2023) include multiple choice (30%), theory (50%) and practical assessment (20%), with the practical through Paper 5 or Paper 6 depending on the school. Edexcel sciences are untiered with practical skills assessed inside the written papers.
- **English:** audit skills, not content: reading for meaning, summary, directed writing, composition, language analysis, and for Literature the set texts and essay planning.
- **Option subjects:** economics, business, computer science or a language each need their own list. Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455, for example, has Paper 1 multiple choice (30%) and Paper 2 structured questions with data (70%).

### Set up the materials

Create one folder, physical or digital, per subject with the syllabus, past papers, mark schemes, class notes, teacher feedback from mocks and a formula or definition sheet. This takes an hour. It saves far more than an hour over six weeks, because every restart in the evening is a search for materials otherwise.

Start the **mistake log** on day one. For each wrong answer write the subject, the question, the type of mistake (recall, method, misread, command word, arithmetic, presentation) and a one-line fix. The log becomes the revision list for weeks 5 and 6.

## Weeks 2 and 3: How Should You Rebuild Weak Topics?

Rebuild weak topics by retrieval, not by rereading. Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar, but familiarity is not recall. Retrieval means closing the page and producing the answer: writing the process, solving the problem, drawing the diagram or explaining the idea aloud. It is slower and more uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the learning.

For each red topic, use the same four-step loop. First, spend 20 to 30 minutes relearning from the textbook or notes, aiming to understand, not to memorise. Second, close the book and write everything you remember on a blank page. Third, check and fill the gaps in a different colour. Fourth, do four to six exam-style questions on the topic and mark them against the scheme. Anything you still miss goes into the mistake log.

:::steps The retrieval loop for one weak topic
Relearn | 20 to 30 minutes, aim to understand
Recall | Close the book, write what you remember
Check | Fill gaps in a second colour
Test | Four to six exam questions, marked
Log | Record what still went wrong
:::

### Subject-specific rebuilding

In **mathematics**, rebuild by doing, not reading: a short explanation, then questions that escalate. Keep every line of working, because method marks depend on it, and practise both calculator and non-calculator versions where the papers differ. In **sciences**, rebuild with a mix of recall and application. Definitions and equations are tested directly, but so are unfamiliar contexts, so after relearning a topic like electricity or rates of reaction, answer a question that places it in a new setting. In **English**, rebuild skills by writing: a timed summary paragraph, a directed-writing piece for a named audience or a literature paragraph with one quotation analysed. Reading model answers is useful only after attempting your own.

Do not rebuild everything. The ambers are important but the reds come first, because a red is a topic that can cost a full question. By the end of week 3 aim to have every red turned amber and most ambers turned green.

## How Should Spaced Retrieval Work Across Six Subjects?

Spaced retrieval means returning to a topic after a gap, so the brain has to work to recall it, and then returning again after a longer gap. It works better than massed repetition because forgetting, followed by effortful recall, strengthens memory. In six weeks with six subjects, the practical version is simple: after you rebuild a topic, test yourself on it again two days later, then a week later, then in week 5 as part of a mixed paper.

Make it easy to run. Keep a single sheet with topics down the side and four small boxes for each: day of rebuild, plus two days, plus seven days, plus final check. Tick a box when a short recall test is passed, and if it fails, the topic returns to the rebuild loop. This is a sheet, not an app, and it takes a minute a day to maintain.

### Using small cards without drowning in them

Cards are good for facts that are tested directly: scientific definitions, equation rearrangements, key terms in economics, formulae that are not given on the paper, spelling lists for a language. They are poor for processes and extended answers. A student with 400 cards in week 4 has created a second job. Keep the deck small, cut cards you know and write new ones from the mistake log. The mistake log is the best source because every card in it is a proven weakness.

### A realistic mid-week rhythm

On a school night with two to three hours of revision, three blocks of 45 minutes with 10-minute breaks work for most students: one rebuild block, one retrieval block on older topics and one practice block with exam questions. Rotate subjects so the same one is not first every night. If a student is tired, shortening the practice block is better than skipping the retrieval block, because retrieval is what prevents forgetting.

## Week 4: How Do You Move from Knowing to Performing?

Week 4 shifts from rebuilding topics to answering whole questions under mild time pressure. Begin with timed sections, not full papers. A section is a block of questions worth about a third of a paper, done in the time that mark allocation implies, with the calculator rule the paper uses. Mark it at once with the official scheme, and be hard on yourself: award marks only where the scheme says a mark exists.

This is also the week to learn the language of the paper. Command words, such as state, describe, explain, calculate, compare, evaluate and justify, decide the depth an answer needs. A student who "explains" when asked to "state" wastes time, and one who "states" when asked to "explain" loses marks. Build a short list for each subject and attach each word to the number of marks it usually carries. The [command-term logic in IB](/blog/ib-command-terms-explained-with-real-examples/) is a different programme, but the habit of reading the verb first transfers directly.

### Mix subjects within a day, topics within a subject

Mixed practice, where questions from different topics are shuffled, trains the skill the exam actually tests: working out what a question wants. After a student has rebuilt a few topics, a weekly mixed set of 20 questions in maths or 30 in a science is more valuable than another block on a single topic.

:::bars Where a typical revision hour should go by week (illustrative guide)
Week 1 audit and set-up | 100 | mostly diagnosis
Weeks 2-3 rebuild by retrieval | 80 | topics and short questions
Week 4 mixed practice | 60 | sections and mixed sets
Weeks 5-6 timed papers and repair | 40 | marked papers, not new notes
A guide to how the balance shifts, not a research finding. The share of new learning falls as the weeks pass.
:::

## Week 5: How Do You Use Full Past Papers Properly?

Full past papers belong in week 5 because by now there is enough secure knowledge for a whole paper to be informative. Use papers from your own board and syllabus code, the most recent that you have not seen, and sit them as in the exam: correct calculator, no notes, a quiet room, the real time limit. Do one paper per major subject this week at least, and in the subjects with more than one paper, alternate which paper you sit.

The paper is only half the work. The other half is marking and correcting, which should take at least as long as the paper itself. Mark against the scheme line by line. For every lost mark, write down which kind of loss it was: knowledge, method, misread question, careless slip, time or presentation. Count each type. The pattern across two or three papers tells you more about next steps than any single score.

:::stats What to record after each timed paper
Marks | Raw score and the paper section
Time | Where the clock was lost
Cause | Knowledge, method, misread, slip, presentation
Fix | One action for each cause
:::

### Common patterns and what they mean

If most losses are **knowledge**, return to the rebuild loop for those topics. If they are **misreads and command words**, practise underlining the verb and the units before starting. If they are **method or working**, rewrite solutions in full and compare them with the scheme. If they are **time**, practise sections with a strict clock and learn to leave a question and return. If they are **slips**, add a check routine for the last five minutes: units, signs, rounding, blank spaces.

Papers in the sciences deserve special care for the practical component. Whether a student sits Paper 5 (practical test) or Paper 6 (alternative to practical), the skills tested are planning, observations, tables, graphs and evaluation. A student can lose marks here for avoidable reasons: unlabelled axes, inconsistent decimal places, missing units. Rehearse one practical-style question per science during this week.

## Week 6: What Should the Final Week Look Like?

The final week should be lighter, sharper and more rehearsal than learning. By now, the main work is done, and the goal is to arrive at each paper rested, with the right tools and clear heads. Spend the first two days on a last pass through the mistake log, the one-page summaries and any red topic that is still red. Do not start new large topics unless the paper is days away and the topic is a high-mark area.

Stage the final week around the actual exam timetable. The day before a paper, spend an hour on a light recall of that subject: formulae, definitions, key diagrams, command words and your most frequent mistakes from the log. Then stop. A student who studies until midnight before a Paper 2 arrives with a tired brain.

### The night before and the morning of

Pack the bag the evening before: admission or candidate documents the school requires, pens, pencils, ruler, the permitted calculator with fresh batteries, a clear water bottle if allowed, a watch if the hall requires it. Eat a proper meal, sleep at a normal hour and avoid last-minute cramming from friends' notes. On the morning, read through the mistake log for that subject, and nothing else.

### In the hall

Read the whole paper before writing. Mark the questions you can do at once, bank those marks, then return to the harder ones. Show working in mathematics, always. In sciences, match the length of the answer to the marks. In English, plan a short outline before writing a composition. Keep a 5-minute reserve for checking, and if a question has defeated you, write what you do know, because partial credit is real.

## How Do You Fit Six Subjects Into One Timetable?

Use a weekly grid, not a daily to-do list. A grid shows at a glance whether any subject has been neglected. The structure below works for a student with five or six subjects and typical school hours. Adjust it for coaching, sports and family commitments, and keep at least one half-day free each week.

| Day | Block 1 | Block 2 | Block 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths rebuild | Science A retrieval | English skill |
| Tuesday | Science B rebuild | Maths practice | Option subject |
| Wednesday | Science C rebuild | English writing | Maths retrieval |
| Thursday | Option subject rebuild | Science A practice | Mistake log |
| Friday | Maths mixed set | Science B retrieval | Light reading |
| Saturday | Timed section or paper | Marking and corrections | Free afternoon |
| Sunday | Planning and weak topics | Light retrieval | Rest |

A good timetable has three features. It rotates subjects so none gets the best hours every day. It includes marking time explicitly, because students undercount it. And it contains slack. A plan with no slack fails the first time a day is lost, and then the whole structure feels broken. Build in a catch-up block on Saturday or Sunday.

## What If Two Papers Fall Close Together?

Exam timetables often cluster papers, so a student may have two heavy papers on consecutive days. Treat the exam timetable as the real plan and work backwards. In weeks 5 and 6, give each paper a "peak" of one dedicated day or two half-days before it, and give the subject sitting after a lighter touch of retrieval only on the day immediately before.

For clustered exams, prioritise the subject with the weakest secure base, because a day of well-aimed work gives the most return there. Do not sacrifice sleep to squeeze in one more hour. A well-rested brain recalls better than a tired one, and the evidence from any teacher's marking experience is that careless errors rise sharply with fatigue.

If the student sits Core and Extended tiers in different subjects or has options that differ from friends, make a personal exam grid, not a copy of someone else's. Check the paper numbers against your own entry, because a student revising for the wrong paper is a loss that no timetable can repair.

## How Much Should You Revise Each Subject?

Weight each subject by two things: how much room there is to gain and how large the paper weight is. A subject where you are at the top of the scale gains little from extra hours; one where you are near a boundary gains a lot. A rough distribution for a student with six subjects and varied strength might give the weakest two subjects roughly a third more time each than the strongest two, with the middle two in between. This is a tilt, not a rule.

It helps to use targets. For each subject write the grade you are realistic about aiming for, the paper where most marks are lost, and the one habit most likely to raise it. Maths often needs more hours because of the number of topics and the cumulative nature of the skills. Sciences often need a balance of recall and application. English needs frequent short writing, not long reading.

No plan can guarantee a grade. What a plan does is turn hours into marks more efficiently than a random approach. If a subject remains stubborn after two weeks of honest work, that is a signal to get a second opinion: a teacher, a tutor or a school mock paper marked by someone else. ## When Does a Tutor Help During These Six Weeks?

A tutor helps in six weeks when the problem is diagnosis or a specific weakness that a student cannot fix alone, and helps less when the problem is volume. If a student is in good order but short of time, a tutor will not add hours. If a student has a red topic they do not understand, a one-to-one explanation in the first fortnight can save a week of struggle.

Practical uses in a six-week window: a diagnostic review of mock papers in week 1, targeted sessions on the two weakest topics in weeks 2 and 3, marking and feedback on a timed paper in week 5, and a short review of presentation and command words in week 6. Fewer, better-aimed sessions work better than daily lessons that duplicate school.

## What Mistakes Ruin a Six-Week Plan?

A few mistakes appear every season. Starting with full past papers in week 1 produces low scores and low morale without a map for improvement. Treating revision as reading is the next one: a student with beautiful colour-coded notes and no retrieval has revised their handwriting. Neglecting a "boring" subject because it is not a favourite is common and costly, because a weak subject usually has the largest gain available.

Others include copying a topper's timetable without regard to your own subjects, skipping marking because it is tedious, leaving the practical papers until the last day, forgetting to rest and ignoring the school's own mock feedback, which is free diagnostic information. A subtler one is changing the plan every week. A plan that is adjusted by the log is flexible; a plan that is rebuilt every Sunday is procrastination dressed as planning.

### What to do when a week goes wrong

Illness, a family event or a bad test result will interrupt the plan. Do not try to make up every missed hour. Shift the plan by a few days, cut a block of lower-priority topics, and protect week 5 and week 6 because they carry the highest value per hour. Tell a parent or teacher what has happened, because plans fail quietly when only one person knows.

## How Can Parents Support Without Nagging?

Parents help most with logistics and mood. Know the exam timetable and display it. Keep the revision space quiet and the phone out of the room during blocks. Offer to quiz a student from a card deck or a mistake log for ten minutes, which is more useful than asking "how many hours did you do?". Make sure meals and sleep are in order, and say clearly that a bad paper is information, not a verdict.

Avoid comparing a child's plan with a friend's, avoid adding extra classes in the final fortnight and avoid announcing predicted grades as expectations. If a student seems stuck, ask the open question, "What is the next thing you need to fix?", and let them answer. Where outside help is needed, the family can talk to the subject teacher first, then consider a tutor for the specific gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a six-week IGCSE revision plan enough if I have not revised at all?

It is enough to make a real difference if the syllabus has been taught, but it will be tight if you are starting from nothing. Spend week 1 on triage, accept that some topics will stay amber, and put your hours into high-mark areas and the weakest subjects. If whole units were never taught, ask your school for the material immediately and treat that as a priority.

### How many hours a day should I revise for IGCSE?

Most students do well with two to three hours on school days and three to five on weekends, rising a little in weeks 4 and 5 and falling in week 6. Quality matters more than quantity: three focused blocks of 45 minutes with retrieval beat five hours of rereading. Reduce the load before cutting sleep, and keep at least one half-day free each week.

### When should I start doing full past papers?

Start with timed sections in week 4 and sit full papers in week 5, once enough of the syllabus is secure for a paper to be informative. Starting too early produces low scores that do not point anywhere. When you do sit papers, use your own board and syllabus code, mark them with the official scheme and log every lost mark by cause.

### Should I revise one subject at a time or all together?

Revise several subjects in a week, rotating them, rather than finishing one subject before starting the next. Rotation spaces your retrieval and prevents a subject from being forgotten. Within a day, give each subject a block of about 45 minutes and rotate which one comes first. Close to an exam, shift weight towards the next paper on the timetable.

### What is the best way to revise for IGCSE sciences?

Combine recall and application. Learn definitions, equations and processes through retrieval, then answer exam questions that put them in unfamiliar contexts. Rehearse the practical skills, such as tables, graphs and evaluation, whichever practical paper your school uses. Mark each question with the scheme and note the wording that earned marks, because science schemes reward specific terms.

### How do I revise for English when there is no content to learn?

Revise skills by writing under time. Practise a summary, a directed-writing piece for a named audience and a composition plan or literature paragraph every week, then compare your work against the mark scheme or a model and note what the examiner rewarded. Read widely for vocabulary, but the improvement comes from writing, receiving feedback and rewriting.

### What if I am behind my plan after two weeks?

Do not try to catch up on every missed hour. Re-run a short audit, protect the highest-value topics and subjects and shorten lower-priority blocks. Keep weeks 5 and 6 intact because they carry the highest value per hour. If you are behind because the plan was unrealistic, reduce the weekly hours and extend the high-priority work, not the total.

### Do I need a tutor for the last six weeks?

Not necessarily. If you can diagnose your own errors, mark papers honestly and keep a routine, you may not. A tutor adds value when there is a red topic you cannot repair alone, when marking a timed paper needs a second pair of eyes or when motivation is the issue. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and the student still has to do the practice.

### How should I use the mock exam results?

Treat them as the first version of your audit. List every lost mark by topic and cause, and compare with the colours from week 1. The mock shows how you perform under real conditions, which self-study cannot. Share the paper with a teacher if the school allows, and ask which two issues would raise the mark most.

### What should I do the day before an exam?

Do a light, targeted review of that subject for an hour or less: formulae, definitions, key diagrams, command words and your most frequent mistakes. Pack your bag, eat properly and sleep at a normal time. Avoid new topics and avoid comparing notes with friends, which usually raises anxiety without adding marks.
