The IGCSE maths mistakes that cost the most marks are rarely exotic. They are the same ten slips, repeated across thousands of scripts: a sign dropped inside a bracket, a fraction added the wrong way, a percentage reversed in the wrong direction, a calculator left in the wrong mode, an answer rounded halfway through. A student who knows the syllabus and still lands a grade below expectation is almost always losing marks in these places, not in the hard questions at the end of the paper.

This guide is for students taking Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 (Core or Extended), Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1, Foundation or Higher), and for parents who want to know what to look for when a marked paper comes home. Each of the ten mistakes below has the wrong working, the right working and a habit that prevents the error from returning. The goal is not to scare you with a list. It is to turn "careless mistakes" into named, fixable patterns, because a mistake with a name can be trained out, and "careless" cannot.

If you suspect the problem is deeper than slips (a gap in algebra from Grade 8, for example), the root-cause view is in [why students struggle in IGCSE Mathematics](/blog/igcse-mathematics-tutoring-guide/). This post stays on the ten errors that show up on the page.

## Why Do Students Lose Marks in IGCSE Maths When They Know the Topic?

Students lose marks in IGCSE maths because examination mathematics rewards accurate, visible, complete working, and knowing a method is only the first step. A student can understand quadratics and still lose marks by expanding a bracket wrongly, by rounding too early, or by answering a different question from the one asked. The topic knowledge is there. The execution on the page is not.

There are three families of loss. **Accuracy losses** come from sign errors, arithmetic slips and calculator entry. **Communication losses** come from missing working, missing units and answers not given in the requested form. **Reading losses** come from answering part of a question, ignoring a command word such as "show that" or "hence", or missing a condition buried in a word problem.

Method marks are the reason this matters. A multi-mark question awards marks for the steps, and those marks are available even when the final answer is wrong, but only if the steps are visible. A student who writes only the final number and gets it wrong scores zero. A student who writes the method and slips on the arithmetic often keeps most of the marks.

## Mistake 1: Losing a Sign When Expanding Brackets

The most common single slip in IGCSE maths is a sign that disappears while a bracket is opened. It looks small and it breaks everything that follows.

**The wrong version.** Simplify 5 − 2(x − 4).

A student writes 5 − 2x − 8 = −3 − 2x. The minus outside the bracket was applied to the first term only.

**The right version.** The −2 multiplies both terms inside: 5 − 2x + 8 = 13 − 2x.

The squared bracket has its own trap. (x − 3)² is not x² − 9. It is (x − 3)(x − 3) = x² − 3x − 3x + 9 = x² − 6x + 9. Students who write x² − 9 have used the difference of two squares pattern on something that is not one.

**The habit.** Draw an arrow from the term outside the bracket to every term inside it, and say the signs aloud in your head: "minus two times minus four is plus eight". For squared brackets, always write the bracket twice before expanding. It costs five seconds and removes the whole category.

## Mistake 2: Treating Fractions as If You Could Add Across

Fractions go wrong in two predictable ways: adding numerators and denominators separately, and cancelling terms that are not factors.

**The wrong version, numeric.** 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7.

**The right version.** Use a common denominator: 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12.

**The wrong version, algebraic.** Simplify (x² + 3x)/x. A student cancels the x with the x² only and writes x² + 3, or cancels "x" across the line from the wrong terms.

**The right version.** Factorise first: x(x + 3)/x = x + 3. In the same way, (2x + 6)/2 is not 2x + 3. Factorise the numerator as 2(x + 3), then cancel the 2 to get x + 3.

The rule that rescues you: you may only cancel factors, never terms. If there is a plus or minus sign in the numerator, put it in brackets as a product before you cancel anything.

**The habit.** Before you cancel, ask "is this a factor or a term?" If you cannot answer, factorise. On a non-calculator paper, write the common denominator line out in full even for easy fractions, because that line is where the method mark lives.

## Mistake 3: Reversing a Percentage in the Wrong Direction

Percentage increase and decrease are well practised. Reverse percentages are where the marks go.

**The question.** After a 15% increase, a jacket costs 230. Find the original price.

**The wrong version.** 15% of 230 = 34.5, so the original is 230 − 34.5 = 195.5.

**The right version.** The 230 is 115% of the original, so original = 230 ÷ 1.15 = 200.

The error comes from taking the percentage of the wrong amount. The 15% was taken from the original price, not from the new one.

Compound change has its own version. A value increases by 5% a year for 3 years. The wrong answer multiplies by 1.15 (adding 5% three times). The right answer multiplies by 1.05³ = 1.157625, because each year's 5% applies to a larger amount.

**The habit.** Always convert to a multiplier first. Write "increase 15% means ×1.15" and "decrease 15% means ×0.85". Then ask "which amount is 100%?" before you divide or multiply. If the question gives you the final amount and asks for the start, you divide.

## Mistake 4: Getting Indices and Standard Form Wrong

Index laws are simple to state and easy to misapply under pressure, and the errors survive for years because the answers look plausible.

**The wrong version.** Simplify (3x²)³. A student writes 3x⁶ (cubing only the power part) or 9x⁵ (multiplying and adding things that should not be combined).

**The right version.** The cube applies to everything inside: 3³ × (x²)³ = 27x⁶.

Negative indices are another source of loss. 2⁻³ is not −8. It is 1/2³ = 1/8. A negative index tells you to take the reciprocal, not to make the number negative.

Standard form errors are mostly about the first number. 0.00047 is 4.7 × 10⁻⁴. A student who writes 47 × 10⁻⁵ has the right value but not standard form, so the mark for "standard form" is lost. Multiplying standard form numbers: (4.5 × 10³) × (2 × 10⁴) = 9 × 10⁷, and if the product of the front numbers had come to 12, you would convert to 1.2 × 10⁸.

**The habit.** Say the law in words as you apply it: "power of a power, multiply the powers" and "negative power, reciprocal". Check every standard form answer against the rule that the front number is at least 1 and less than 10.

## Mistake 5: Letting the Calculator Think for You

On the calculator papers, the machine is a help and a hazard. Three entry errors cost more marks than any hard topic.

**Bracket entry.** −3² on many calculators gives −9, because the square applies before the minus. If the question needs (−3)², you must enter the brackets. For a quadratic evaluated at a negative value, the missing bracket produces a wrong value that looks respectable.

**Fraction entry.** Typing 12 ÷ 2 × 3 gives 18, because the calculator works left to right. If you meant 12 divided by (2 × 3), the answer is 2. Use brackets, or use the fraction key if your calculator has one.

**Angle mode.** sin 30 should be 0.5. If the calculator is in radians, you get −0.988, and every trigonometry answer for the paper is wrong. Check the mode before you start the paper, and again after any reset.

A fourth failure is not a keying error but a thinking one: accepting an answer that cannot be right. If a question asks for 19% of 200 and the calculator shows 380, a student who has estimated 40 knows immediately that something went wrong.

**The habit.** Estimate first, then calculate, then compare. And write down the calculation you typed in, in brackets, so that a slip is visible to the marker and to you.

## Mistake 6: Rounding Too Early or to the Wrong Accuracy

Rounding in the middle of a calculation moves the final answer, and the accuracy marks are lost even though the method is right.

**The wrong version.** Calculate 17 ÷ 6 × 300. A student rounds 17 ÷ 6 to 2.83, then multiplies: 2.83 × 300 = 849.

**The right version.** Keep the full value: 17 ÷ 6 × 300 = 850 exactly.

The gap looks small, but accuracy marks are awarded for answers within the stated tolerance, and early rounding in a multi-step problem can push you out of it.

There are two ways to avoid it: store the unrounded value in the calculator memory or answer key, or keep at least two more significant figures than the final answer needs, and round only at the end.

The accuracy of the final answer matters too. The instructions printed on the front of the paper tell you the default accuracy for answers that are not exact (for many papers, three significant figures, with a different rule for angles in degrees). Read that instruction at the start of the paper, not at the end. If the question says "give your answer in terms of π" or "exact value", do not round at all: a circle with radius 3 has area 9π, not 28.3.

**The habit.** Write the unrounded value on the line, and round only when you write the final answer line. If you round, say what you rounded to: "= 849.6 = 850 (3 s.f.)".

## Mistake 7: Using the Wrong Trigonometry or Bearing Rule

Trigonometry questions go wrong at the very first step: choosing which ratio to use, or applying Pythagoras to a triangle that has no right angle.

**The wrong version.** A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 13 and an angle of 35°. To find the side opposite the angle, a student uses cosine: 13 cos 35° = 10.65.

**The right version.** Opposite and hypotenuse give sine: 13 sin 35° = 7.46 (to 3 s.f.).

**The habit for ratios.** Label the three sides before choosing: hypotenuse (opposite the right angle), opposite (across from the angle you are using), adjacent (the remaining side). Then write "SOH CAH TOA" at the top of the page and circle the two sides involved. If the triangle has no right angle, the sine rule or cosine rule is needed, and Pythagoras is not allowed.

Bearings carry their own set of errors. A bearing is measured clockwise from north and written with three figures. If the bearing of B from A is 065°, the bearing of A from B is 065° + 180° = 245°. A student who writes 115° has subtracted instead of adding the back-bearing, or has measured from the wrong north line.

**The habit for bearings.** Draw a north line at the point you are travelling from, every time, and mark the angle clockwise from it. Do not reuse the north line from the other point.

## Mistake 8: Mishandling Probability Trees

Probability marks go where the problem has a hidden condition: with or without replacement, and whether you need "and" (multiply) or "or" (add).

**The question.** A bag contains 4 red and 6 blue counters. Two are taken at random without replacement. Find the probability that both are red.

**The wrong version.** 4/10 × 4/10 = 16/100 = 4/25. The student has replaced the first counter without being told to.

**The right version.** After taking one red counter, there are 3 red among 9 counters: 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15.

A second common loss: adding where the question needs multiplying. "Red then blue" on a tree is a branch product. "Red then blue, or blue then red" is two products added together.

**The habit.** Always write the number of items left on the second branch before you fill in the fractions, and read the question for the words "without replacement". On a tree diagram, check that the probabilities on each set of branches sum to 1. If one set sums to 0.9 or 1.1, the tree is wrong before you do any arithmetic.

## Mistake 9: Ignoring the Command Word

Command words tell you what the examiner will pay for. Ignoring them is the reading error that costs the most marks per question, because the working does not match what was asked.

**"Show that."** The answer is given, so the marks are for the method. For "Show that x = 2 is a solution of 2x² − 3x − 2 = 0", write the substitution in full: 2(2)² − 3(2) − 2 = 8 − 6 − 2 = 0. Writing "0" or "yes" scores nothing.

**"Hence."** Use the previous part. A student who solves from scratch by a different method may be marked as not following the instruction, and loses the marks for the link.

**"Exact."** Leave surds, fractions and π unevaluated. A decimal approximation loses the accuracy mark.

**"Estimate."** Round to one significant figure first, then calculate. Using the calculator for a full-precision answer fails the instruction.

**"Explain" or "Give a reason."** A sentence is needed, not a number. In geometry, this usually means quoting a named angle fact: "angles in a triangle sum to 180°", "angles on a straight line sum to 180°".

**The habit.** Underline the command word and the quantity asked for before you start. At the end, return to the question and check that your last line answers it, in the required form, with units.

## Mistake 10: Leaving Working Out and Leaving Gaps

The last of the ten IGCSE maths mistakes is about the page, not the topic. Skipped working, blank questions and misjudged time are responsible for more lost marks than any single topic.

**The skipped-working version.** For a 3-mark question, a student writes the answer 7.2 and moves on. If it is right, full marks. If it is wrong, nothing, even if the first two lines in the head were correct.

**The right version.** Write the setup line, the substitution and the answer. Each of these is a point on the mark scheme, and each is available even when a later step slips.

**Blank space.** A student leaves a question blank because the first step is unclear. A mark for a formula, a labelled diagram or a first sensible step is often available, and a blank scores zero with certainty.

**Time.** Divide the minutes on the front of the paper by the number of marks to get a marks-to-minutes rate, and use it as a guide. A question worth 2 marks that has taken you six minutes is a signal to move on and return later.

On the non-calculator papers (Paper 1 for Core and Paper 2 for Extended in Cambridge 0580), written working carries even more weight, because there is no machine to hide behind. Fraction work, surds, exact trigonometric values and algebraic manipulation must be visible.

**The habit.** On every paper, do a first pass for the questions you can do, a second pass for the ones you started, and a final check for blanks. Reserve the last few minutes for a quick check of signs, units and the form of the answer.

## How Do You Build an Error Log That Works?

Naming ten IGCSE maths mistakes is the first step. Training them out needs a record of which ones are yours, because most students have two or three that account for most of the loss.

A useful error log has four columns: the question, what you did, the cause and the fix. The cause is the part students skip. "Careless" is not a cause. "Did not write the bracket twice" is a cause. "Rushed the second step" is a symptom. "Did not read the word 'exact'" is a cause.

| Question | What I did | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expand 5 − 2(x − 4) | Wrote 5 − 2x − 8 | Applied minus to first term only | Arrow to each term |
| Reverse percentage, 15% | Took 15% off the new price | Did not ask what is 100% | Write the multiplier first |
| Probability, two counters | Used 4/10 twice | Missed "without replacement" | Underline the condition |
| Area of circle, exact | Gave 28.3 | Missed the word "exact" | Underline command words |

Review the log weekly. An entry that appears three times is a habit, not an accident, and it needs five or six targeted questions of its own, not more general practice. The approach matches how a strong tutor works: look at a script, name the pattern, build a small drill that targets it, then check it with a fresh question two weeks later.

:::steps The error-log loop
Mark | Use the mark scheme, not a guess
Name | Write the cause, not "careless"
Drill | Five targeted questions on the pattern
Recheck | A fresh question two weeks later
:::

## Mistakes in How You Revise, Not Just How You Calculate

Some of the most expensive IGCSE maths mistakes happen before the exam. Three recur.

**Revising by topic only.** Thirty questions on one topic teach the method. They do not teach you to recognise which method a mixed question needs, which is what the paper tests. Include mixed sets from the first month of revision.

**Marking by feel.** A student who looks at the mark scheme answer, thinks "I nearly had it" and gives themselves the marks is not learning where marks go. Mark strictly, and award only what the scheme awards. The use of past papers, when to begin and how many to do, is covered in [why past papers are key to IGCSE maths success](/blog/why-past-papers-are-key-to-igcse-maths-success/).

**Entering the wrong tier.** In Cambridge 0580, Core candidates reach a maximum of grade C, and Extended candidates can reach A*. In Edexcel 4MA1, the choice is between Foundation and Higher. A student who sits Core and is capable of Extended has capped their result before the exam begins. A student entered for Extended who is not secure on Grade 8 algebra loses marks across the paper. Tier decisions are made by the school, so raise the question early. The parent-facing explanation of the two syllabuses, including the Additional Mathematics 0606 decision, is in [the IGCSE Maths 0580 and 0606 parent guide](/blog/igcse-maths-parent-guide-0580-0606/).

For the top-grade perspective, [how to score an A* in IGCSE Maths 0580](/blog/how-to-score-a-star-in-igcse-maths-0580/) looks at the topics and accuracy habits that matter most at the upper end, and the [12-week IGCSE Maths 0580 study plan](/blog/3-month-study-plan-for-igcse-maths-0580/) shows how to schedule the drills above across a term.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common mistake in IGCSE maths?

The most common mistake is a sign error, usually when a negative number multiplies a bracket or when a negative value is substituted into a formula. It is small, easy to make under pressure and hard to see when you read your own working. The fix is mechanical: draw an arrow from the outside term to each term inside the bracket and write the sign of each product before simplifying.

### How do I stop making careless mistakes in IGCSE maths?

Stop treating them as careless and name each one. A mistake with a cause, such as "did not bracket the negative on the calculator", can be fixed with a habit. Keep an error log, review it weekly and drill any pattern that appears three times. Slow, visible working on the first pass, and a short check at the end of each paper, remove most slips.

### Do I lose marks for not showing working in IGCSE maths?

Yes, whenever the answer is wrong or the question says "show that". Method marks are awarded for visible steps, and a wrong answer with no working scores nothing. A correct answer without working may still score full marks on some questions, but you should not rely on it. On non-calculator papers, working matters even more, because the method is the main evidence.

### Should I round at every step in IGCSE maths?

No. Keep the full value or at least two extra significant figures through the calculation, and round only on the final answer line. Early rounding shifts the result and can push it outside the accepted range. If the question asks for an exact value, do not round at all, and leave answers in terms of surds, fractions or π.

### Why do I get different answers on my calculator?

Usually because of bracket entry, angle mode or the order of operations. Typing 12 ÷ 2 × 3 gives 18, not 2, and −3² gives −9, not 9. Trigonometry answers go wrong if the calculator is in radians when degrees are needed. Check the mode at the start of the paper and estimate each answer before you accept it.

### Is it better to leave a question blank or guess?

Attempt it. A blank scores zero with certainty, while a formula, a labelled diagram or a first sensible step can earn a method mark. On multi-part questions, a later part may be worth marks even if an earlier part defeated you, and follow-through marks can reward correct method using your earlier answer. Leave a question temporarily, return to it, and fill in whatever you can.

### How many past papers should I do to fix these mistakes?

Quality of marking matters more than quantity. A student who completes a handful of papers, marks them strictly against the mark scheme and logs each error will improve more than a student who attempts many and checks only the final answers. Start with topic questions, move to mixed sets, then timed papers. The exact sequence is set out in the past-papers guide linked above.

### Can I fix these mistakes without a tutor?

Yes, if you can mark honestly, keep an error log and drill the patterns. Many students do. A tutor helps most when the pattern is hard to see: when marks are lost for several different reasons, when confidence is low, or when the time before the exam series is short. Ask whether you can name your top two errors without looking. If you cannot, outside diagnosis is worth considering.

### Are the mistakes the same for Cambridge 0580 and Edexcel 4MA1?

Largely, yes. Both reward visible method and accurate answers, and both punish the same sign, fraction, percentage and calculator errors. The differences are structural: Cambridge 0580 has Core and Extended tiers with specific non-calculator and calculator papers, while Edexcel 4MA1 has Foundation and Higher tiers with calculator papers. Check your syllabus and the front of the paper for the exact rules on accuracy and working.

### Is my child making too many mistakes, or is it normal?

Some slips are normal, particularly in the first year, and a few lost marks per paper is common even for strong students. The warning signs are repeated errors in the same category, a drop in marks between topic tests and mixed papers, and a student who says "I knew how to do it". Those point to habits or recall, and a short diagnostic can separate the two.

### What should we do if the mistakes are not improving after a month?

Check whether the log is being reviewed and whether the drills target the named causes. If both are in place, the cause may be a gap that practice cannot fix, such as weak Grade 8 algebra, or the student may be in the wrong tier. At that point a diagnostic session with an experienced tutor is a sensible step, subject to tutor availability.
