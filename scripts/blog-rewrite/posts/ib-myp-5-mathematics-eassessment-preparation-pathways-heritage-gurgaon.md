## What Is MYP eAssessment in Mathematics?

MYP eAssessment is the optional, externally assessed part of the International Baccalaureate (IB) Middle Years Programme (MYP) at the end of MYP 5, made up of on-screen examinations and ePortfolios. The MYP covers five years (MYP 1 to 5, roughly ages 11 to 16) across eight subject groups, and every subject is assessed against four criteria (A to D), each scored 0 to 8.

For Mathematics the criteria are:

- **A, Knowing and understanding**
- **B, Investigating patterns**
- **C, Communicating**
- **D, Applying mathematics in real-life contexts**

The criterion total out of 32 converts to a final 1 to 7 grade through IB grade boundaries.

:::stats MYP Mathematics at a glance
4 | Criteria, A to D
0-8 | Score per criterion
32 | Maximum criterion total
1-7 | Final MYP grade
:::

What families should take from this is simple. The eAssessment is one way the IB gathers evidence of a student's work against those same criteria, and it is delivered on a screen. The mathematics a student needs does not change. The way they must show it does.

## Does My Child's School Offer It, and What Should I Ask?

Ask the school directly, because the answer decides how much of this guide applies. MYP eAssessment is optional and each school decides whether to offer it. Some schools enter all MYP 5 students, some enter a group and some do not offer it at all.

Questions worth putting to the MYP coordinator or the maths teacher:

1. Is our school registered for MYP eAssessment this year, and is mathematics included?
2. Which parts are on-screen examinations, and which are ePortfolio work?
3. When are the sessions, and does the school run a practice session on the same platform?
4. What device will the student use: a school machine, or their own?
5. Which tools are allowed on screen, and is paper allowed for working?
6. How will results be reported, and how do they relate to the criterion grades the school already awards?
7. What does the school do to prepare students, and where could outside help usefully fit?

Write the answers down. Dates, devices and tool rules change between sessions and from school to school, and a guess in this area costs more than it saves. If the school does not offer the eAssessment, the preparation below still helps, because the skills (reading carefully, showing reasoning, working under time) are the same ones that school-based assessment rewards.

:::bars Criterion marks that make up the 32 total
A Knowing and understanding | 25 | 8 marks
B Investigating patterns | 25 | 8 marks
C Communicating | 25 | 8 marks
D Real-life contexts | 25 | 8 marks
Each criterion carries equal weight in the 32-mark total, so a weak criterion cannot be ignored.

## What Is Different About Doing Mathematics on a Screen?

The difference is not the mathematics. It is everything around it: how a question is displayed, how an answer is entered, how a diagram is read and how working is recorded. Students who are strong on paper often lose marks for reasons they never notice in class.

| Paper habit | Screen version | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Scribbling working beside the question | Working has to go on rough paper or into an answer box | Steps are lost, and the final answer cannot be checked |
| Drawing a graph by hand | Reading a graph shown on screen, or using the tool provided | Misreading scales; unfamiliar tool slows the student |
| Writing a fraction naturally | Entering a fraction or power with the keyboard or editor | Wrong format; a correct value entered incorrectly |
| Flipping back to a previous page | Scrolling or navigating between screens | Time lost; the student forgets the earlier result |
| Underlining the key words | Cannot mark the screen | Command words and units are missed |

Each of these is trainable, and none needs a special course. It needs deliberate practice on a screen, in the same way a student practises writing with a pen before a handwritten exam. Ask the school for any sample or practice material the IB or the school provides, and practise on it so the interface is familiar before the day.

## How Do the Four Criteria Show Up in an On-Screen Setting?

All four criteria matter, but they show up differently on a screen, and the preparation should target the form each takes. The summary below is a guide to the skills involved, not a statement of which criteria any given paper samples. Check the current IB guidance and your school for that.

| Criterion | The skill | What to practise on screen |
|---|---|---|
| A Knowing and understanding | Select and apply methods accurately | Accurate entry of results; checking an answer before submitting |
| B Investigating patterns | Notice, test and generalise | Reading tables and sequences shown on screen; writing a general rule in a box |
| C Communicating | Clear reasoning and correct notation | Typing mathematical expressions clearly; explaining a step in a sentence |
| D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts | Model a situation, solve, interpret | Reading a long context carefully; stating what the result means |

### Criterion A on screen

Knowing and understanding rewards accurate use of the right method. On a screen the risk is entry error. A correct calculation typed in the wrong form, or an answer rounded at the wrong stage, loses marks that the student would have kept on paper where the working is visible. The fix is a habit: write the working on paper, check the result against an estimate, then type it.

### Criterion B on screen

Pattern investigation rewards exploring before concluding. A student shown a table or a growing diagram should record observations, state a conjecture, test it on another case and then generalise. For a deeper treatment of that habit, see [IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/). On screen, the challenge is often the final step: expressing a general rule in a form the platform accepts. Practise writing rules in words and in symbols, so the student can adapt.

### Criterion C on screen

Communication is the criterion most affected by the screen. Typing mathematics is slower than writing it, so students either skip steps or run out of time. A realistic target is a clear sequence of short statements: define the variable, state the method, show the key line, give the answer with units. Practise this routine until it is automatic.

### Criterion D on screen

Real-life tasks are long, and a long context on a screen is easy to skim. Train the student to read the whole situation once, list what is given and what is asked, and only then start. The mathematical work is the same as on paper; the reading discipline is what changes. For modelling routines and how a tutor gradually withdraws support, see [MYP maths problem solving](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-ajay-vatsyayan-sir-gurgaon/).

## What Are the Most Common On-Screen Mistakes?

They are mostly small, mostly avoidable and mostly invisible to the student until someone watches them work. A tutor or parent who sits with the student during a screen-based practice will see most of them in one session.

- **Skipping the working because "it is only an answer box".** If the platform gives marks for method, the box is where it goes. If it does not, the working on paper is what lets the student check.
- **Entering an answer in the wrong form.** A decimal where a fraction was asked, a missing unit, a rounded value that should have been exact.
- **Misreading a displayed graph or table.** Scales that do not start at zero, axes with unusual intervals, unit labels ignored.
- **Spending too long on one item.** On paper a student sees the whole page and moves on; on a screen the next question is hidden, so the sense of pacing is lost.
- **Not reviewing before submission.** Students who would reread a paper do not reread a screen.
- **Treating the tool as the method.** Using whatever tool is available without checking whether the result is sensible.

A useful drill is a five-minute "error audit": after any screen-based practice, the student lists each mark they think they lost and which of the six causes above applies. After a few weeks, the list shortens.

## A 12-Week Plan for MYP 5 Mathematics eAssessment Preparation

A 12-week plan for MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation is long enough to build habits and short enough to hold a student's attention. Start it so that it finishes comfortably before the school's session date, with a few spare weeks, and adjust it to the school calendar. The plan below assumes a student who already attends school and has a tutor or parent supporting practice for a few hours a week.

:::timeline A 12-week preparation arc
Weeks 1-2 | Diagnosis: school work, a screen task, error audit
Weeks 3-5 | Repair gaps in A and C; introduce screen entry habits
Weeks 6-8 | Investigations (B) and real-life tasks (D) on screen
Weeks 9-10 | Timed sections on screen, with review
Weeks 11-12 | Full practice, rest, logistics check
:::

| Weeks | Focus | A typical weekly pattern |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Diagnosis | Review the latest school assessments by criterion; one screen-based task under mild time pressure; sort mistakes by cause |
| 3 to 5 | Foundations | Targeted work on the weakest topics; practise writing short, clear steps; screen entry practice on easy items |
| 6 to 8 | Investigation and application | One pattern task and one real-life task each week; write conclusions in context; review by criterion |
| 9 to 10 | Timing | Timed sections on screen; pacing rules (for example, a fixed check-point); review every lost mark |
| 11 to 12 | Consolidation | One full practice under realistic conditions; light revision only; check device, login, headphones, charger, quiet space |

### The sequence matters more than the volume

Timed practice is not the starting point. A student with large conceptual gaps who is thrown into timed screen practice becomes anxious and learns little. The order that works is: understand the concept, practise the method, apply it to unfamiliar contexts, then add time. Speed that grows from understanding holds up under pressure. Speed drilled early often collapses on an unfamiliar question.

### A weekly rhythm

1. **Monday to Wednesday:** short daily sessions of twenty to thirty minutes on the week's focus.
2. **One tutor or parent-supported session:** the student works on screen while the helper watches and does not hint.
3. **Weekend:** one timed section, marked against the criteria, with the error audit.
4. **Sunday evening:** five minutes to set next week's focus from the audit.

## How Should Sessions With a Tutor Be Timed?

Sessions should be timed around the school calendar, the student's energy and the traffic, in that order. A tutor can add most in the weeks when the student has the capacity to absorb new habits, which is usually the first half of the plan, and less in the final fortnight, when rest and routine matter more than new material.

- **Earlier is better.** Begin in the first term of MYP 5 where possible, with one lesson a week. A student who starts twelve weeks before the session can still benefit, but with less margin.
- **Avoid the evenings after a long school day** for the demanding tasks (pattern investigations, real-life modelling). Use them for lighter retrieval and error-audit work.
- **Match format to the task.** Screen-based practice suits online sessions, where the tutor can see the student's screen. Written reasoning and diagrams suit home sessions with paper.
- **Leave the final two or three days free.** Last-minute cramming does not help; sleep and a calm routine do.

## Standard or Extended: Does It Change the Preparation?

Yes, in content and in emphasis. Schools often split MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics into standard and extended mathematics, and extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA). A student in extended mathematics has more content to secure; a student in standard has more room to work on communication and application.

Whichever a student takes, the preparation priorities are similar: secure the core topics, practise reasoning in words and symbols, and rehearse the screen routine. ## What Does Good Preparation Look Like in a Session?

A good session has a clear shape and leaves the student with a specific task. It is not a long list of questions. A typical ninety-minute session in the middle of the plan might run like this.

1. **Retrieval (10 minutes).** The student explains, without notes, the method used in last week's real-life task.
2. **Targeted teaching (25 minutes).** One concept or habit, such as stating a general rule from a pattern, taught with a fresh example.
3. **Screen practice (25 minutes).** The student works a short on-screen task while the tutor watches silently.
4. **Review (20 minutes).** The tutor and student go through the work by criterion and update the error audit.
5. **Plan (10 minutes).** One or two specific tasks for the week, and the reason for each.

The silent watching is the most useful part. It shows where the student hesitates, what they skip and whether they reread, none of which a marked script reveals.

## What Does Concept-First Preparation Mean Here?

It means preparing the thinking, not the questions. A student cannot predict every question, and trying to memorise likely items produces a fragile skill that breaks the moment the presentation changes. Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, describes the approach as a progression: concept, then pattern, then application, then expression.

**Concept.** Understand why a method works, not only how to run it.

**Pattern.** Learn to see relationships and test them.

**Application.** Use the mathematics in unfamiliar and real situations.

**Expression.** Communicate the reasoning so another person can follow it.

A student who understands why a rule holds can recognise it when it appears in a graph, in a context or in a table. That flexibility is what unfamiliar questions need. For the conceptual side of MYP maths in detail, see [concept-first MYP maths tutoring](/blog/concept-first-ib-myp-mathematics-tutoring-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/), and for how Ajay's sessions run, [Ajay Vatsyayan's approach to MYP maths](/blog/ib-myp-maths-tutor-pathways-heritage-gurgaon-ajay-vatsyayan/).

Preparation also includes a method for facing a question that looks new. A student can ask: what is given, what is asked, what concept might apply, can I draw it, is there a pattern, can I simplify, can I test an idea? Seven short questions give a starting point when nothing looks familiar, and a starting point is worth more than a blank.

## How Do You Build Confidence Without Adding Pressure?

By making progress visible and keeping the stakes of any single practice low. Many MYP 5 students are capable but anxious, and a screen-based assessment adds the unknown of an interface. The most reliable confidence builders are practical.

- **Familiarity.** Practise on the actual platform or sample as soon as the school allows. Anxiety is largest about the unknown.
- **An error log.** Seeing mistakes categorised and shrinking is a better source of confidence than reassurance.
- **Realistic rehearsals.** One full practice under realistic conditions in the last fortnight, with the device, the room and the timing the student will have.
- **Sleep and routine.** A student who is rested does better than one who has added another hour of revision.
- **A parent who asks the right question.** "What did you learn from that practice?" rather than "What did you get?"

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation?

It is the work a student does to be ready for the on-screen part of MYP assessment in mathematics: secure content, clear reasoning, and comfortable use of the screen format. It is not cramming predicted questions.

Good preparation covers the four criteria, screen entry habits and timing. It starts well before the session and begins by confirming with the school what the format is.

### Is MYP eAssessment compulsory?

No. MYP eAssessment in MYP 5, made up of on-screen examinations and ePortfolios, is optional, and each school decides whether to offer it.

Ask the MYP coordinator whether your school is registered and whether mathematics is included. There is no single national MYP examination in India.

### Does the eAssessment change the maths my child needs to know?

No. The mathematics is the same MYP content assessed against criteria A to D. What changes is how questions are displayed and how answers and working are recorded.

That is why screen practice matters. A student who is strong on paper can lose marks to entry errors, misread graphs or rushed reading.

### When should preparation start?

For MYP 5 mathematics eAssessment preparation, ideally start twelve weeks or more before the session, and in the first term of MYP 5 for students who want a gentle start. Check the school's date and count back.

Starting earlier allows a sequence from understanding to timed practice. Starting later still helps, but the plan should be shorter and focus on the biggest gaps.

### How much timed practice is right?

Enough to build pacing, but only after the concepts are secure. Timed practice before understanding tends to produce anxiety and shallow speed.

A common pattern is to start timed sections around week nine of a twelve-week plan, with one full realistic rehearsal in the final fortnight. Review every lost mark afterwards.

### Can my child use paper for working?

That depends on the rules for the session, which the school and the IB's current guidance set. Ask the coordinator.

Whatever the rule, practise a routine that keeps working visible, because it makes errors easy to find and, where the platform awards method marks, protects them.

### Should my child have a tutor for eAssessment preparation?

Not necessarily. A tutor helps most when marks are lost for reasons the student cannot find, when written reasoning is weak or when screen work causes anxiety.

If the student is progressing steadily, a parent-supported routine may be enough. A trial session can show whether a tutor would add anything.

### Is online or home tutoring better for screen-based preparation?

Online suits screen practice because the tutor can see the student's screen. Home suits written reasoning and diagrams on paper.

Many families combine them. Gurgaon traffic and gated-society entry can shorten home lessons on weekdays, so online on school nights and home at weekends is common.

### What if my child is anxious about the screen format?

Reduce the unknown. Practise on the platform or sample as soon as it is available, rehearse once under realistic conditions and keep the routine calm.

Track progress with an error log rather than scores, and protect sleep. If anxiety is high, tell the school and the tutor early.

### Will a tutor secure a high MYP grade?

No. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and IB Gram does not promise one.

A good tutor finds the real causes of lost marks faster and builds habits that hold under pressure. Judge by diagnosis, a written plan, an error log and growing independence.
