The fee also has to cover the reading of last week's work, the planning of a session around one child's errors, the criterion-by-criterion feedback, and the scarcity of people who can do all of that for IB assessment rather than for a generic textbook. ## What does ₹5,000 per hour actually pay for?

It pays for the whole cycle of a lesson, not only the lesson. A one-to-one MYP maths hour usually sits on top of preparation before it, feedback after it, and the time a specialist spent learning to read IB assessment. Because a tutor can only sell a limited number of teaching hours each week, every unseen hour has to be recovered inside the billed ones. That is the honest mechanism behind a premium rate.

Take a family of costs that never appear on an invoice:

- **Reading the student's work.** A MYP 4 unit test or a Criterion B investigation has to be read, not glanced at. A tutor who marks it against the descriptors needs time that the student never sees.
- **Building the next session.** Questions chosen for this child's gap, not pulled from a stock worksheet.
- **Written or spoken feedback.** Telling a student "your generalisation in part (c) is a pattern, not a rule" takes thought, and writing it down so the student can reuse it takes more.
- **Travel and slot loss.** A home visit across Gurgaon in evening traffic consumes a block that cannot be sold twice.
- **Staying current.** Reading the school's unit planners, the subject guide and the criterion descriptors when they change.

None of this proves a tutor does it. It explains why a tutor who does can honestly cost much more than one who turns up with a photocopied worksheet.

## How the hidden hours add up in a tutor's week

The cleanest way to understand a premium rate is to build a tutor's week from the bottom. The numbers below are an illustration of structure, not a claim about any named person, and they use hours, not rupees, so you can plug in your own quote.

Suppose a specialist teaches fourteen billed hours a week. Each billed hour needs roughly half an hour of preparation if the lesson is genuinely tailored, and a further quarter of an hour of work afterwards: marking a submitted task, logging an error pattern, sending a note. That is about forty-five minutes of unbilled work attached to every sixty billed. Fourteen teaching hours carry another ten or eleven hours behind them. Add travel for home visits, a cancellation or two, and the months when school holidays empty the diary, and the tutor is working a full week to bill a part of it.

The consequence is simple. A tutor who bills fourteen hours cannot charge like a tutor who runs six batches of twenty. The two are in different businesses.

:::bars Where a premium tutor's week goes (illustrative)
Billed lessons | 40 | 14 hrs
Preparation | 22 | 7 hrs
Marking and feedback | 18 | 6 hrs
Travel, gaps, cancellations | 20 | 7 hrs
Illustrative split for a one-to-one specialist; real weeks vary by tutor and by month.
:::

Read the chart as an argument about structure, not about a particular tutor. If preparation and marking shrink to near zero, the premium argument collapses with them, because the tutor is selling the same lesson as everyone else at a higher price.

## Why MYP maths is not "normal" maths for a tutor

The International Baccalaureate (IB) assesses MYP maths against four criteria: A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating, and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. Each is scored 0 to 8, the total out of 32 converts to a final grade from 1 to 7, and there is no single national MYP examination in India. Schools write their own tasks, so what the student faces is school-specific and criterion-marked.

That matters for tutoring in three ways.

First, a perfect chapter test can coexist with a weak grade. A student who gets every calculation right but writes "x = 12" with no explanation, no definition of the variable and no check against the context may collect most of Criterion A and little of Criterion C or D.

Second, Criterion B tasks reward a process, not an answer. A student must notice a pattern, test it, state a general rule and justify it. A tutor trained on drill-and-practice finds this hard to coach, because there is no worksheet to hand over.

Third, the criteria are read by the school's own teacher, so a good tutor works with the descriptors the school actually uses. The detailed criterion language is explained for parents in [MYP maths criteria A, B, C and D explained for parents](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/), and the student-facing version is in [MYP mathematics criteria A, B, C and D](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/).

A generic maths tutor can learn all of this. The question for a parent is whether they already have, and the section on testing a tutor below shows how to find out in one conversation.

## What a premium rate should buy, line by line

Do not accept a general promise of "specialist teaching". Ask for the fee to be translated into things you can check. A rate of ₹5,000 per hour is defensible when most of the following are present, and weaker with each one that is missing.

| What you are told you are paying for | What it should look like in practice | How you would notice if it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | A first session or two spent finding which criterion and which skill is costing marks | Lesson one is just "let us start chapter 5" |
| Criterion knowledge | The tutor marks your child's work against A-D and explains the gap | Feedback is "be careful" or "practise more" |
| Tailored sessions | Each lesson targets a stated weakness from the last one | Every student gets the same worksheet |
| Written follow-up | A short note or error log after each session | Nothing in writing; parents hear "it went well" |
| Unfamiliar-problem teaching | The student practises choosing a method, not applying a named one | Only topic-by-topic drill |
| Review points | A planned check of progress against school task results | Progress is described only by the tutor's feeling |
| Continuity | The same person each week, and the same notes | Substitutes, or a tutor who forgets last week |

A premium tutor who is honest will say which of these they do and which they do not. A rate that bundles all seven and a rate that bundles two are not the same product even if the invoice says the same number.

## Is that rate a fair figure for a specialist?

It is a figure parents ask about, and whether it is fair depends on the market and the person. It is not an official rate, not an average, and not what IB Gram charges. Fees are set by individual tutors and move with programme, subject level, the tutor's experience, home travel versus online, session length and package. Pricing a specialist per hour hides most of that, so the sensible comparison is the whole arrangement: hours per month, what is included, and what you would pay for the same outcome another way.

Some honest points on each side.

**Reasons a high rate can be fair**

- The tutor teaches fewer students and so cannot spread preparation cost across a batch.
- The tutor is one of a small number who can coach Criterion B and D tasks, not just chapter exercises.
- Sessions are at home in a locality where travel eats a lot of the diary.
- The tutor gives written feedback and keeps a record.

**Reasons it may not be**

- The rate is simply a signal of status, and the lesson is a standard hour.
- The tutor has strong maths but no sight of how your child's school writes tasks.
- The fee includes nothing the student would not get from a cheaper, equally attentive tutor.
- The need is small: a student who is comfortable but wants a little extra practice.

A useful sanity check is to ask how many hours a month the plan needs. A student who needs eight billed hours a month is in a very different position from one who is told to take three sessions a week for the whole year. Ask the tutor to defend the number of hours, not only the rate.

## Does a high hourly rate mean a better tutor?

No. A high rate tells you what the tutor charges, not what they deliver. Rate and quality are correlated only loosely, because rate is partly a market signal (neighbourhood, reputation, demand) and partly a cost recovery, and neither guarantees a better lesson. A modest tutor with excellent criterion knowledge can outperform an expensive one who teaches generic maths, and the reverse also happens.

Treat price as a prompt to ask questions, not as an answer. If the tutor is willing to explain exactly how an hour is used, show a sample of feedback with the student's name removed, and describe how they would diagnose your child, the rate becomes something you can judge. If the response to any of those requests is a polite vagueness, the rate has told you nothing about quality.

One more point parents rarely consider: a fee that looks high per hour can be cheaper per outcome than a lower fee if it needs fewer sessions to fix the problem. The reverse is equally possible. That is why the conversation about the plan matters more than the conversation about the rate.

## What a premium session looks like in practice

Consider a student in MYP 4 who is described as "good at maths" but keeps landing in the middle of the grade range on school tasks. A premium first hour might not touch a single new topic. The tutor asks the student to bring the last three marked tasks and a recent notebook. Together they read the criterion descriptors, the teacher's comments and the marks. The tutor sorts the lost marks into four columns, A to D, and finds that most of them sit in C and D.

The second session works on one weak habit at a time. The tutor sets a real-world problem about, say, a phone plan with two pricing options, and asks the student only to write down what mathematics they would use and why, before any calculation. The next week the student solves the same style of problem and writes a conclusion that links the answer back to the context. The week after, the tutor sets a short Criterion B-style pattern task and asks the student to state a general rule and test it with one more case.

Notice what the fee is paying for. None of this is hard maths. It is the tutor's ability to see where the marks went, to choose a task that exposes it, and to give feedback specific enough to change behaviour next time. A tutor without that diagnostic skill would likely have said "do more equations" and billed an hour of equations.

Now a second student, in MYP 3, who has lost confidence after a poor term and whose arithmetic fluency is shaky. For this child the same premium tutor would be a poor use of money. The need is rebuilding fundamentals with patient repetition, and a good tutor at a lower rate can do that well. Matching the person to the problem is the whole point, and we return to it below.

## The Concept, Application, Expression approach

Ajay Vatsyayan, IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, teaches around a sequence that is useful for understanding why specialist MYP tutoring is structured differently from drill. It runs Concept, then Application, then Expression.

- **Concept.** The student understands why the mathematics works, so a formula is a result they could rebuild rather than a phrase they memorised.
- **Application.** The student moves the idea into unfamiliar settings, including real-life contexts where the method is not announced.
- **Expression.** The student learns to write the reasoning down: defining variables, stating the method, interpreting the answer.

The three stages line up with the criteria. Concept feeds Criterion A, Application feeds B and D, Expression feeds C. A student who is strong at the first and weak at the third is the student who "knows it but cannot show it", a very common profile in MYP 4 and MYP 5. If a tutor of any price cannot describe how they work on all three, that is the useful signal.

## When is a premium MYP maths tutor worth it?

A premium tutor is worth considering when the problem is a specific, diagnosable gap in how the student earns criterion marks, and when the student has enough remaining time in the programme for the work to pay off. It is least worth it when the need is routine practice, homework supervision or rebuilding basics, because those can be met well at lower cost.

Look for a combination of these signals:

1. The student's understanding is decent but marks are stuck across several tasks, which points to criterion habits rather than knowledge.
2. Teacher comments repeat the same phrase: "explain your reasoning", "justify", "relate to the context".
3. The student freezes when a question does not name the method.
4. A MYP 4 or MYP 5 student is heading into DP and the family wants the Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA) route open. Extended mathematics in MYP 4-5 prepares for AA, and the school's coordinator makes the final placement.
5. There is time. A tutor needs weeks to change a habit, so a fortnight before a test is a poor moment to start.

If three or more of these are true, a conversation with a specialist is reasonable. If none are, a premium rate is hard to justify.

## When is a premium MYP tutor not worth it?

A premium tutor is not worth it when the student is already progressing comfortably, when the need is only homework help, when the gaps are basic arithmetic or algebra that any patient tutor can repair, or when the family cannot sustain the hours that would make the work effective. Paying for a few expensive sessions and then stopping rarely changes a habit.

Specific cases where money is better spent elsewhere:

- **A student who is coping and wants reassurance.** A short check-in with the school's maths teacher is free and often enough.
- **A student with foundational gaps.** Fractions, negative numbers, linear equations. A patient tutor can fix these without any IB specialism, and repetition matters more than specialisation.
- **A student whose real problem is something else.** Anxiety, sleep, workload, or a language barrier will not be solved by a better maths tutor.
- **A family under pressure to buy the "best".** Parents sometimes buy the top rate as insurance. It is not insurance. No tutor can guarantee a grade, and an expensive tutor who is a poor fit for your child is a poor fit at any price.
- **A plan that is mostly travel.** If much of the fee is a home visit across the city and the lesson would work just as well online, consider the mode. The home-versus-online trade-off is set out in [IB home tutor vs online tutor in Gurgaon](/blog/ib-home-tutor-vs-online-tutor-gurgaon/).

It is a fair outcome of this post if a reader decides they do not need a premium tutor. The value of knowing what is behind the rate is exactly that you can say no with confidence.

## How to test a tutor before you pay

You can find out in one conversation, and in one trial session, whether the premium is real. Use the steps below, in order. They work for any tutor, at any price.

1. **Ask what the tutor does outside the lesson.** Preparation, marking, feedback, notes. Listen for specific answers, not adjectives.
2. **Ask them to explain the four criteria in plain words.** A, B, C and D, with an example of what loses marks in each. Someone who has taught MYP will do this easily.
3. **Give them a real piece of your child's marked work.** Ask what they see. A specialist will name the criterion and the habit within minutes.
4. **Ask how they would diagnose your child.** The answer should involve looking at work, not a test to rank the student.
5. **Request a demo session.** A trial hour shows how the tutor talks to your child, which no description can.
6. **Ask for the plan in hours.** How many sessions a month, for how long, and how you will know it is working.
7. **Agree a review point.** After four to six weeks, look at the next school task and decide whether to continue.

A tutor who objects to any of these steps is giving you information.

## Questions to ask before you pay the fee

The old advice about five questions still holds, but a longer list is more useful when real money is involved. Take the ones that matter to your child.

- Which MYP years and subjects do you teach, and how many students do you take at once?
- How do you adapt when the school's task style differs from the textbook?
- What written feedback will we receive after each session, and in what form?
- How do you teach Criterion B and D, where there is no single right method?
- What do you do when a student is not improving after a month?
- What is your cancellation and make-up policy?
- If we choose to stop, is there any commitment beyond the next session?
- Will the same person teach every session?

If a tutor answers honestly that they do not do something, treat it as a point in their favour. A tutor who claims to do everything is less believable than one who describes what they do well.

## Red flags that justify walking away

Premium pricing makes some behaviours more costly to ignore.

- **A guaranteed grade.** No tutor can guarantee a 7 or any other result.
- **A fee that cannot be explained.** If the tutor cannot say what is in the hour, the premium is a label.
- **A claimed link with a school or the IB.** Claims of official affiliation should be verified, and IB Gram is not affiliated with any school named in our posts either.
- **No interest in the student's actual work.** A tutor who never asks to see a marked task is teaching a syllabus, not your child.
- **Pressure to prepay a large block.** A trial session and a short initial commitment protect you.
- **"IB is just harder CBSE."** That is a sign the tutor has not studied the criteria. The differences between the two teaching approaches are explained in [IB MYP maths vs CBSE maths teaching approach](/blog/ib-myp-maths-vs-cbse-maths-teaching-approach/).

None of these is automatically fatal in a tutor with an otherwise good record, but several together are enough to look elsewhere.

## Looking at the total, not the hour

Hourly rates make a plan feel smaller or larger than it is. A better question is the total commitment over a defined period, and what it is meant to change. Consider three plans for the same MYP 5 student, described only in hours.

- **Short and targeted.** Six to eight sessions over two months, aimed at one weakness, with a review at the end.
- **Steady support.** One session a week through a term, with notes and a check after each school task.
- **Intensive window.** Several sessions a week for a few weeks before an eAssessment or a major task. MYP eAssessment in MYP 5 is optional and each school decides whether to offer it, so check with the school before planning around it.

Each of these can cost the same total with a different hourly rate. The short, targeted plan with an expensive specialist can be cheaper than a long, vague plan with a modest tutor, and it can also be a waste if the diagnosis was wrong. ## Why parents choose premium, and how to verify it is real

Parents who choose a premium tutor usually give three reasons: diagnosis, continuity and criterion knowledge. Each of those can be checked. Diagnosis shows up in the first two sessions: if nothing in them tells you something you did not know about your child's work, the diagnosis was thin. Continuity shows up in the notes: if next week's session starts from this week's error, the tutor is tracking. Criterion knowledge shows up in feedback: if a tutor can say which descriptor a piece of work met and which it missed, the knowledge is real.

## What if the student is moving into DP maths?

A MYP 5 student with a DP choice ahead is a special case, because the cost of a wrong fit continues for two years. DP Mathematics comes as Analysis and Approaches (AA), which is algebraic and proof-heavy, and Applications and Interpretation (AI), which is modelling and statistics-heavy, each at Higher Level (HL) or Standard Level (SL). Students heading for engineering or economics degrees are often advised towards AA, but the right choice depends on the degree and the student, and the school's DP coordinator makes the final placement.

A premium tutor with DP experience can help a MYP 5 student see what the next step requires: the shift to exam papers, the demands of the maths exploration, the need for fluent algebra. That bridge is a reasonable use of a specialist hour. ## Common mistakes parents make with premium quotes

Parents are rarely careless. The mistakes below come from a reasonable wish to do the best for a child, and each is avoidable.

**Treating the rate as a quality score.** Rate is a signal of cost and demand. It says nothing about whether this tutor suits your child.

**Hiring before diagnosing.** Booking a block of sessions before anyone has looked at the student's marked tasks usually means paying to find out the problem.

**Starting too late.** A tutor can change habits across a term; they cannot rescue a task due in four days. Premium rates are particularly poor value for last-minute rescue.

**Letting the tutor replace the school.** A tutor works alongside the student's own teacher. If the student has not asked the teacher what a criterion means, a tutor is a costly substitute for a free conversation.

**Buying hours instead of a plan.** Ask for a purpose, a duration and a review.

**Ignoring the student's view.** A fourteen-year-old who dislikes a tutor learns little from them. The trial hour is partly for the student to decide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are IB tutors so expensive?

Not every IB tutor is expensive, but a specialist's rate has to cover unseen preparation, marking and feedback as well as teaching time. A tutor who sells a limited number of one-to-one hours cannot spread those costs across a batch, so the hourly fee is higher. The price reflects real value only if the preparation and feedback genuinely take place, which you can check by asking what happens outside the lesson.

### Is ₹5,000 per hour worth it for an MYP maths student?

It can be worth it for a student with a specific, diagnosable gap in criterion habits and enough time for the work to take effect. It is hard to justify for routine homework help or basic fluency. Judge the value by the diagnosis, the plan and the feedback, not by the rate alone. A short trial hour shows more than any description of the tutor's credentials.

### Does a high hourly rate mean better marks?

No, and no tutor can guarantee a grade. Results depend on the student's starting point, effort, consistency, school instruction and the match with the tutor. A premium tutor can offer better diagnosis and sharper feedback, which improve the odds of progress, but a promise of a particular outcome is a red flag, not a selling point.

### How can I tell whether a premium tutor really knows MYP maths?

Ask them to explain Criteria A, B, C and D in plain words and to read a piece of your child's marked work. A real specialist names the criterion where marks were lost within minutes and explains the habit behind it. Someone who answers with "more practice" or talks only about chapters is probably teaching general maths under an IB label.

### What should be included in an hourly fee for MYP maths?

A one-to-one hour should come with preparation for that student, feedback on work the student brings, and some record of what was covered and what to do next. Ask the tutor to list what is in the fee. If the list is only "sixty minutes of teaching", compare it with other tutors on that basis and decide accordingly.

### Can a regular maths tutor teach MYP maths?

Often yes, if they take the time to learn the criteria and the school's task style. A strong teacher of conventional maths can adapt, especially for MYP 3 and for fundamentals. The risk is for criterion-heavy work in MYP 4 and 5, where a tutor who does not know how Criterion B and D are marked can spend effort on the wrong skills. Test this before assuming.

### When should an MYP student start with a tutor?

Start when there is a specific problem and enough time to work on it, not at a fixed year. A student who is losing marks on the same criterion across several tasks has a reason to start. Waiting until a week before a major task limits what any tutor can do. The timing guide, [when to start IB tutoring](/blog/when-to-start-ib-tutoring-gurgaon/), goes through MYP 3 to 5.

### Is group coaching a better use of money than a premium tutor?

It depends on the problem. Group coaching can give practice volume and a regular rhythm at a lower cost, and suits a student who needs exposure to many questions. It gives less feedback on criteria and less adaptation to one child's weaknesses. A premium tutor suits specific gaps in how marks are earned. Many families use one for a defined stage and then the other.

### What if we pay for a premium tutor and see no progress after a month?

Ask the tutor to show you what changed in the student's work and what they will try next. A month is short, but there should be evidence of a diagnosis, a plan and a change in at least one habit. If there is none, ask for a different approach or a different match. With IB Gram, a family can ask for a different tutor, subject to availability.

### Does the tutor's location change the fee?

Yes. A home visit in a distant society during evening traffic takes more of the tutor's diary than the lesson itself, and the cost is recovered in the fee. Online sessions remove most of that. If the lesson works as well on screen, online can lower the total, though some students concentrate better with a tutor in the room.

### How many sessions does a MYP student usually need?

There is no standard number. A targeted plan on one criterion may need a handful of sessions, while support across a whole term needs weekly lessons. Ask the tutor to propose a number tied to the diagnosis and to set a review point after four to six weeks, so you can adjust based on the next school task.

## Next steps

1. Gather your child's last three marked MYP maths tasks and write down which criterion lost the most marks. If you cannot tell, that is the first job for a tutor or the school's teacher.
2. Decide what problem you are hiring someone to solve, and ask any tutor you speak to for a plan in hours with a review date, not just a rate.
3. Book a trial session, let your child judge the fit, and look at the next school task before you commit to more.
