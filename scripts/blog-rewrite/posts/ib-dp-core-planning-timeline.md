A workable IB DP core timeline puts the Extended Essay (EE) question in place by the end of the first term of DP1, finishes an EE draft before DP2 begins, keeps Creativity, Activity, Service (CAS) running weekly from the first month, and treats Theory of Knowledge (TOK) as two separate deadlines, the exhibition and the essay, that each need a quiet window. Do that and the core stops competing with subject Internal Assessments (IAs) and mock exams. Skip it and all three arrive together in the worst six months of the Diploma.

This guide is for DP1 and DP2 students in the International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP), and for the parents who watch the calendar fill up. It does not teach how to write the essay or what TOK examiners reward; those have their own guides. It answers one question: given that the core has three parts, each with its own deadline, and that your subject teachers are also setting IAs, what should you be doing in each term, and what should you refuse to leave until later?

School calendars differ. Your DP coordinator's dates always override anything printed here. What follows is the shape that works in most Indian IB schools, where DP1 starts around April or August and the May examination session falls at the end of DP2.

## What Is the IB DP Core and Why Does Its Timing Matter?

The DP core is the part of the Diploma that sits outside the six subject groups. It has three components: CAS, the Extended Essay and Theory of Knowledge. Together with the six subjects it forms the full programme. TOK and the EE are graded A to E and combine for up to 3 points on top of the 42 available from six subjects, so the maximum Diploma score is 45. CAS carries no grade, but it must be completed for the Diploma to be awarded.

Timing matters more here than in any subject, for a structural reason. A subject teacher drags you along: there is a lesson on Monday, a test on Friday, a topic that moves on whether you are ready or not. The core has far less of that pull. Much of it is self-managed, and it is graded late. A chemistry gap shows up in a test within weeks. An EE that has not been started shows up eighteen months later, in November of DP2, when it is expensive to fix.

There is also a asymmetry in what failure costs. An E in either TOK or the EE is a failing condition, and CAS incomplete means no Diploma regardless of subject scores. A student with excellent subject grades can lose the whole qualification to a core component that was treated as background. That is rare. It is also entirely preventable with a calendar.

:::stats The three parts of the core at a glance
CAS | Completed, not graded
EE | Up to 4,000 words, about 40 hours
TOK | Exhibition plus essay of up to 1,600 words
3 | Maximum points from TOK and EE together
:::

### Which part has the longest lead time?

The Extended Essay. It is an independent research essay of up to 4,000 words, supervised by a teacher at your own school, with reflection sessions recorded along the way, and the work adds up to roughly 40 hours. Forty hours sounds modest until you remember it is spread across reading, a research question that changes twice, data or source collection, drafting, and revision after supervisor comments. In practice the elapsed time is closer to nine months than nine weeks, because the hours cannot be done in a single stretch.

CAS has the longest duration but the lowest weekly load. TOK has the shortest lead time but a hard prescribed-title deadline. So the planning problem is mostly about the EE, with the other two fitted around it.

## The Four Collision Points in a Two-Year Diploma

Before the term-by-term plan, it helps to see where the pressure actually lands. Over two years there are four places where core work and subject work fight for the same weeks. Most late-night panic can be traced to one of them.

| Collision point | When it typically happens | What collides | How to defuse it |
|---|---|---|---|
| End of DP1 exams | Final term of DP1 | School year-end tests, EE research, first IA drafts | Settle the EE question before the exam term starts |
| The DP2 opening | First term of DP2 | EE final draft, TOK exhibition, IA drafts in three or four subjects | Arrive with an EE draft already written |
| Mock season | Several months before May; each school sets its own dates | TOK essay, last IA submissions, mock papers | Finish the EE and CAS reviews before mocks start |
| The final stretch | Between mocks and May | Revision, loose IA edits, CAS closing | Close everything that is not an exam by this point |

The second collision point is the one families underestimate. DP2 term one has more deadlines than any other stretch: university application documents, predicted-grade discussions, IA submissions, the EE, and the TOK exhibition in many schools. A student who walks into it with no EE draft has to write 4,000 words in the same weeks a physics IA is due. A student who walks in with a rough draft has to revise it, which is a very different workload.

## DP1 Term 1: Set the Systems, Not the Outcomes

The first term is about habits and a few small decisions. Nobody expects an EE title in September. Everyone should have a CAS routine by October.

### CAS: start a weekly slot, not a grand plan

CAS asks for ongoing engagement across creativity, physical activity and service, with reflection on what you learned. The most common first-term error is to chase one impressive event and then do nothing for four months. A better pattern is a weekly activity you can sustain through DP2: a sport or training habit, an instrument, a community teaching slot, a school club you actually help run.

Pick the schedule first and the activity second. If Tuesday and Saturday afternoons are the only protected slots your week allows, choose something that fits them. A student in Gurgaon who commutes forty minutes to school and has IA-heavy evenings should not sign up for an activity that needs a drive across the city on weekday evenings.

Record as you go. Photograph, note, reflect within a few days while you still remember what actually happened. Reflections written eight months later read like fiction, and CAS coordinators can tell.

### EE: notice, do not decide

This term, build a list of two or three subjects where you could happily spend forty hours. The honest filter is not "which subject scores highest" but "which subject do I have a real question in". Students who pick the subject they expect to score well in, rather than the one they are curious about, tend to hit a wall at about the 2,000-word mark. Check your school's rules on which subjects supervise the EE and how many students each teacher can take; popular supervisors fill up early.

### TOK: keep an example file

TOK rewards specific, real examples. Start a simple running document with one-line entries: a news story where two experts disagreed, a method used in your biology IA that shaped what you could conclude, a historical source you doubted in history class. You will mine this for the exhibition and the essay. Ten minutes a week is plenty.

## DP1 Term 2: Make the Extended Essay Real

This is the term where an idea becomes a project. It is also the term where most students quietly lose a month, because nothing is due yet.

### Choosing the subject and supervisor

By the middle of this term, confirm your subject and your supervisor. If your school allocates supervisors, ask for a first meeting immediately; if you choose, ask the teacher who knows the subject best and will actually reply to messages. Reflection sessions with the supervisor are part of the formal process and are recorded, so the first conversation is the start of the paper trail, not an optional chat.

### From topic to workable question

A topic is "inflation". A question is something you could answer, argue and defend within the word limit using sources you can reach. Test every candidate question against four checks:

1. Can I answer it in roughly 4,000 words without skimming?
2. Does it need analysis, or can I just describe?
3. Can I get the sources or data in the next six weeks without special access?
4. Does it fit the assessment approach of the subject I chose?

A question about the effect of a particular policy in a particular country across a defined period passes these. A question about "the causes of inflation" does not. For a fuller treatment of research questions, supervisor sessions and the criteria, the lane belongs to [how to write an IB Extended Essay](/blog/how-to-write-a-perfect-ib-extended-essay-ee/). The planning point here is only that the question must be settled in time.

### TOK exhibition: practise the move early

The exhibition uses three objects linked to one of 35 IA prompts, up to 950 words, internally assessed, and it counts for one third of the TOK grade. You do not need your objects yet. You do need to understand the move it asks for: starting from a real object and showing what it reveals about knowledge in the world. Try the exercise once on any object on your desk. It exposes how hard it is to write about the link rather than the object.

### CAS: the first honest audit

At the end of term two, open your CAS records and ask whether there is a creative, an active and a service strand, and whether the reflections say anything beyond "I enjoyed it". If one strand is missing, add it now while there is still a year and a half. Closing a missing strand in DP2 is possible but uncomfortable.

## DP1 Term 3: Lock the Research Question and Read

The final term of DP1 is crowded with year-end tests and sometimes the first IA drafts. It still has to carry two essential EE jobs.

**Lock the question.** Agree the wording with your supervisor and write it down. You can refine phrasing later, but the scope should not change after this point. A question that is still being reinvented in August of DP2 means you have not been reading the right material.

**Build an outline and a reading log.** Not a draft, an outline: a one-page skeleton showing the argument in four or five moves, with the source or data you expect to use for each. Log every source with a line on what it contributes. A reading log written during research is the difference between a bibliography built in an hour and one that takes a weekend of regret.

This is also when subject IAs begin to bite. Chemistry, physics or biology investigations, the mathematics exploration, history and economics commentaries: each subject has its own timeline and its own teacher setting dates. The way these interact with the core is covered under the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/); for planning purposes, add every IA deadline you know to the same calendar as your EE milestones. The point is to see overlaps in April, not discover them in September.

IA work in subjects | 20 | about 20%
EE reading and outline | 12 | about 12%
CAS activity and reflection | 8 | about 8%
TOK examples and practice | 5 | about 5%

## The Summer Between DP1 and DP2: Write the First EE Draft

This is the single decision that most affects how DP2 feels. The break is long, the subject load is zero, and the EE needs a continuous block of attention that term time rarely offers.

A realistic target is a full first draft, rough and imperfect, by the time school reopens. A student who writes 4,000 words across the break at a pace of, say, 500 words on each of eight writing days has done it without pain. The sentences will be clumsy. The argument may need restructuring. That is what the first term of DP2 is for.

### What a good summer EE week looks like

- Two or three focused sessions of two to three hours each, not daily guilt.
- One session spent only on sources and data, one only on writing.
- A message to the supervisor before the break, asking whether they will read a draft in the first week back and what format they want.
- A rest day that is actually a rest day. The aim is a draft, not burnout in June.

Many families in Gurgaon travel in the break, take summer programmes, or go abroad for university visits. That is fine. A plan built around eight writing days fits around most trips. What does not work is an EE plan that says "I will do it when I get back".

The CAS side of the break is easier. Use it for something with a clear start and end: a service project you can run in two weeks, a creative piece, a training block. Record it immediately.

If a draft is not realistic because the student has switched topics, say so in July, not in October. Ask the supervisor whether an intensive session or two in the first fortnight of DP2 could rescue the timeline. A late honest message to a supervisor costs one awkward conversation; an undisclosed gap costs the supervisor's goodwill when you most need it.

## DP2 Term 1: Revise, Submit, and Still Make the Exhibition

By now the shape of the work changes. The EE is no longer a creative project; it is a revision project with a deadline.

### The EE: revise against the argument

Reread your draft with a single question: does each section help answer my research question? Trim anything that is background padding. Check that every claim has evidence behind it, that every source in the text appears in the bibliography and vice versa, and that your own reflections on the research process are recorded as the school requires. Supervisors typically give feedback on a draft; follow their guidance on format, since the EE guide was updated for first assessment in 2027 and you should follow the version your DP coordinator issues rather than any criteria list quoted online.

Treat supervisor feedback as a deadline, not a courtesy. If your supervisor can read the draft in the second week and you submit in the fourth, you have two weeks to respond. If you submit it in the third week of a month in which five other students also submit, the feedback arrives late and the revision window collapses.

### The TOK exhibition: do it once, properly

Many schools schedule the exhibition in this term or just before. Pick objects that you can actually discuss, not ones that merely look impressive. A plain, specific object, such as a lab data sheet or a school photograph, usually makes for a better exhibition than a famous artefact you know nothing about. Write each object commentary around the same question: what does this object show about knowledge in the real world, and why does it matter here? Keep within the 950-word limit; the prompt is assessed, not the object.

### CAS: begin the closing review

Check the evidence file again. Make sure the CAS project, if your school requires one, has been completed rather than merely planned. Hold the formal conversations your coordinator asks for. Do not let CAS drop; it is easy to pause a weekly activity in the busiest term and then find a four-month gap in the record.

## Before Mocks: Finish What Can Be Finished

Mock exams are where the DP2 plan either holds or breaks. Predicted grades usually come from them, and those grades feed university applications. A mock paper sat while a TOK essay is half-written and an EE is still under revision is a mock paper sat at half strength.

A useful rule: **nothing that is not an exam should still be open at the start of mock season, except the TOK essay and any final IA edits the school has agreed**. In practice that means the EE is submitted or submitted-pending-one-fix, the exhibition is complete, and CAS has only reflections left.

Schools set their own mock dates and their own internal deadlines for each component, usually several months before May. Some put the TOK essay in the term before mocks, some after. Your job is to read the actual dates the school has issued, enter them in a single document, and work backwards from the one that comes first.

:::steps A weekly core check, ten minutes on Sunday
CAS | Did I log this week's activity
EE | One concrete step done or booked
TOK | One example or planning note added
IA | Any deadline inside 14 days
Calendar | Anything new from school entered
:::

### How the TOK essay fits

The essay answers one of six prescribed titles released for each exam session, runs up to 1,600 words, is externally assessed, and counts for two thirds of the TOK grade. Because titles are released at a set point in the session, the essay window is the part of the core you cannot pre-write. What you can pre-build is the skill: practise unpacking a title, choosing two areas of knowledge, and finding examples from your own file. The course covers the core theme (knowledge and the knower), two of five optional themes and five areas of knowledge: history, the human sciences, the natural sciences, mathematics and the arts. Students who have used their example file since DP1 arrive at the essay with material; those who have not often spend the week hunting examples they could have noted in October.

## Why Students Fall Behind, and the Pattern That Works

It is useful to name the three failure patterns, because each has a different fix.

**The slow drift.** Nothing is wrong in October, nothing is wrong in December, and in March there is a very large gap. This is usually CAS: one skipped reflection, then ten. The fix is smallness. A five-line reflection written within a few days beats a perfect one never written.

**The topic hunt.** The student cannot settle on an EE topic and keeps returning to the shortlist every few weeks. It feels like diligence. It is avoidance. The fix is a deadline set with the supervisor: by this date I commit, and I change only the phrasing afterwards.

**The late discovery.** The student realises in DP2 that the EE, an IA in two subjects and the TOK essay all want the same weekend. By this point the choice is which one to do badly. The fix is earlier: collect every date in September, put them in one document, and use the four collision points as a check.

A typical case: consider a DP1 student taking three Higher Level subjects who treats the core as "later". By the end of DP1 term 3 the EE has no confirmed question and the summer is half taken by a family trip. The student can still recover, but only by cutting something, usually sleep. A student who takes the same load but writes a 500-word outline in April and drafts 2,000 words in June arrives at DP2 with a very different year ahead. The ability is the same. The calendar is not.

### A note on supervisors and teachers

Your subject teachers may be your EE supervisor, your TOK teacher and the person marking an IA. They will not remember every deadline. Do not rely on a reminder that never arrives. A short email at the start of each term asking for any new dates keeps both sides honest.

## What Parents Can Do Without Taking Over

Parents often feel the pressure first because they see the calendar fill up while the student still seems relaxed. The line to hold is that the core is the student's work: the EE, the CAS reflections and the TOK writing must be the student's own, and no one may write, rewrite or edit the submission for them. Within that line, there is plenty a family can do.

- Put the school's core dates on a shared wall calendar, once, in September.
- Ask one question a week, such as "what is the next core thing due", rather than reading drafts.
- Protect the writing blocks in the summer, including from family plans, where reasonable.
- Watch for the early signs of overload: sleep dropping, a subject grade slipping, a student avoiding the topic when it comes up.
- Keep university planning separate from core planning; they share a calendar but not a conversation. See [IB university preparation](/blog/ib-university-preparation-guide/) for how the application timeline interacts with predicted grades.

If a student is drifting and a family wants outside help, specialist support exists for the Extended Essay and TOK, and a tutor who teaches the underlying research or argument skill can help a student find their way. A tutor can teach a skill, question reasoning and point at a criterion; the writing remains the student's own, and IB Gram does not match tutors to write or edit submitted work. You can see what that looks like in practice on the pages for an [IB Extended Essay tutor in Gurgaon](/ib-extended-essay-tutor-in-gurgaon/) and an [IB TOK tutor in Gurgaon](/ib-tok-tutor-in-gurgaon/).

## When Should a Student Get Outside Help With the Core?

A student should look for help when the core falls behind its own timeline, not when the final deadline is close. Three signals suggest it is time: the EE research question is still unsettled at the end of DP1 term 2, there is no draft by the start of DP2, or CAS reflections have a gap of more than a couple of months. Early help is cheaper in stress and in hours.

The honest counterpoint: many students do not need a tutor to follow an IB DP core timeline at all. If the school has a strong EE programme, a supervisor who meets regularly and a CAS coordinator who checks records, the plan in this guide is enough on its own. Outside support makes sense when the supervisor is overloaded, the student works better with a regular external meeting, or the family wants someone to check pacing. Ask what the support will actually do, whether it is regular, and who owns the writing. If the answer to the last question is not "the student", walk away.

## How the Core Fits With Subject IAs

The core and the subject IAs share one scarce resource: independent working hours in the evenings and at weekends. In DP1 term 3 and DP2 term 1 the two compete directly, since a lab write-up or a mathematics exploration needs the same quiet block as an EE section.

Three practical habits help:

1. **Stagger, do not stack.** If two IAs are due close together, move EE writing earlier so it is not in the same fortnight.
2. **Use the same skill twice.** A student writing a biology EE and a biology IA is building research design, data handling and evaluation across both. Where the subject allows it, notice the overlap, though never reuse text between submissions.
3. **Ask each teacher for their IA calendar in the first week.** Subject IAs follow different timetables. Put them all in the same document as your core dates.

This is the practical heart of any IB DP core timeline: one calendar, not five. For the rules of the IAs themselves, including what teachers and tutors may and may not do, see the [IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/), and for worked examples of strong and weak IA choices, [IB IA examples](/blog/complete-guide-to-ib-internal-assessment-ia-with-examples/).

## What Does a Calm DP2 Look Like?

A calm DP2 looks like a student who walks into the first term with an EE draft, who has an exhibition booked into a known week, who has not missed a CAS reflection in two months, and who treats the TOK essay as a defined task with a start date. Subject IAs are still demanding, mocks are still stressful, and university applications are still a lot. But nothing is a surprise.

A calm DP2 is the payoff of a sensible IB DP core timeline, and it does not mean an easy year. It means the hard parts arrive one at a time. The student who got the core out of the way early has the evenings in the spring for the thing that actually decides the final grade: examination preparation. The last stretch is covered in [the last 30 days before IB exams](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/), and the wider DP revision structure in the [IB exam preparation guide](/blog/ib-exam-preparation-guide/). Neither is something you want to begin while an EE is still open.

## If You Are Already Behind: A Recovery Plan

Plenty of students read a guide like this in the wrong month. Here is how to recover from each common late start.

### Behind at the start of DP2, no EE draft

Tell the supervisor this week. Agree a reduced scope if the question is too large. Set a draft date three weeks out with a minimum word count per week. Pause optional commitments, including extra CAS events, until the draft exists. Protect two weekend mornings for writing and do not move them.

### CAS reflections missing for several months

Do not fabricate. Write honest, short reflections now that describe what you did and what you learned, dated truthfully. Talk to the CAS coordinator; they have seen this before and would rather hear it in October than May. Then resume weekly logging.

### TOK essay title released and nothing planned

Spend the first two days only on the title: unpack the key words, decide what the title is actually asking, choose two areas of knowledge. Write a plan before a paragraph. A plan reviewed by your TOK teacher before writing beats a draft that needs structural rework.

### Everything due in the same fortnight

List every deadline with its true date. Identify which one has the hardest edge (the one that cannot move) and plan backwards from it. Then ask each teacher whether any soft date can shift. The hard edges are usually fewer than the panic suggests.

## How This Plan Changes by School and Subject

No timeline survives contact with a school calendar unchanged. A few common variations are worth planning for.

**A school that front-loads the exhibition.** Some schools run the TOK exhibition in DP1. If yours does, the exhibition work in the plan above moves forward a term, and the TOK essay becomes the main DP2 task.

**An EE in a lab science.** Sciences need data collection, which may depend on lab access or experimental time. If your investigation needs a growing season, a reaction series or repeated measures, the lab work must happen in DP1 or the summer. Writing can wait; the data cannot.

**An EE in a humanities or language subject.** Sources are easier to reach, but argument is harder to build. Reading time should be longer and drafting later.

**A world studies or interdisciplinary essay.** These require two perspectives held together. Allow extra time to settle the question and to keep it from sprawling.

**Students on a heavy co-curricular load.** Sports trials, music examinations and debating calendars do not shrink for the Diploma. Count them honestly when estimating weekly hours. A student with six practice sessions a week has a different EE calendar from one with two.

**Students joining from another board.** A student arriving in DP1 after CBSE, ICSE or IGCSE has usually not met reflective journals, independent research or criterion-referenced marking. See [from IGCSE to IB DP](/blog/from-igcse-mathematics-to-ib-dp-building-a-strong-foundation/) for the maths side of that shift; for the core, add an extra month of lead time for the EE.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I start the Extended Essay in the IB Diploma?

Start in the second term of DP1 by choosing a subject and supervisor, and have the research question settled by the end of DP1. Reading and an outline follow in the third term, and a full draft is best written over the summer break. Starting only in DP2 is possible but compresses a long process into the busiest months.

### How many hours does the Extended Essay actually take?

The guidance is roughly 40 hours of work for an essay of up to 4,000 words. In practice the elapsed time is much longer because research, drafting and revision cannot be done in one sitting. Plan for the hours to be spread across about nine months, with the heaviest block in the summer break.

### Can I do CAS in the last year only?

You can start late, but it is risky. CAS expects sustained engagement and honest reflection over time, and a compressed record is easy to spot. Starting in the first month of DP1 with one weekly activity is far lighter than trying to build eighteen months of evidence in the final terms.

### Do CAS, EE and TOK affect my IB score?

Yes for two of them. TOK and the EE are graded A to E and combine for up to 3 diploma points, and an E in either is a failing condition. CAS is not graded, but it must be completed for the Diploma to be awarded. So the core can add points, but it can also remove the Diploma if neglected.

### What is the biggest mistake students make with the core timeline?

Waiting for a deadline to feel close before starting the EE. Because the core has little weekly pull compared with subject lessons, the work drifts until DP2 term one, where it collides with IAs, applications and mocks. Fixing the research question in DP1 and drafting in the summer removes most of the risk.

### Should my EE and my IA be in the same subject?

It can help, since research design and evaluation skills transfer, but the pieces must be entirely separate, with no reused text. The risk is workload: two pieces in one subject can peak together. If you choose the same subject, stagger your milestones and tell both teachers.

### What if my EE supervisor is slow to reply?

Set the next meeting before leaving the current one, and email with a specific question and a date. If feedback is still late, tell your DP coordinator early. Your school has a process for this, and a late supervisor should not become a late essay. Keep records of the sessions you did attend.

### Is it worth getting a tutor for the Extended Essay or TOK?

Only if the support fits a real gap. A tutor can teach research skills, test your reasoning and help you read the criteria, but cannot write or edit your submission. If your school's supervision is strong, you may not need one. If you are stuck on structure or argument, a regular external session can help.

### Can I finish the TOK essay before my mocks?

Usually you should, or be close. The essay title is released at a fixed point in the session and schools set their own internal deadline. Pre-build the skills and your example file so the essay is a defined task, not a research project, and aim to have the plan and most of the draft finished before mocks begin.

### What if I have already fallen behind in DP2?

Tell your supervisor and coordinator now, list every deadline with its true date, and plan backwards from the one that cannot move. Pause optional commitments until the EE has a draft. Most late starts are recoverable if you act in weeks, not months. If you want help pacing the recovery, book a free consultation.
