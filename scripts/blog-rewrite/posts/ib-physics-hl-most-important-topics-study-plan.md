A good IB Physics HL study plan does three things: it follows the current syllabus themes, it spends the most time on the topics that feed the most questions, and it turns every practice paper into a list of specific errors to fix. Students who only reread notes tend to feel prepared and then meet a question they have never seen. Students who work in a cycle of learn, practise, analyse and revisit rarely do.

This guide is for International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma Programme (DP) students taking Physics at Higher Level (HL), and for parents who want to understand what a sensible plan looks like. It covers how the course is structured, which topics deserve priority and why, how to study in DP1 and DP2, a six-week final revision schedule, how to use past papers, and the habits that separate steady 6s and 7s from students who plateau. It is written for the current syllabus, first assessed in 2025; always check the subject guide your school issues, because details change.

No plan is magic, and no tutor can guarantee a grade. What a plan can do is make your effort count. Read the sections you need, and use the headings to jump around.

## What Does the IB Physics HL Course Look Like?

IB Physics HL is a two-year course with 240 recommended teaching hours, compared with 150 for Standard Level (SL). It is assessed through external exams and an internal assessment (IA). Under the syllabus first assessed in 2025, Paper 1 has two parts, 1A multiple choice and 1B data-based questions, and Paper 2 has short-answer and extended-response questions. The IA is a scientific investigation worth 20%, marked on four criteria of 6 marks each: Research design, Data analysis, Conclusion and Evaluation. HL and SL share the structure of the assessment. HL adds Additional Higher Level (AHL) content.

That means about 80% of the grade comes from examinations. The IA is the 20% you control entirely, and it is also where many students lose easy marks through poor planning.

:::stats IB Physics HL at a glance
240 | Recommended teaching hours at HL
20% | Internal assessment (scientific investigation)
4 | IA criteria, 6 marks each
1-7 | Final subject grade scale
:::

The syllabus is organised into five themes, labelled A to E. Each mixes content from classical and modern physics, and each includes AHL material that only HL students study.

:::bars The five themes at a glance (equal bars; weightings are not official)
A Space, time and motion | 20 | Mechanics
B The particulate nature of matter | 20 | Thermal and circuits
C Wave behaviour | 20 | Waves
D Fields | 20 | Gravity, electric, magnetic
E Nuclear and quantum physics | 20 | Atoms and nuclei
The IB does not publish theme-by-theme mark weightings, so the bars are equal on purpose. Priority comes from how topics connect, not from official percentages.
:::

If you remember one thing from this section, remember this: the themes are not isolated boxes. Energy, fields and waves keep returning, and a Paper 2 question can borrow from two themes at once. A study plan that treats each theme as a sealed unit will leave gaps.

## Which Topics Matter Most in IB Physics HL?

The topics that matter most are the ones that sit underneath many others: kinematics and forces in Theme A, energy and circuits in Theme B, wave properties in Theme C, and fields in Theme D. If these are weak, the rest of the course wobbles. Theme E (nuclear and quantum) is smaller and easier to revise quickly, but it is where students lose marks to unfamiliar definitions.

The IB does not publish how many marks each theme carries, so the ranking below is a judgement about revision payoff, not an official weighting. It is based on how often a topic is a prerequisite for others and how much time students typically need to master it.

| Priority | Topic cluster | Why it repays study | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Highest | Kinematics, forces, momentum, work and energy (A) | Underlies almost everything else | Mixing vector and scalar; sign errors |
| Highest | Fields: gravitational, electric, magnetic (D) | Heavy at HL; links to circular motion and energy | Forgetting direction; treating fields as formulas only |
| High | Waves, superposition, standing waves, diffraction (C) | Many descriptive and quantitative questions | Vague explanations; mixing interference conditions |
| High | Thermal physics and gas laws (B) | Calculation and explanation both tested | Using Celsius not kelvin |
| High | Current, circuits, resistance (B) | Data questions and the IA draw on it | Series and parallel confusion |
| Medium | Nuclear: radioactive decay, fission, fusion (E) | Mostly recall and calculation | Half-life graphs; binding energy sign |
| Medium | Quantum and atomic structure (E) | Conceptual; short answers | Describing, not explaining |
| AHL | Rigid body mechanics, relativity, thermodynamics, induction | HL only; frequently the hardest part | Skipping because it looks unfamiliar |

Use this as a starting point and adjust to your own errors. A student whose mocks keep dropping marks on circular motion has a Theme A and D problem regardless of the table.

### Theme A: Space, time and motion

This is the engine of the course. Kinematics (describing motion with graphs and equations), forces and momentum (Newton's laws, impulse, collisions), and work, energy and power are used throughout the other themes. At HL you add rigid body mechanics (torque, moment of inertia, angular motion) and relativity.

Priority skills: drawing and using free-body diagrams, resolving vectors, reading gradients and areas from motion graphs, applying conservation of momentum and energy to collisions, and circular motion with centripetal force. Students often learn each formula and still fail to choose between them. The remedy is to identify the physical principle first and the equation second.

### Theme B: The particulate nature of matter

This theme covers thermal energy transfers, the greenhouse effect, gas laws, thermodynamics (AHL) and current and circuits. It is a mix of conceptual and calculation-heavy material. Students tend to find circuits manageable and thermodynamics (entropy, the first and second laws, cycles on pressure-volume graphs) much harder.

Priority skills: converting to kelvin, interpreting pressure-volume graphs, relating microscopic and macroscopic descriptions of a gas, solving circuits with internal resistance, and using the correct combination rules.

### Theme C: Wave behaviour

Simple harmonic motion, the wave model, wave phenomena (reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference), standing waves and resonance, and the Doppler effect. Many marks here are for explanation. "Explain why the pattern changes" asks for the condition for constructive or destructive interference in terms of path difference, not the word "interference" alone.

Priority skills: relating phase and path difference, drawing and labelling wave diagrams, applying the diffraction and double-slit relationships, and describing standing waves in strings and pipes.

### Theme D: Fields

Gravitational fields, electric and magnetic fields, motion in electromagnetic fields, and induction (AHL). This is the theme that most separates HL from SL. Fields link back to energy and circular motion, so a weak Theme A shows up here.

Priority skills: field lines and potential, the relationship between force, field strength and potential energy, orbital motion, charged particles in magnetic fields, and Faraday's and Lenz's laws. Explain direction every time. Many marks go on the sign.

### Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physics

Structure of the atom, quantum physics, radioactive decay, fission, and fusion and stars. Recall matters, so definitions need to be exact. Unfamiliar contexts, such as a medical isotope or a stellar process, test the same ideas in new wording.

Priority skills: balancing decay equations, interpreting decay curves and half-life, mass-energy relationships in nuclear reactions, and energy levels in atoms.

## How Should You Structure an IB Physics HL Study Plan?

Structure the plan in three layers: a weekly routine that never changes, a topic cycle that moves through the themes, and a calendar of checkpoints tied to your school's tests and mocks. The weekly routine builds habit, the topic cycle ensures coverage, and the checkpoints tell you whether the plan is working.

1. Fix the weekly routine first: three or four short sessions of 45 to 60 minutes, plus one longer block for a timed set.
2. Assign each session a job: concept review, topic questions, harder HL problems, or error review. Never "revise physics".
3. Move through the themes in the order your school teaches, but revisit earlier themes on a rotating basis each week.
4. Keep a single error log, in a notebook or document, where every lost mark is recorded with its cause.
5. At the end of each month, sit a mixed timed set and compare results with the last one.
6. Use the log, not the calendar, to decide what to study next.

A plan that lives in your head will fail. Put it on paper, share it with your teacher or tutor, and change it whenever the evidence says to.

## What Should You Do in DP1 for Physics HL?

In DP1 the aim is understanding, not exam speed. The best DP1 plan keeps up with the class, practises problems from each unit within a week of learning it, and reviews earlier units briefly every week so they do not decay.

A typical week might look like this. After a class on, say, momentum, do 10 to 15 questions the same week, mixing easy and hard. At the weekend, spend 30 minutes revisiting last month's topic with five questions chosen at random. Record every error in the log. Before each unit test, spend two sessions on mixed questions, not rereading.

:::timeline A DP1 physics term
Weeks 1 to 3 | Learn the unit; ten questions within a week of each lesson
Weeks 4 to 6 | Add harder HL questions; start the error log properly
Week 7 | Mixed set covering the whole unit, timed
Week 8 | Review the set; fix the three most common errors
Weeks 9 to 12 | Next unit, with a weekly five-question review of the last one
:::

The thing most DP1 students underestimate is the gap between understanding a worked solution and producing one. Cover the solution, attempt the question, then check. If you look at the answer first, you are practising recognition, which the exam does not test.

DP1 is also the time to start thinking about the IA. Pick a question you can measure, in a topic you understand, with equipment you can access. A modest, well-controlled investigation scores better than an ambitious, muddled one. The detailed guidance on the investigation is in [the complete guide to IB Internal Assessment with examples](/blog/complete-guide-to-ib-internal-assessment-ia-with-examples/).

## What Should You Do in DP2 for Physics HL?

In DP2 the course goes faster and the stakes rise. The plan shifts from learning new content to consolidating it, and from topic questions to mixed and timed questions. The IA also needs time, and many students lose weeks in the autumn term because they underestimate it.

A sensible DP2 pattern: finish new content in the first half of the year while keeping a weekly mixed set; after the IA is submitted, move to paper-style practice; after your school's mock, use the results to rebuild the plan around the weak themes.

| Stage of DP2 | Main focus | Weekly rhythm |
|---|---|---|
| Autumn term | Finish content, complete the IA | 3 topic sessions, 1 mixed set, IA work |
| After the IA | Consolidate themes | 2 topic sessions, 1 timed paper section, error review |
| Mock preparation | Paper practice | 1 timed section per week, 2 review sessions |
| After the mock | Targeted repair | Plan built from the mock error log |
| Final weeks | Full papers and recall | See the six-week schedule below |

If you are balancing physics with other HL subjects, the Extended Essay and Theory of Knowledge, protect the weekly routine above everything else. Consistency beats occasional heroics. [The IB DP core planning timeline](/blog/ib-dp-core-planning-timeline/) shows how the demands of the core fall across the two years.

## What Is a Good Six-Week IB Physics HL Study Plan Before the Exams?

A good six-week IB Physics HL study plan moves from gap-finding to mixed practice to full timed papers: weeks one and two repair the weakest themes, weeks three and four rotate through all five themes with questions only, week five mixes everything in timed sections, and week six sits full papers under real conditions. Every week ends with an error review.

This schedule assumes about 8 to 10 hours of physics a week, alongside other subjects. Scale it up or down to your situation.

:::timeline Final six weeks of IB Physics HL revision
Week 1 | Diagnose with a full past paper; rank themes by lost marks
Week 2 | Repair the two weakest themes with topic questions
Week 3 | Mechanics and fields: mixed questions, formula recall
Week 4 | Waves, thermal and circuits: mixed questions, definitions
Week 5 | Nuclear and quantum plus mixed timed sections across all themes
Week 6 | Full timed papers, error log review, light recall only the day before
:::

### Week by week in more detail

**Week 1: diagnose.** Sit a full past paper or a mock under timed conditions. Mark it strictly against the markscheme. Sort every lost mark into one of four causes (see the error categories later in this guide). Then rank the themes by lost marks. This is the only week where you do not choose a topic in advance.

**Week 2: repair.** Take the two themes at the top of your list. For each, relearn the three or four ideas that feed most questions, then do questions in blocks of ten, checking after each block. Do not move on until you can explain a topic aloud without notes.

**Week 3: mechanics and fields.** These are the highest-priority clusters. Mix questions from Themes A and D, because the exam will. Practise circular motion with forces, orbital energy, and charged particles in fields. Rehearse the equations that must be recalled and the ones given in the data booklet, so you know which is which.

**Week 4: waves, thermal, circuits.** Focus on explanation. For each question, write the answer as a short, logically ordered argument with the key physical terms. Practise sketching graphs and wave diagrams quickly. Do circuit questions with internal resistance and non-ohmic components.

**Week 5: nuclear and quantum, then mixed sections.** Theme E needs precise recall: definitions, decay equations, energy level transitions. Spend the first half of the week on it, and the second half on timed mixed sections of 30 to 40 minutes, no notes.

**Week 6: full papers.** Sit at least two full papers in realistic conditions: same time of day as the real exam, same break pattern, no phone. After each, update the error log and spend twice as long reviewing as sitting. The final two days should be light: recall sheets, a few definitions, early sleep.

## How to Use Past Papers Without Wasting Them

Use past papers as a diagnostic tool, not a scoreboard. The number at the top matters less than the reasons behind the lost marks. Papers are also finite, so do not burn through them all in the first month.

A good routine for each paper:

1. Attempt it under timed conditions with only what the exam allows.
2. Mark it strictly with the official markscheme, giving no benefit of the doubt.
3. For every lost mark, write one line in the error log: the question, the cause and the fix.
4. Redo the question the next day without looking at the solution.
5. A week later, redo the three questions you got most wrong.

Past papers, markschemes and subject reports come through your school or the IB's official channels. Subject reports are underrated: they describe in plain words what candidates did well and badly in a session, and the same mistakes recur year after year.

Because the syllabus changed for first assessment in 2025, be careful with older papers. Content and question formats differ. Use older papers for topic practice where the content still matches, and check with your teacher which questions are still relevant. Practice from your school's mock and the IB's sample material for the current structure will be closer to what you will meet.

## The Four Types of Error and How to Fix Each

Every lost mark in physics belongs to one of four categories, and each needs a different cure. Sorting errors honestly is the single most useful habit in the course.

| Error type | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Concept error | You did not understand the physics | Relearn the idea from a different source; explain it aloud; then do easy questions before hard ones |
| Application error | You knew the concept but could not use it in the new context | Practise unfamiliar contexts; start every question by naming the principle |
| Calculation error | Correct method, wrong arithmetic, units or significant figures | Slow down; write units on every line; check order of magnitude |
| Exam technique error | You misread the question, skipped working or misunderstood a command term | Underline command terms; show every step; allocate time per mark |

Most students overestimate concept errors and underestimate technique errors. If a third of your lost marks come from misreading questions, more content revision will not help. [IB command terms explained with real examples](/blog/ib-command-terms-explained-with-real-examples/) shows how the depth required changes between "state", "describe", "explain" and "evaluate".

## How to Get Better at Numerical Problems

Better numerical problem-solving comes from using the same method every time until it is automatic. The method below works for most Paper 2 calculations, and it also reduces avoidable errors because each step catches a different type of mistake.

1. Read the question twice and underline what is asked.
2. Write down the known quantities with units.
3. Write down what you need to find.
4. Draw a diagram if one would help, and label it.
5. Name the physical principle (conservation of energy, Newton's second law, Gauss-style field reasoning, and so on).
6. Choose the equation from your recall or the data booklet.
7. Rearrange the equation symbolically before substituting numbers.
8. Substitute values with units and calculate.
9. Check whether the answer is reasonable in size and sign.
10. State the answer with the right units and sensible significant figures.

Step 7 is the one most students skip, and it is the one that prevents the most errors. Symbolic rearrangement also gives you partial credit when a number is wrong, because the method is visible. In HL questions, where several steps chain together, a clean symbolic line can rescue marks that a single arithmetic slip would lose.

## Data, Graphs and Experimental Skills

The practical side of the course is not just the IA. Paper 1B and parts of Paper 2 test data-based questions, and the skills carry directly into the investigation. Many students treat these as a separate topic. In reality they are marks available for habit, not for memory.

Make sure you can do the following without hesitation:

- Identify independent, dependent and control variables, and state how each is measured.
- Estimate and propagate uncertainties, including percentage uncertainty in a product or a power.
- Plot, linearise and read graphs; find gradients and intercepts and say what they represent.
- Draw error bars and judge the best-fit and worst-fit lines.
- Evaluate a method: say what limited the result, how large the effect is, and what change would reduce it.

Practise explaining how an experiment could be improved, not just that errors exist. "Human error" is not an acceptable evaluation; "the stopwatch reaction time of about 0.2 seconds is a large fraction of a 1.5 second fall time, so a light gate would reduce the uncertainty" is. The IA's Data analysis and Evaluation criteria reward exactly this kind of specific reasoning.

For the investigation, remember that the IB's academic integrity rules apply: the work is your own, a teacher can give feedback on one draft, and no tutor or parent may write or edit it. A tutor can teach you how to plan, how to handle uncertainty and how to read your own criteria, but the report must be yours. The topics and common pitfalls are covered in [the IB Internal Assessment guide](/blog/ib-internal-assessment-guide/).

## A Daily Routine That Works

A workable daily routine is short and specific. Long, vague sessions produce the feeling of work without the substance. This is a model you can adapt, for a day when you have an hour and a quarter.

:::steps A focused 75-minute physics session
Recall | 15 minutes: definitions and equations, covered
Practise | 30 minutes: topic questions from the current theme
Stretch | 20 minutes: harder HL problems, no looking up
Review | 10 minutes: log every error with its cause
:::

On days with more time, extend the stretch block, not the rereading. Reread notes only to fix a specific gap that your error log has exposed.

On days with less time, do the recall and review blocks and skip the rest. A short, honest session beats a long distracted one.

## Common Mistakes in IB Physics HL Revision

These patterns appear again and again in students who work hard and still plateau.

- **Rereading instead of retrieving.** Notes feel familiar, which feels like learning. Test yourself with a blank page.
- **Memorising equations without conditions.** Know when an equation applies and when it does not, such as constant acceleration, ideal gases and small angles.
- **Skipping AHL material.** HL papers contain it, and it is often where the highest marks are separated.
- **Ignoring units and directions.** Many marks go on sign and unit. Treat both as part of the answer.
- **Doing only the questions you like.** Your weakest theme is where the marks are.
- **Leaving the IA until it is too late.** It is 20% of the grade and the one component you can fully prepare.
- **Practising without reviewing.** A paper unmarked and unanalysed adds almost nothing.
- **Studying only at the last minute.** Physics rewards spaced repetition. Ten short sessions beat two long ones.

## When Does an IB Physics HL Student Need Extra Help?

A student needs extra help when the same errors keep returning despite regular practice, when mock scores are inconsistent, or when HL questions feel far harder than classroom exercises. That pattern usually signals a missing foundation or a method problem that is hard to see alone.

Signs it may be time:

- Several topics remain weak after a full revision cycle.
- You understand the lesson but cannot start an unfamiliar question.
- Past-paper scores swing widely between similar papers.
- You cannot say what you got wrong or why.
- Your study time is high but your marks are flat.

What good support looks like: a diagnostic that sorts errors, a plan aimed at the two or three biggest gaps, practice with unfamiliar questions, and feedback on written explanations. ## How Does Physics HL Fit With Your Other Subjects?

Physics HL often sits alongside Mathematics and another science or economics, and the combination shapes how much time you have. The most common pairing is Physics HL with Mathematics Analysis and Approaches (AA) HL, because the maths supports the physics directly: calculus for kinematics, vectors for forces and fields, and algebra throughout. If your maths is shaky, your physics will wobble, and the other way round.

Plan your weekly time with this in mind. Do not give physics all your effort in the weeks before a physics test and starve the others, because the next test will arrive soon after. Aim for a steady allocation and flex it by a few hours rather than by days. If the maths is the weak link, fix it first, since it improves both subjects. ## How Do You Revise in the Last Month?

In the last month, replace new content with retrieval, mixed practice and timed papers. The shape of the final month is covered in [how toppers study in the last 30 days before IB exams](/blog/ib-exam-strategy-how-toppers-study-in-the-last-30-days/), and for physics the main adjustments are these.

Keep a one-page sheet for each theme listing the equations you must recall, the definitions you must be able to state exactly, and the three or four errors you personally make. Review the sheets daily. Sit papers on the same schedule as the real exam. Sleep properly, because sleep consolidates problem-solving skills better than another late hour of rereading.

On exam day, read each question fully, underline command terms, and answer the question asked. If a question looks unfamiliar, break it into smaller parts. Many difficult physics questions are familiar principles in unfamiliar clothes. Leave a hard question after a fair attempt and return to it. Show working for every calculation so that partial credit is available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the most important topics in IB Physics HL?

The most important topics are mechanics (kinematics, forces, momentum, energy), fields, waves and circuits, because they underlie much of the rest of the course. Theme E, nuclear and quantum physics, is smaller but needs exact recall. The IB does not publish theme weightings, so prioritise by your own error log and by what your school teaches, and always follow the current subject guide.

### How many hours a week should I study IB Physics HL?

There is no fixed number, but most students do well with about four to six hours a week outside class in DP1 and six to ten in DP2, rising before mocks. Quality matters more than totals: a focused hour with questions and an error review beats three hours of rereading. Adjust to your other subjects and keep the routine steady.

### Is IB Physics HL harder than Chemistry HL or Biology HL?

It depends on the student. Physics HL leans on mathematics and abstract reasoning; Chemistry HL on calculations and mechanisms; Biology HL on content volume and extended responses. A student strong in maths often finds physics natural, while one who prefers memory and description may find biology easier. Judge by your strengths and by the university course you want.

### How do I get a 7 in IB Physics HL?

A 7 comes from strong conceptual understanding, accurate calculations, clean written explanations and good exam technique, built over two years. No plan guarantees it. The habits that most reliably help are working past papers under timed conditions, keeping an honest error log, mastering AHL content, and taking the IA seriously. Start early and revisit weak themes repeatedly.

### Should I memorise the formulas or use the data booklet?

Learn which formulas are in the data booklet and which are not, and be fluent with the booklet's layout. Memorising is not enough, because the exam tests whether you can choose and apply an equation. Understand where each comes from, what its conditions are and what each symbol means. Then the booklet becomes a quick check, not a crutch.

### How should I use past papers for the new syllabus?

Use papers for the current syllabus where available, and older ones only for topics whose content still matches. Always mark with the official markscheme and log errors by cause. Be careful with older Paper 1 and Paper 2 formats, which differ from the structure first assessed in 2025. Ask your teacher which older questions remain relevant, and use the school's mocks heavily.

### What should I do if I am weak in AHL topics?

Treat AHL as part of the course, not an optional extra. Start with the first principles of the topic, such as torque or induction, and build up with simple questions before attempting HL ones. Ask your teacher for clarifying examples, and write short explanations in your own words. AHL material is often where papers separate the highest grades, so steady work pays off.

### How do I improve in Paper 1B data questions?

Practise reading graphs, tables and experimental setups quickly, then answering in terms of the physics. Learn to propagate uncertainty, linearise relationships and judge a method's limits. The same skills help the IA. Use topic-based data questions first, then timed sections. Review each one by asking what the data actually shows, rather than what you expected it to show.

### When should I start working on my Physics IA?

Start thinking about it in the second half of DP1 and draft the plan before the summer, so that DP2 begins with data collection. Choose a question you can measure well with equipment you can access, and plan the analysis before the experiment. A simple, well-controlled investigation usually beats an ambitious one. The report must be your own work.

### Is it worth getting a tutor for IB Physics HL?

It is worth it when a specific problem persists, such as weak problem-solving, poor explanations or a lack of structure, and when the tutor can diagnose and plan, not only explain. It is not needed by every student. Try a trial session and judge by the diagnosis, the plan and the questions asked. A good tutor also helps you become independent.

### What if my physics marks do not improve after a month of revision?

Check how you are revising before assuming you need more hours. If you mostly reread, switch to retrieval and timed questions. Review your error log to see whether concept, application, calculation or technique errors dominate, and target that one. If the pattern is unclear after another few weeks, ask your teacher or a tutor to review your scripts with you.
