MYP maths problem solving is the skill of facing a question you have never seen and making progress anyway: working out what is being asked, deciding which mathematics applies, doing it, and checking that the answer makes sense in the situation. In the International Baccalaureate (IB) Middle Years Programme (MYP), this skill is assessed most directly in Criterion D, applying mathematics in real-life contexts, and it also decides how well a student does in the other three criteria.

This guide is for parents and students in MYP 3, 4 and 5, mostly in Gurgaon and Gurugram, and for anyone teaching them. I teach MYP and Diploma Programme (DP) mathematics as IB Gram's IB Mathematics mentor, and the habits described below come from watching what separates students who freeze on unfamiliar tasks from those who start.

It deliberately stays in one lane. The reasoning work of Criterion B, which is investigating patterns, generalising and proving, is covered in [IB MYP Mathematics reasoning skills](/blog/ib-myp-mathematics-problem-solving-reasoning-skills/). Here the focus is real-life tasks and modelling, a routine you can use on any unfamiliar problem, and the way a tutor should gradually take support away until the student no longer needs it.

## What Does MYP Maths Problem Solving Actually Mean?

In the MYP, problem solving means taking a situation that is not already written as a maths question, deciding what to calculate, calculating it, and judging whether the result is reasonable. It is different from solving an equation you are handed, because the first and last steps, setting up and checking, are the student's job.

A routine question says "Solve 3x + 7 = 25." A problem-solving task says that a school is planning a sports day, gives a table of costs, a budget and a list of constraints, and asks which option is best and why. The mathematics inside may be no harder than the equation. The difficulty is that nobody has told the student which equation to write.

That difference explains a pattern I see in almost every cohort. A student can score well in school tests that follow the textbook chapter and then lose most of the marks on a real-life task, not because the maths is beyond them but because the task does not announce its method. The student looks for a familiar template, finds none and either stops or guesses.

The MYP assesses each subject on four criteria, each scored 0 to 8, and the total out of 32 converts to a final grade of 1 to 7 through grade boundaries. In mathematics the criteria are A Knowing and understanding, B Investigating patterns, C Communicating, and D Applying mathematics in real-life contexts. A student whose criterion A is strong and criterion D is weak has learned the content and not yet learned to use it. Closing that gap is a different job from learning more content, and it is the job of problem solving.

## Why Do Students Freeze on Unfamiliar Questions?

Students freeze because they have learned to recognise question types and not to analyse situations. When a question does not match a type they know, they have no process to fall back on, and the absence feels like an inability.

Five specific causes appear again and again.

**Procedural dependence.** The student has linked each chapter to a formula and looks for the chapter first. When the question hides its chapter, the student is lost.

**Reading failure.** The student may know the maths and still misread the situation, missing a unit, a constraint or what the final question is.

**Fragmented knowledge.** The student knows ratios, graphs and equations as separate boxes and has not practised moving between them. Real-life tasks mix them.

**Fear of being wrong.** Many capable students will not write anything down unless they know it is right. That makes every unfamiliar problem a blank page.

**No transfer practice.** The student has done fifty questions on one concept in one context and none on the same concept in another.

Each has a different remedy, which is why handing out more worksheets rarely fixes it. The first step is always diagnosis: watching the student attempt a problem and noting where the process stops. ## What Does Criterion D Ask For?

Criterion D asks the student to apply mathematics to an authentic situation: to work out which elements of the situation matter, to choose and use a suitable mathematical strategy, to reach a solution, and to justify how accurate it is and whether it makes sense in context. The exact wording of the strands is in the current MYP mathematics guide and in your school's task-specific clarifications, so confirm those with the teacher.

In broad terms, there are five moves inside every D-style task.

1. **Identify** what in the situation is mathematical and what is decoration.
2. **Choose** a strategy: a table, a graph, an equation, a ratio, a diagram, a model.
3. **Apply** the strategy correctly to get a result.
4. **Check** the degree of accuracy: rounding, units, assumptions.
5. **Justify** that the result makes sense in the real situation, and say what its limits are.

Students usually do the third move well, the first and second moderately, and the fourth and fifth poorly or not at all. That is where marks go missing. A student who computes 17.3 buses and writes "17.3 buses" has applied mathematics but has not checked whether it makes sense in the situation, and a good tutor will ask "can you hire 0.3 of a bus?"

A task is typically set around a context: a school event, a business, a sports measurement, an environmental or health question, a design problem. The context matters because it supplies constraints that the student must notice. The mathematics might be linear relationships, rates, proportional reasoning, area and volume, simple exponential growth, statistics or trigonometry, depending on the year and school. Schools often split MYP 4 and 5 mathematics into standard and extended, and extended covers additional topics that prepare a student for DP Mathematics Analysis and Approaches.

## What Is a Reliable Routine for Any Unfamiliar Problem?

A reliable routine is a short, fixed sequence of moves that the student can run on any problem, so that "I don't know where to start" is replaced by "I know the first move." The routine below is the one I teach, and it works across MYP years and carries into DP.

1. **Read twice, then restate.** Read the problem once for the story and once for the numbers. Restate the question in your own words, in one sentence, starting with "I need to find...".
2. **List what is given and what is missing.** Write quantities with units. Mark anything that looks relevant but has no number.
3. **Draw or tabulate.** Make a sketch, a table or a graph outline. If you cannot draw it, you may not understand it yet.
4. **Link to a relationship.** Ask what connects the quantities: a rate, a ratio, a formula, a pattern. Name it before writing any equation.
5. **Try a small case.** Put in easy numbers to see how the relationship behaves. This catches misunderstandings before they cost marks.
6. **Solve.** Carry out the mathematics, showing steps.
7. **Check against the situation.** Is the answer the right size, the right unit, the right kind of number? What assumption did you make?
8. **Say it in a sentence.** Write the conclusion in the language of the problem, not just a number.

The routine looks long on paper. In practice, a practised student runs steps one to five in under two minutes, and those two minutes are where unfamiliar problems stop being unfamiliar. The routine also builds the written evidence that Criterion C rewards, which is why the criteria reinforce one another. For a closer look at all four, see [MYP maths criteria A, B, C and D explained](/blog/ib-criterion-a-b-c-d-explained-myp-mathematics-gurgaon/) and, for the parent's view of the report, [MYP maths criteria explained for parents](/blog/ib-myp-maths-criteria-a-b-c-d-explained-for-parents/).

Restate | Say what you must find
List | Given, missing, units
Draw | Sketch, table or graph
Link | Name the relationship
Try small | Test with easy numbers
Solve and check | Work, then test sense
:::

## How Does the Routine Work on a Real Task?

Here is a worked example of the kind that appears in MYP 4 and 5, with the thinking shown. The numbers are invented for illustration, and no currency is attached because the structure is what matters.

**The task.** Two taxi companies serve a school trip. Company A charges a fixed fee of 60 plus 8 per kilometre. Company B charges a fixed fee of 20 plus 12 per kilometre. The school wants the cheaper option for a trip of a given length. Which company should it choose, and does the answer depend on the distance?

**Restate.** I need to find which company is cheaper and whether this changes with distance.

**List.** Given: two fixed fees and two rates. Missing: the distance. Unit: kilometres and money.

**Draw.** Two straight lines on one graph, cost against distance. A starts higher, B climbs faster. They will cross.

**Link.** Each cost is a linear model: cost = fixed fee + rate x distance. So A: C = 60 + 8d. B: C = 20 + 12d.

**Try small.** At d = 0, A is 60 and B is 20, so B is cheaper. At d = 20, A is 220 and B is 260, so A is cheaper. The order flips somewhere between.

**Solve.** Set the costs equal: 60 + 8d = 20 + 12d, so 40 = 4d and d = 10. At 10 km the companies cost the same, 140 each.

**Check.** Test d = 5: A is 100, B is 80, so B is cheaper. Test d = 15: A is 180, B is 200, so A is cheaper. The crossing makes sense.

**Say it.** For trips shorter than 10 km, Company B is cheaper. At 10 km, they cost the same. For longer trips, Company A is cheaper. If the trip is exactly 10 km, other factors such as comfort or availability should decide. This model assumes the fees and rates do not change with time of day or traffic, which a real quote might.

Notice what earned the marks. The algebra was a few lines. The rest was interpretation: noticing that the answer depends on distance, testing either side of the crossing, and stating the assumption. A student who only solved the equation would produce "d = 10" and be unsure what it meant.

## How Do You Teach Modelling, Not Just Calculation?

Modelling means deciding which parts of a messy situation to keep, writing a mathematical description of them, using it, and then asking how far to trust it. Teach it by making the student build the model, not receive it.

A good modelling session has four stages: simplify the situation, represent it mathematically, use the representation, and evaluate the result. The last stage is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that moves Criterion D from a middle band to a top band.

Take a second example: a town's population is 20,000 and grows by 3% per year. A student asked "how large will it be in ten years?" can multiply 20,000 by 1.03 ten times, or use 20,000 x 1.03^10, which is about 26,878. That is the calculation. The modelling questions are: what assumption makes the growth rate constant? What would change if the growth rate fell, or if people moved out? Is "26,878" a sensible level of precision, or should it be rounded to the nearest hundred? Which of these answers a parent would want to hear in a planning meeting?

Teach students to say these things out loud. A session in which the tutor asks "what are we assuming?" and "how sure are we?" at the end of every task builds a habit that carries through the DP, where the Internal Assessment (IA) exploration rewards exactly this sort of reflection on a model's limits.

## Which Real-Life Contexts Are Worth Practising?

The most useful contexts are those where a student can feel the constraint. A student who has never thought about how a phone plan works will not enjoy a phone-plan problem; a student who plays cricket may engage immediately with a problem about run rates. Variety matters more than any single context.

A sensible practice rotation covers five families of situation.

| Context family | Typical mathematics | The decision the student must make |
|---|---|---|
| Money and plans | Linear models, percentages, break-even | Which option is cheaper, and when? |
| Growth and decay | Percentage change, exponential models | How reliable is the prediction? |
| Measurement and design | Area, volume, scale, trigonometry | Which dimensions meet the constraints? |
| Data and chance | Averages, spread, probability | What does the data really support? |
| Rates and travel | Speed, ratio, unit conversion | Which units and assumptions matter? |

The mix should follow the student's weaker area. A student who handles money problems but struggles with measurement will gain more from three measurement tasks than from a fourth money one. The year-by-year topic map, including how standard and extended differ, is in [MYP 4 and MYP 5 mathematics tutoring](/blog/ib-myp-4-myp-5-mathematics-tutoring-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/).

## What Does "Withdrawing Scaffolding" Mean in Practice?

Withdrawing scaffolding means giving the student a lot of structure at the start of a skill and removing it in planned steps until the student does everything alone. It is the difference between teaching a student to solve problems and solving problems in front of a student.

A tutor who solves every problem, however clearly, produces a student who can follow solutions and cannot generate them. The aim is the reverse: each week, the tutor does a little less. I use five levels.

| Level | What the tutor does | What the student does |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Model | Solves a problem aloud, naming each move | Watches and answers questions |
| 2. Guide | Asks which move comes next | Chooses the move and does it |
| 3. Cue | Gives a one-line cue card of the routine | Runs the routine, asks if stuck |
| 4. Check | Says nothing until the student finishes | Solves alone, then explains choices |
| 5. Transfer | Presents an unfamiliar context | Solves alone and critiques the model |

A student moves up when two consecutive tasks at a level go smoothly. They move back down, without shame, when a new kind of problem breaks the routine. The point is not a fixed timetable but a visible direction: over a term, the tutor's talk time should fall and the student's should rise.

:::timeline A term of fading support
Weeks 1-2 | Model and guide; routine named
Weeks 3-4 | Guide and cue; student leads setup
Weeks 5-8 | Cue and check; new contexts added
Weeks 9-10 | Check and transfer; tutor mostly silent
Weeks 11-12 | Transfer tasks; review what still needs help
:::

If the tutor is still at level 1 or 2 after two months, the arrangement is producing dependence. A parent can tell by asking the student to describe how they started the last problem: a student who says "I restated the question, drew a table and tried small numbers" is running the routine; one who says "sir showed me" is not.

## How Does a Tutor Handle a Student Who Asks "Which Formula?"

A tutor handles it by never answering directly and by turning the question into the first step of the routine. The student's request for a formula is a request to skip the thinking, and giving the formula teaches the student to ask again next time.

Consider a typical MYP 4 student who, on every new problem, asks "which formula do I use?" The habit looks harmless, and it is the main obstacle to independent work. The tutor responds with questions: what is the problem describing? What quantities do we have? What might connect them? Can you draw it? Within a few sessions, the student begins to ask these questions silently, and the request for a formula fades.

A related habit is the student who will not write anything until sure. Here the tutor lowers the stakes: "write down anything you are sure of, even one number." A line on the page makes the next line easier, and a student who writes the given information has already completed step two of the routine. Over time, the student learns that writing is how you find out what you know, not a test of whether you already know it.

## How Does Criterion D Connect to the Other Three?

Problem solving is not a separate subject but the place where A, B, C and D meet. Strength in one rarely compensates for a missing link in another.

Criterion A supplies the content knowledge, such as how to rearrange a formula or interpret a gradient. Without it, the student cannot carry out step six of the routine. Criterion B supplies the habit of noticing structure, which helps with steps three, four and five. Criterion C supplies the communication, which is the evidence in step eight. Criterion D supplies the situation and the justification.

This is why a student often shows a pattern like A 7, B 5, C 4, D 4: strong content, weaker pattern use, and weak writing and application. A tutor can read the pattern from a report and aim the work. [How can an IB MYP maths student move from a 5 to a 6 or 7](/blog/how-can-an-ib-myp-maths-student-move-from-5-to-6-or-7/) explains which criterion moves fastest and how criterion totals map to final grades, and [the 12-week improvement plan](/blog/improve-ib-myp-mathematics-performance-gurgaon-pathways-heritage/) shows how to schedule the work.

Concept understanding matters here too. A student who knows what a ratio means, and not just how to compute one, recognises it inside a recipe, a map scale or a currency exchange without being told. That idea is developed in [concept-first MYP maths tutoring](/blog/concept-first-ib-myp-mathematics-tutoring-pathways-heritage-gurgaon/).

## What Does a Problem-Solving Session Look Like?

A good session is mostly the student working, with the tutor asking questions and watching. A typical hour has four parts: a quick retrieval of last week's routine, a fresh task attempted by the student, a debrief on the choices made and a short independent task to take home.

In the first ten minutes, the tutor asks the student to restate the routine from memory and apply it to a quick, familiar problem. In the next twenty-five minutes, the student attempts a new real-life task with the tutor at the lowest support level appropriate. The tutor notes where the student pauses, what the student writes first and what the student skips. The next fifteen minutes are the debrief: the tutor asks the student to explain why each choice was made, and compares the student's approach with a second route to the same answer. The last ten minutes set one task for the week and ask the student to say how they will start it.

What the parent sees afterwards is a short note naming the move that was weakest, such as "justifying the answer in context," and the task that will practise it. A parent can also ask for the student's attempt, with the check and the sentence at the end, saved in a folder. Over a month, the folder shows the routine becoming visible in the writing.

## How Can Parents Help Without Solving the Problems?

Parents help most by asking questions, not giving answers. The three questions that do the most are: what is the problem asking, what have you tried, and how do you know your answer makes sense?

Avoid showing your own method, even if it is faster. A student who adopts a parent's method without understanding it loses the chance to build their own. If you can see the student is stuck, ask them to draw the situation, which unblocks many problems on its own. If they are still stuck after ten minutes, stop and note the question for the tutor or teacher, which is itself useful evidence of where the gap is.

Everyday life is a free source of problem-solving practice. A restaurant bill, a travel plan with a budget, a scale on a map, the unit price of two pack sizes at a shop: each is a small modelling task. Asking "which is the better buy, and how do you know?" takes thirty seconds and builds the habit that school tasks demand. ## What Mistakes Cost the Most Marks in Real-Life Tasks?

Five mistakes recur. Each is easy to fix once seen.

1. **Answering the wrong question.** The student computes something correct but not what was asked. Fix: restate the question before starting and compare at the end.
2. **No units or wrong units.** A correct number in the wrong unit is a wrong answer. Fix: write units on every line.
3. **Unreasonable rounding.** Giving 17.3 buses or a currency to six decimals. Fix: ask what precision the situation supports.
4. **No check against the context.** The answer is never tested for sense. Fix: always test a value either side or estimate roughly first.
5. **No stated assumptions.** The model is used as if it were reality. Fix: write one assumption and one limitation.

A sixth, subtle one: writing too little. A correct final answer with no visible reasoning earns far less than a slightly flawed answer with clear reasoning in Criterion C and D. Show the thinking.

## When Does a Student Need a Tutor for This?

A student needs help when the pattern is persistent: strong marks on routine questions and weak marks on real-life or unfamiliar tasks, a habit of stopping at a blank page, or a repeated gap between criterion A and criterion D. If the student is already attempting unfamiliar tasks and improving, school teaching and practice may be enough.

The best time to start is early in MYP 4, before the assessment load of MYP 5 and the Personal Project, because the habits take weeks to build and months to become automatic. A student in MYP 5 moving towards the DP benefits particularly, since DP Paper 3 and the IA ask for the same independence at a higher level, and the flagship [guide to scoring a 7 in IB Math AA HL](/blog/how-to-score-7-ib-math-aa-hl/) shows where this early work pays off. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is MYP maths problem solving?

It is the ability to take an unfamiliar or real-life situation, decide what mathematics applies, carry it out and check the result makes sense. In the MYP it is assessed mainly through Criterion D, applying mathematics in real-life contexts, and it supports Criteria A to C as well. It differs from routine practice because the student, not the question, chooses the method.

### Why does my child do well in tests but badly on real-life tasks?

Tests often follow a chapter and name their own method, while real-life tasks do not. A student who has learned to recognise question types can look strong in the first and lost in the second. The fix is a routine for analysing situations, plus practice across several contexts, not more of the same worksheets. A tutor can diagnose which step stops the student.

### How do I know if my child is problem solving or just memorising?

Give them a problem they have not seen and watch how they start. A problem solver restates the question, draws or lists information and tries a small case. A memoriser asks which formula to use or looks for the chapter. Another test: ask them to explain why they chose a method. Memorised methods rarely come with reasons.

### Can problem solving be taught, or is it talent?

It can be taught. Talent explains who finds it easy at first; a routine, graded practice and fading support build it in most students. The change is usually visible in the student's writing within four to six weeks: more setup, clearer units and a final sentence in context. Progress is gradual, and no tutor can guarantee a particular grade.

### How long does a student need a tutor for problem solving?

Often a term or two of weekly sessions is enough to establish the routine, followed by a review. The aim is for the tutor to become less necessary, so a plan with no end point is a warning sign. Some students return for a short block before MYP 5 assessments or before the move to DP.

### Should my child use a calculator for real-life tasks?

Use whatever the task permits, but understand the answer before and after. A calculator helps with arithmetic and graphs, and it cannot choose the model or check sense. Students who reach for the calculator before restating the question tend to compute the wrong thing quickly. Estimate first, calculate second, compare the two.

### What if my child hates word problems?

Start with problems from their own interests, such as sport, games, music or cooking, so the context carries meaning. Then make the routine the thing they practise, not the problem type. Dislike of word problems is usually a reading or confidence issue, and short, successful attempts at the first four steps of the routine rebuild it.

### Is MYP maths problem solving the same as the DP Internal Assessment?

They are related, not the same. The IA, the mathematics exploration, is 20% of the DP grade, is 12 to 20 pages, and rewards the same habits: choosing a model, using it and reflecting on its limits. MYP real-life tasks are shorter. Practising the routine in MYP is one of the best preparations for the IA later.

### Does a tutor help with MYP assessed tasks at school?

A tutor can teach the skills and explain what a criterion asks, but the student must produce the assessed work. A tutor who writes or edits a submission puts the student's result at risk, and schools check authorship. Good tutoring uses practice tasks of the same kind, not the assessed task itself.
